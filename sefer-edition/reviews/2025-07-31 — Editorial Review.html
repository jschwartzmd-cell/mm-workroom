31 July 2025 — עַל נַהֲרוֹת בָבֶל — A Study of תְּהִלּים קל"ז

Independent review · read fresh, no exposure to the writing process · 5 October 2026

1.Overall Read

A pre-Tishah B'Av walk through Tehillim 137 organized as an eighteen-section verse-by-verse study of a nine-pasuk perek. The document runs to roughly 3,900 words with an eleven-note Mareh Mekomos apparatus. The piece has a strong narrative spine — Malbim-settled-and-crying (II), Radak-tears-prevent-song (III), Midrash-brutal-march (VI), the bitten thumbs (IX–X), and the Malbim's re-reading of the harsh close as gemul (XVI) — and three stretches of genuine power: the Alshich's “worst part of galus is when we cannot cry” paired with the 1967 Kotel callout (VI); the Section VIII question on Divine silence in the Shoah and on Shimchat Torah 5784; and the mashal of the queen and the mukeh shechin (X). This is sefer-worthy material.

The document's structural problems are two. First, two different sections carry the title “The Eternal Question” (V and VIII) — a header-hierarchy error that will confuse a reader navigating the table of contents. Second, Section VIII asks the heaviest question in the document (“Where were the malachim in Auschwitz? In Kfar Aza?”) and Section XII is explicitly titled “The Answer to Our Question” — but the “answer” in XII is really a redirection (“are we ready to bite our fingers off?”) rather than an answer, and the ethical weight of that pivot — from murdered yidden as evidence of Divine silence to a demand on the surviving generation — is not acknowledged. Footnote reference numbers appear in body-order 9, 3, 1, 4, 5, 7, 6, 11, 2, 8, 10 and will need renumbering before print.

2.What Works

  • The three-reading structure for pasuk b' (Section III) — literal, Radak, Malbim — is the document's finest architectural move. Each reading sits in its own sub-header; each earns its space; and the Malbim's wordplay on עֲרֵבִים as sweet things is deployed as the structural key that unlocks the rest of the perek. The botanical gloss on Salix babylonica is a quiet grace note — a reader will remember it.
  • The 1967 Kotel soldier callout (Section VI) is the document's most memorable single passage. It is well-placed (immediately after the Alshich's chiddush on desensitization), it carries its own evidentiary weight (fn11 is appropriately hedged on dialogue attribution), and the final line — “Only a yid could give that answer” — closes the Alshich's pshat cleanly.
  • Section VIII's willingness to voice the uncomfortable question — “Where were the malachim in Auschwitz? In Kfar Aza?” — is a brave editorial choice and the right one for a pre-Tishah B'Av piece in 2025. The authorial self-location (“grandson of Holocaust survivors, great-grandson of those who died al kiddush Hashem”) gives the question standing it would not otherwise have. The flag in fn2 (“not fully resolvable in human terms”) is the right register.
  • The mashal of the queen and the mukeh shechin (Section X) is deployed with real precision. The queen's rebuke — “I did not pour wine for the king when he asked, and now you want me to pour for a mukeh shechin?” — folds in both the cheit that caused the churban and the Jewish refusal to replace one master with another. The transition from mashal to nimshal is seamless.
  • The “midget” motif in Section IX works as a rhetorical device — it reduces Nevuchadnezzar to his proper scale in the Ribbono shel Olam's universe and lets the maiming of the thumbs read as proportionate rather than grotesque. The Chazal-cited physical smallness is a nice fulcrum between the literal and the metaphysical.
  • The “portfolio” image in Section XVII — “we carry a portfolio: Torah, avodah, meaning, olam haba, schar v'onesh, gan eden, gehinnom — an entire world” — is a portable image that will survive the sefer page. It also lands cleanly against the “they want to wrestle it out of our hands” clause.
  • The expanded notes on Malbim (fn1), Midrash Shocher Tov (fn7), Perfidy (fn10) and the 1967 story (fn11) are substantive and well-paced. Fn10 in particular — the Ben Hecht biographical sketch with the frank statement that “the Rebbe brings it as recommended reading not as an endorsement of every claim” — is the right academic-flag treatment for a controversial recommendation.
  • The lead paragraph orients the reader well in roughly 110 words — sketching the Malbim-Midrash-Alshich-Shoah-closing arc without rehearsing the whole document. A model opening for the series.

3.Where It's Weak or Could Be Sharper

Two sections carry the identical title “The Eternal Question” (V and VIII). This is a hard editorial error for a printed table of contents. Section V is really “Pasuk d': How Can We Sing Shir Hashem?” — a brief one-paragraph gloss on “אֵיך נָּשׁיר”; Section VIII is “Where Were the Malachim?”. Rename to disambiguate.

Section VIII asks a question that Section XII does not actually answer. VIII asks: “Where were the malachim seventy-five years ago in Auschwitz? Where were they in Kfar Aza?” — a theodicy question. XII (titled “The Answer to Our Question”) pivots to: “are we ready to bite our fingers off?” — a mussar demand on the surviving generation. The pivot is defensible but needs explicit acknowledgment: the question is not being answered on its own terms (“here is why Hashem was silent”) but reframed (“here is what the question demands of us”). As written, the document glides past the pivot. Two sentences of explicit framing — “The Midrash does not answer the theodicy question directly; it reframes it as a question put to us” — would carry the reader honestly across the gap. Without that, the “calculus is different” closing of XII risks reading as the kind of answer the author himself flagged as unsatisfactory in VIII.

Sections IV and V are each a single short paragraph. IV (pasuk g') is one page-fraction of body before an h3; V (pasuk d') is a single paragraph — too short to carry a Roman numeral at the same level as III (which is three sub-sections) or VI (which is four). Consider folding IV and V into III, or collapsing both into a single “Pesukim g'–d': Bavel's Demand and the Jewish Refusal” section. The eighteen-section numbering oversells the structural weight.

Section XI's closing paragraph on “תלי תלים של הרוגים” as the Midrash's reading of “ותוֹללֵינוּ שִׂמְחָה” moves very fast from the piles of corpses to “they took delight in their restraint”. The claim that the yidden felt simchah looking at the piles of their slaughtered brothers is a theological-emotional claim of extraordinary weight. The passage needs a slower hand — one additional sentence situating what kind of simchah this is (the simchah of “we did not break” as distinct from any affective pleasure in the deaths themselves). As written, the reader is asked to absorb the Midrash's most daring psychological claim in a single conjunction (“yet, says the Midrash”).

The lecture's “I”-voice intrudes unevenly. Section VIII is in the first person throughout (“I want to ask a question… I know we are not supposed to ask…”); Section XVII shifts to “I think the Malbim understands”; most other sections are third-person exposition. For a sefer edition, the editor must decide: either (a) retain the maggid shiur's first-person voice throughout (the Lichtenstein model), (b) convert to third-person throughout (the Soloveitchik-published-shiurim model), or (c) distinguish typographically between exposition and the author's own voice. The current mixed register will read as inconsistent on the page.

Section XV (“Edom on the Sidelines”) presents two approaches and a “Deeper Pshat” that are not synthesized. The two approaches (Midrash's Churban Bayis Rishon reading, and the forward-reference to Churban Bayis Sheni) sit as a list; the Malbim-ish third reading on “עָרוּ עָרוּ” as uprooting the inner yesod arrives without connective tissue. Either integrate the Malbim as a third item in the list, or make explicit that the author adopts the third reading in preference to the Midrash's historical one.

Section XVI's one-word resolution via gemul is handled quickly (“the Malbim resolves it in one word”) and then expanded in “The Meaning of Gemul”. Good move. But the subsequent paragraph — “eventually it turns back upon you” — introduces a theological claim (middah k'neged middah at the national-historical scale) that deserves either citation to parallel sources (Rambam's Iggeres Teiman, the Netziv on Devarim 32, etc.) or more hedging. As written, the Malbim's hermeneutical move is being asked to carry a hashkafah load the single word gemul cannot carry alone.

4.Factual / Citation Concerns

Fn7 attributes the biting-of-the-thumbs Midrash and the queen/mukeh shechin mashal to Midrash Tehillim (Shocher Tov) on perek 137. The biting-of-the-thumbs narrative is more commonly cited to Eicha Rabbah (introduction and on Eicha 2) and to the sugya on Sanhedrin 92b; the queen-and-mukeh shechin mashal is also found in Yalkut Shimoni. Shocher Tov on 137 does carry much of the material but not all. Verify the precise location of each sub-component before print, and consider citing the parallel sources (“see also Eicha Rabbah and Yalkut”) in the expanded note.

Fn9 cites the minhag to say Al Naharos Bavel before benching on weekdays to Shulchan Aruch Orach Chaim siman 1. Siman 1 is the opening of SA on daily chashivus and the posture of a yid; the minhag of Al Naharos Bavel before birkas hamazon is not in siman 1 proper — it is more commonly cited to Mishnah Berurah siman 1 (Be'ur Halachah, drawing on the AriZal) or treated as a minhag grounded in the Shelah and the Siddur HaAriZal. The SA itself does not codify it. Tighten the citation (“see Mishnah Berurah there, and the Siddur HaAriZal”).

Fn6 (Alshich HaKadosh) presents the chiddush that “the worst part of galus is when we cannot cry” as the Alshich's own framing. The Alshich on Tehillim 137 does develop a reading in which “גַם בָּכִינוּ” marks a spiritual achievement, but the sharp aphoristic form (“the worst part of galus…”) reads more as the maggid shiur's crystallization than as the Alshich's own language. Verify that this specific formulation is in the Alshich, or soften the attribution (“as the Alshich's reading implies”).

Fn11 (the 1967 Kotel story) opens the historical frame with “Mota Gur” as the IDF commander who reached the Kotel. The body of the shiur attributes the proclamation “הַר הַבַּיית בְּיָדֵינוּ” to Rav Goren, while fn11 attributes the proclamation to Rav Goren and the arrival to Mota Gur. Historically: Mota Gur's radio call was “הַר הַבַּיית בְּיָדֵינוּ — אַנִי חוֹזֵר”; Rav Goren's proclamation at the Kotel was the more liturgical sequence (shofar, El Malei Rachamim, declarations). The body conflates the two. Clean up: give Mota Gur his line and Rav Goren his.

Fn2 glosses “Shimchat Torah two years ago” as “Shimchat Torah 5784 (October 7, 2023)”. In Eretz Yisrael October 7, 2023 was Shimini Atzeres/Shimchat Torah (one day); in chutz la'aretz it was Shimini Atzeres and Shimchat Torah was October 8. The shiur appears to be delivered in chutz la'aretz (the Perfidy reference, the cocktail-party scene, the Yiddish register). Either hedge (“Shimini Atzeres/Shimchat Torah 5784”) or add the Eretz Yisrael context for the date to be exact.

The physical-smallness claim about Nevuchadnezzar in Section IX (“Chazal say Nevuchadnezzar was small”) deserves a citation. Shabbos 149b and Chagigah 13a discuss Nevuchadnezzar but the specific claim of physical smallness is less obviously grounded; it may be a conflation with the Daniel-era descriptions. A one-line footnote with the Chazal source would protect the rhetorical move.

Section III's Yirmiyahu citation (fn4) is given as “pesukim in Yirmiyahu”. The pesukim are specifically Yirmiyahu 29:5–7 (the Iggeres to the Babylonian exiles). Fn4 gives this correctly in the expanded note; the body should name “Iggeres Yirmiyahu, perek 29” rather than the vaguer “pesukim in Yirmiyahu”.

The Linnaeus-botanical claim (Section III) that Salix babylonica is “named after this very pasuk” is correct in spirit but technically imprecise: Linnaeus named the species in Species Plantarum (1753) drawing on the pasuk through the Vulgate tradition, not directly from Tehillim 137. The botanical identification is also contested — many scholars now hold that the biblical aravim are poplars (Populus euphratica), not willows. A one-line hedge in a footnote would protect the lovely detail without claiming more than is warranted.

5.Small Polish Suggestions

  • Footnote renumbering. Body-order of references is 9, 3, 1, 4, 5, 7, 6, 11, 2, 8, 10. For a printed sefer this needs to run 1, 2, 3, … in body-order. The current order reflects the drafting history, not the reader's path.
  • “The Rebbe” versus “Rabbi Ephrati”. The body uses “The Rebbe” in Sections VIII and XVI (“The Rebbe never understood it as a child”); the title block and footer identify the maggid shiur as “Rabbi Ephrati”. Standardize to one form — “the maggid shiur”, “Rabbi Ephrati”, or “the Rebbe” — and keep it consistent.
  • The “midget” repetition in Section IX — the word appears five times in a single section. It works as a motif but is tipping toward overused. Consider trimming to three, or varying the term (“this mamzer”, “this little man”) on the middle occurrences.
  • Yiddishisms without gloss. “Dignitet” (IV, X), “grub” (XIII), “Olam-Haba'dikeh yidden” / “Yerushalayim'dikeh yidden” / “Shir Hashem'dikeh yidden” (XVIII) are shiur-register and work orally; for a sefer edition, each wants a one-time italicized gloss on first use (“dignity”, “coarse”, “yidden of the next world”).
  • The “schnell, schnell, dogs barking” aside in Section VI is a Holocaust echo that works in a live shiur. For a sefer reader a decade from now, consider either a parenthetical gloss (“schnell — the Nazi guards' command”) or integration into surrounding prose.
  • The “Schindler's List — actually, perhaps not” aside in Section XVII is charming in oral delivery but reads as an unresolved parenthetical in print. Either drop the aside, or expand into a one-sentence note on why the maggid shiur hesitates to recommend it.
  • The “cocktail party” frame in Section XVII (“the next time we sit with one for a cocktail”) is specific and vivid. Consider whether the register fits the pre-Tishah B'Av gravity of the surrounding paragraphs; a slightly broader framing (“the next time we are drawn into their world”) may age better and avoid the class-coded specificity.
  • The Hebrew in blockquotes is unvoweled (“על נהרות בבל שם ישבנו גם בכינו”) while inline Hebrew elsewhere in the document carries nekudos. For Tehillim especially the standard is voweled text in the quoted pesukim. Standardize.
  • Section numbering runs to XVIII for a nine-pasuk perek. The ratio is high. Consider consolidating per the suggestion above (fold IV–V into III; fold XII into XI; fold XVIII's two short paragraphs into XVII's closing). A twelve- to fourteen-section structure would read better on the page.
  • The “Reworked 3 May 2026” date in the header is a drafting marker that will not want to appear in the printed sefer. Convert to a colophon at the end, or drop for the sefer edition.
  • The lead paragraph italics — the whole paragraph is set in italic — makes a 110-word opening harder to read. Consider removing the italic treatment or breaking into two shorter paragraphs.

6.Bottom Line

A sefer-worthy pre-Tishah B'Av piece built on a strong Malbim-Alshich-Midrash spine, with three genuine peaks — the 1967 Kotel callout (VI), the Section VIII question on Divine silence in the Shoah and on Shimchat Torah 5784, and the mashal of the queen and the mukeh shechin (X). The document's two hard editorial issues are (a) two different sections carrying the title “The Eternal Question” (V and VIII) and (b) Section XII presenting itself as “The Answer to Our Question” without acknowledging that it reframes rather than answers the theodicy question of VIII — a two-sentence framing would carry the reader honestly across the gap. Verify the Midrash-source attribution in fn7 (Eicha Rabbah and Sanhedrin 92b parallels), tighten the Shulchan Aruch siman 1 citation in fn9, separate the Mota Gur / Rav Goren attributions in fn11 and body, and renumber footnotes in body-order. Consolidate the eighteen sections to twelve or fourteen; standardize the “Rebbe”/“Rabbi Ephrati” reference. Fix these and this is one of the strongest entries in the Tishah B'Av-adjacent material in the series.
