Mareh Mekamos · Shiur Review

23 June 2024 — הַקְטֹרֶת — History, Mystery, and Impact

Content-based review · reviewer read the transcript, summary, and finished write-up · 10 August 2026

1.The Shiur in One Paragraph

This shiur, delivered on the morning Rav Moshe Wolfson zt"l was niftar, moved through three interlocking topics: the place of korbanos and ketores within the seder tefillah; the contested history and authenticity of Sefer HaZohar; and the Zohar's extraordinary teaching that ketores is more beloved by Hakadosh Baruch Hu than tefillah itself. The opening tribute to Rav Wolfson set the thematic key for everything that followed. From there the shiur moved outward: why yeshivos skip korbanos (the Mishnah Berurah's heter); why we say ketores at all (the Zohar in Parshas Vayakhel); and then deep into the Zohar's own biographical scandal — R' Moshe de León, R' Yitzchak of Akko's years-long quest, the widow's startling testimony, the Yaavetz's Mitpachas Sefarim, R' Elazar Fleckeles' sworn declaration — before arriving at the traditional resolution through the Arizal's authority and the Shelah HaKadosh's last will. The final notes — the ketores as segulah, the three pesukim, the hidden Radak on Tehillim 20:10 — landed with real force. A shiur that uses one small passage in the siddur as a window onto the entire post-medieval transformation of Jewish prayer.

2.What Was Strongest

  • The R' Yitzchak of Akko narrative. The detective-story structure — years of travel, the solemn oath, the deathbed timing, the widow after shiva — is told with exceptional dramatic control. More than entertaining, it crystallizes the epistemological problem of the Zohar in a single vivid human story. The observation that two competing traditions about authenticity already existed before R' Yitzchak even left Eretz Yisrael is a particularly sharp historiographic point that most treatments of the subject miss.
  • The Yaavetz and Fleckeles presentation. The Rav does not paper over the dissent. He names serious authorities who doubted the Zohar's complete attribution and explains their reasoning honestly — Fleckeles' linguistic proofs and his gratitude to the emperor for suppressing Frankist printings are details a less scrupulous maggid shiur would have quietly omitted. This honesty makes the eventual mesorah-based affirmation more, not less, credible.
  • The battle-cry Radak on Tehillim 20:10. Genuinely novel for most of the audience. Making a familiar pasuk suddenly carry the full weight of ancient Jewish warfare — and tying it back to the ketores sequence — is a model of how a good shiur can make a person never hear a familiar prayer the same way again.
  • The Shelah HaKadosh's last will. “Don't forget to add the ketores” — as a historical pivot point for why all subsequent siddurim included the passage, this biographical anchor is far more memorable than a dry liturgical-history account would have been. The Parshas Vayetzei / Shaar HaShamayim naming story adds a layer that readers will retain.
  • The opening anecdote on Rav Wolfson. Functionally an introduction, but it also makes a theological argument no abstract framing could achieve: here is what genuine davening looks like when it is real. The detail of his body shuddering at Nishmas kol chai — from age eighty-eight through ninety-four — is the kind of image that stays.

3.What Was Weaker or Worth Pushing Back On

The central gap: the shiur never explains why the ketores is more beloved than tefillah. The Zohar says so, the Arizal built on it, the Shelah embedded it in every siddur — but the actual theological content of the claim is left unaddressed. The Rav acknowledges this gap honestly (“I don't understand the Zohar; I could only read how great it is”), but a shiur titled on the ketores owes the audience more than a concession. The word ketores sharing its root with kesher (bond) points toward the idea that the ketores represents a direct, unmediated binding with the Divine that bypasses the intercessory structure of prayer. If that is the Zohar's claim, it is worth stating even provisionally — leaving the central theological proposition as a naked assertion weakens everything that surrounds it.

The mesorah-based affirmation is structurally circular. We trust the Zohar because the Arizal authenticated it; we trust the Arizal because Klal Yisrael trusts the Arizal. This is not wrong — mesorah works precisely this way — but presented without any positive argument for the Zohar's content and depth, it sounds like an argument-stopper rather than an argument. The shiur could have observed that every major posek since the sixteenth century has ruled on Zohar-based practices without flinching, or that R' Yitzchak of Akko's own later reversal is itself a form of testimony. Any of these would give the tradition-side more intellectual substance.

The Shabsai Tzvi and Frankist section is disproportionate. The material is real and important as context, but the shiur spends considerable time on Eva Frank's cult without connecting the material explicitly back to the central topic. The connection — that Zohar-misuse led to pressure on the Zohar's legitimacy, which is why Fleckeles felt the need to swear publicly — is implicit, not stated. Tightening this section would have left room for the kabbalistic substance of the ketores itself.

4.Halachic / Lomdish Observations

The mechanics of the korbanos heter are left unexplored. The Mishnah Berurah's ruling is cited — an osek b'Torah may skip korbanos — but the underlying question is not engaged: is the recitation of korbanos a substitute for the sacrifice itself, an enhancement of the tefillah that follows, or a separate inyan entirely? These three framings carry significantly different halachic implications for when it is obligatory vs. optional vs. supererogatory.

The machloikes on ketores timing in Maseches Krisos is raised but dropped. The Rav mentions disagreement among the Amoraim about whether ketores was offered before or after zerikas dam of the tamid, but does not explain which opinion our siddur's arrangement reflects or why. If the accepted arrangement follows one opinion over the other, that opinion deserves identification.

The kavvanah requirement creates an unstated lomdishe problem. The Zohar conditions the protection of ketores on kavvanah. The Chofetz Chaim's comment is invoked: take five minutes to learn the ingredients. But what is the halachic status of ketores said without kavvanah? Does it have no value? Diminished value? The conditional framing of the Zohar suggests a meaningful distinction that the shiur does not resolve.

A potential lomdishe tension between ketores and tefillah. If the Zohar teaches that ketores is more chaviv than tefillah, a question lurks: which takes priority when time is genuinely short? The shiur treats them as purely additive (say both), which is the practical answer, but the theoretical question of relative halachic weight given the Zohar's statement is not addressed.

5.Follow-up Questions Worth Asking the Rav

Q1.What is the actual kabbalistic reason that ketores is more beloved by Hakadosh Baruch Hu than tefillah? The shiur gestures at kesher (bond) as the root explanation, but is there a fuller account — in the Zohar itself, in the Arizal's writings, or in the Shelah — that would let us understand the claim rather than simply accept it?
Q2.The Vilna Gaon apparently rejected certain pieces of Zohar, and Rav Chaim of Volozhin records this but withholds the specifics. Are there any traditions among the talmidei haGra — in the Gaon's own writings or in manuscripts — that point to which passages he doubted? And does this affect how we poskin from Zohar in practical halacha?
Q3.If the Yaavetz and Fleckeles both effectively concede that not all of Zohar is from Rashbi — and both were recognized poskim — does that affect the halachic weight of Zohar-derived practices like the ketores recitation? How do we poskin from a sefer whose attribution is uncertain piece by piece?
Q4.R' Yitzchak of Akko later wrote in Otzar HaChaim that he had become convinced of the Zohar's authenticity. Is there any hint in that work of what changed his mind, or is this genuinely a complete mystery in the manuscript tradition?
Q5.Before the Arizal's influence and the Shelah's siddur, the ketores was not recited before and after davening. Are there any contemporary poskim who still hold this practice is optional rather than obligatory, given that its basis is Zohar rather than Talmudic law? And does the Zohar's conditional framing — “if you say it with kavvanah” — suggest a halacha that applies only when that condition can actually be met?

6.Bottom Line

A genuinely rich shiur — intellectually honest, historically meaty, and punctuated by several moments (the R' Yitzchak of Akko story, the battle-cry Radak) that are worth the hour on their own. Its weakness is precisely in the theological payoff: a shiur titled “Ketores — History, Mystery, and Impact” excels at history and mystery but concedes that the deepest question — why the ketores holds its extraordinary rank before the Almighty — exceeds the Rav's own grasp of the kabbalistic material. That honesty is admirable, but a learned talmid chochom leaves knowing vastly more about the Zohar's contested origins than about the ketores itself. The shiur opens a door and reveals a vast room; it does not yet bring the audience inside.
