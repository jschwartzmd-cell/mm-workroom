Sunday, 8 February 2026 — The Three פרשיות, הודו, and שיר השירים

Independent review · read fresh, from the finished HTML only (as delivered, before part-3 corrections) · 8 November 2026

1.Overall Read

An eleven-section write-up with a short closing summary (roughly 4,600 words of body plus a twenty-three-note Mareh Mekomos apparatus) of a Sunday-morning shiur that closes weekday Shacharis with three short parshiyos, opens the Shabbos prayers with Hodu, and then turns to Shir HaShirim. The arc is clear and it is an easy read: the opening hesped sets a tone that the close returns to; the three parshiyos are neatly parallel; the Hodu section is a well-told story; and the second half, built on the single number 117, is the strongest writing in the piece. The hard editorial issues are six: (a) Section III attributes a “minhag in Jerusalem” and a “takanah” to a Spanish author, and it is not clear where the idea comes from; (b) the Seder HaYom sentence in Section IV does not set up the question that follows it; (c) the Rokeach is born “at the end of the seventeenth century” in the body and “c. 1665” in fn 21; (d) the account of the rats in Section VIII and fn 15 do not agree; (e) a garbled, nested title in Section IX (“שו״ת יעריות שלמה in שו״ת יונת אלם”); and (f) an internal working note left in fn 5 (“file 50, fn 1; file 51”).

2.What Works

  • The opening. The hesped says what it needs in a few short paragraphs, and the sentence “What endures when הקדוש ברוך הוא separates the נשמה from the גוף? Only the מצוות, the תורה, and the חסד” is exactly right.
  • The Chacham Tzvi and his tefillin (III). A good short story with a point: the minute and a half, the son watching, “Abba, what are you saying?” It earns its place as the reason the passage migrated into the siddur.
  • The theological question (IV). “What does it mean to ask for יראת שמים when everything but it is in Heaven’s hands?” is posed cleanly, and the answer, however briefly, is memorable (“He has no choice, כביכול”).
  • The reason for the daily recital (V). The hamarbeh lo he’diph paragraph and its caution against too much hishtadlus is the most useful practical passage in the piece; the Gra’s twist, and the answer to it, are neatly done.
  • The Hodu story (VII). The Besht’s plan, the storm, the arrival on Erev Shabbos, the realization of מניעות and the institution of the custom are well paced; the admission that these are legends keeps the story in its place.
  • The arithmetic (X–XI). The 144 − 27 = 117 and 3 × 39 = 117 are set out so that a reader can check them; the callout for the latecomer is a pleasant touch.
  • Footnote hygiene. Twenty-three notes in body order with matching back-links; the Hebrew-inline convention is consistent.

3.Where It’s Weak or Could Be Sharper

Section III: “a מנהג in ירושלים” and “a תקנה.” Rabbeinu Yonah (fn 2: Gerona, c. 1180–1263) is said to note a minhag of Jerusalem to recite the passage daily, which then “became a takanah.” Nothing says why a Spanish author would speak of a Jerusalem custom, whose takanah it was, or how a custom becomes one; fn 2 repeats it as “the מנהג ירושלים of his era.” This needs a source or should be cut. As written it reads like a slip.

Section IV: the Seder HaYom sentence and the question that follows. The text says R’ Moshe ben Machir writes that reciting Parshas HaTeshuvah with kavanah “strengthens his resolve for תשובה through the entire year,” and the next subsection then begins: “An important question: how can a yid be מכוון that all the רשעים in כלל ישראל should do תשובה…” The word mechaven and the resha’im have not been introduced; the reader has to guess that the previous sentence was meant to say it. Fn 4 also says the daily recital “was instituted by R’ Moshe ben Machir and others,” which the body does not say.

Section V: תשב"ץ and the Maharam’s students. The body says Tashbetz is an acronym for תלמיד שמשון בר צדוק. A reader who knows the genre will hesitate: the letters tav-shin-bet-tzadi read naturally as Teshuvos Shimshon bar Tzadok, and the later Tashbetz that fn 10 mentions is likewise a responsa title. Second, the Maharam (d. 1293) is said to have been the rebbe of “the אגודה”; the Agudah is the work of R’ Alexander Suslin HaKohen, who lived long after the Maharam, so it cannot be a student’s work in this sense. (The Hagahos Maimoniyos would fit.)

Section V: “חכמי ישראל struggled.” The sentence sets up why the passage moved to the end of the siddur, and it is vague. The point is that someone objected to praying for parnassah before davening; say who, or say “it was objected.”

Section VIII: Rishonim and the Arizal. “The ראשונים write that the only time a yid can become a vessel… is just before שבת… The מנהג is ancient — it comes from the האריז״ל and his תלמידים.” The Ari is sixteenth-century, hardly a Rishon and hardly “ancient”; and fn 18 attributes the point to the Shaar HaKavanos. Choose one, or say that the lecturer says Rishonim.

Section VIII and fn 15: the rats and the decree. The body says the Gemara explains that scrolls were stored beside the terumah, rats came for the terumah and damaged the scrolls, so the Sages decreed that hands become tamei. Fn 15 quotes the Gemara (in Hebrew, with an ellipsis) as saying that the Sages enacted that books “disqualify the terumah” (“פוסלין את התרומה”), and nothing in the quotation mentions mice. The decree is stated two ways (hands that touch; books that disqualify) and the rats are unquoted. Either they are the lecturer’s gloss or the Gemara’s; say which.

Section IX: “The שו״ת יעריות שלמה in שו״ת יונת אלם.” Two titles nested in each other, neither one a name a reader will recognize; and the point is a remark about being “photographed” by an eighteenth-century author, which cannot be what is meant. It looks like a transcription error and an anachronism.

Section IX: the Rokeach’s dates and city. The body: born “at the end of the seventeenth century”; fn 21: “c. 1665–1741”. The body says Amsterdam’s community was enormous “in the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries”; fn 19 and fn 21 give 1735 and 1735–1740. The body says “both then living in Amsterdam” of the Yaavetz and the Rokeach, while the quoted responsum speaks of the rabbi of “my father’s city” (the Yaavetz is usually placed in Altona). And “Such a thing had never happened in our history” is an unsourced superlative.

Voice. The body keeps the lecturer’s first person (“I will not be מספיד today,” “I do not know when ‘חס ושלום’ last appeared in a משנה”), his dates (“the night of February 7”) and his asides (“Try telling that to the יונגעלייט…”; “this caused a small commotion this morning”). Earlier reviews flagged the same; it is a decision for the edition, but it should be made consistently. Register is mixed (“yid,” “yidden,” “gabbai,” “heilig” against “the attendant”).

4.Factual / Citation Concerns

Fn 5. “Same author and work as referenced in earlier shiurim (file 50, fn 1; file 51, etc.)” is an internal working note and must go.

Fn 6 and the body. The body says the Gemara in Berachos teaches “whoever davens for his friend… is answered first”; fn 6 cites Bava Kamma 92a and Bereishis Rabbah 52:5 and adds in brackets “the שיעור referenced ברכות; the principal מקור is בבא קמא.” A published note cannot correct its own text; fix the body and drop the bracket. (The Midrash reference should be checked.)

Fn 7. The note cites Berachos 33b for “everything is in heaven’s hands except fear of heaven” but does not name the Sage; and the Maharsha’s resolution is rendered in the note as a request for “closeness,” which is not what the body says (“He has no choice”). The logic is missing in both: why would a request for fear of Him bind Him when other requests do not?

Fn 9. The note says the Shulchan Aruch’s opening siman “includes the recital of Parshas HaMan daily as a segulah for parnassah.” The seif number (1:5) is in the short title; verify that this seif says that and does not merely list the passage among those it is good to say. (The Beis Yosef and Tur are said to bring a Yerushalmi; a specific citation would help.)

Fn 13–14. Fn 13 says Hodu is “recited universally” in Nusach Sefard shuls; the body says “almost every.” Fn 14 says the Maor Einayim records the whole account of the journey and rescue; the body attributes to it only that the Besht instituted Hodu. Also the body lists the four categories as imprisoned, sea, ill, wilderness; fn 13 lists wilderness, prisoners, ill, seafarers. Same four, different orders; pick one.

Fn 15 and 16. Both give Hebrew “quotations” of the Gemara and Mishnah with ellipses; fn 16 quotes the Mishnah as “כיום שניתן בו” and the body gives a different wording for the end of the same sentence (“שכל כתבי הקודש קודש”). One text should be used and checked against the printed Mishnah.

Fn 19–22. A specific siman (Sheilas Yaavetz I:170), a specific museum (“Jewish Historical Museum (Amsterdam)” for a coin the body says is in “museums”), a specific Zohar folio (I, 62b) and “cf. Tikkunei Zohar.” None of these is in the body, none is hedged. Fn 21 gives “buried in Tzfat,” while the body does not say where.

Fn 23 and Section XI. The note says the Gemara in Sanhedrin 21b “develops Shlomo’s violation of all three.” To my memory the sugya there concerns wives and horses (“I will multiply and not turn away”); check that gold is there. And the claim that each lav “in principle carries malkus” deserves a source.

5.Small Polish Suggestions

  • The heading “תשב״ץ קטן and the מהר״ם מרוטנבורג’s Personal ערבות” mixes scripts in a way the other headings do not; make them parallel.
  • “The הייליגער בעל שם טוב”, “holy אלשיך”, “holy אור החיים”: pick an honorific convention and keep it.
  • Headings X and XI use the number 117 in different forms (“The Calculation of the 117 פסוקים” and “The 117 of שלמה המלך’s Atonement”); the second reads oddly.
  • “The גמרא in שבת” appears without a daf in the body; add it once.
  • The dedication appears both as a div and again in the colophon; one is enough.
  • The closing summary adds a new idea (“the world of יראה, the world of תשובה, the world of פרנסה”) that the body does not set up. Either build it or drop it.
  • The Hebrew date is missing from the header (earlier editions carry it).

6.Bottom Line

A readable, well-ordered write-up with a very good second half. It needs one mechanical pass (the “Jerusalem minhag,” the Seder HaYom sentence, the Tashbetz acronym and the Agudah, the nested title in Section IX, the Rokeach’s dates), one clean-up of the apparatus (remove the “file 50” note and the bracketed correction in fn 6, harmonize the Mishnah quotations, hedge fn 9, 13, 14 and 19–23), and a decision about voice. With those done it can stand as a sefer chapter.
