6 April 2025 — Preparing for פֶּסַח — בְּדִיקַת חָמֵץ and בִּיעוּר חָמֵץ

Independent review · read fresh, no exposure to the writing process · 7 September 2026

1.Overall Read

A well-constructed erev Pesach piece with a clean architecture: uniqueness of the issur (II) → Rambam-bittul baseline (III) → Tosafos/Ran on the rabbinic layer (IV) → Minchas Chinuch chakirah with three nafka minos (V, VI) → pivot to avodah through Radbaz and Chasam Sofer (VII–IX) → historical excursion on mechiras chametz (X). The lomdish-to-avodah pivot is executed at exactly the right moment and Section VII loops the avodah reading back into the first nafka mina, which is the piece's structural achievement.

Strongest stretch is Section VI (the three nafka minos): each of the three is presented with the alternatives cleanly bulleted, the objections named, and the chakirah doing real work. Weakest stretches are the Reb Itzele Charif opening callout (charming but hangs a footnote on an anecdote whose attribution the note itself concedes is oral masorah), the mechiras chametz section (which promises a full shiur and then delivers half of one), and a handful of small footnote-scope issues. The footnote apparatus is well-organized but numbered in shiur-delivery order (13, 1, 4, 3, 2, 7, 5, 6, 11, 8, 10, 9, 12) rather than in body order — this must be renumbered before print.

2.What Works

  • Section VI (the three nafka minos of the Minchas Chinuch) is the piece's lomdish centerpiece and is executed with real discipline. Each nafka mina gets its own subhead, its own bulleted resolution on both sides of the chakirah, and its own natural objection (mitzvah ha-ba'ah b'aveirah for #2, the general rule of kisui ha-dam/ten zuzim for #3). This is the model of how a lomdish section should read.
  • The pivot from Section VI to Section VII is timed exactly right. The chakirah ends; before the reader has time to ask “so what?”, Section VII opens the Chasam Sofer's matzah-from-chametz-grain question. And crucially, the final paragraph of Section VII loops the avodah reading back into the first nafka mina (the gluten-free Jew). This is genuine synthesis, not stapling.
  • The Rambam-bittul baseline (Section III) is set up beautifully: the blockquoted nusach of “kol chamira,” the dust-on-the-floor image, and the small utopian coda (“a neighbor on Pesach needs a sandwich? Help yourself”) gives the reader an intuitive feel for how radical the Rambam's position actually is before the tekanas chazal is introduced. Rhetorically well-shaped.
  • The mechiras chametz historical framing (Section X) is genuinely fresh material for a hilchos Pesach piece. The krechtsmer economy, the noble-distillery-lessee parnassah, the century-anchored dating: the reader learns something they will not have heard before, and the footnote (fn12) usefully names the Beis Meir, Chok Yaakov, Pnei Yehoshua, and Shulchan Aruch ha-Rav as the actual developers of the heter. This is the sefer's best kind of contribution.
  • The Chasam Sofer's matzah question (Section VII) is rhetorically well-built. The kefulos aside (the Rav's own father sitting on erev Pesach checking pounds of matzos for folds) grounds the abstract point in a specific, memorable practice; the rice/sirchon aside makes the counter-example airtight.
  • The dual-layer mareh mekamos apparatus continues to earn its place. The expanded notes on the Rambam's “yevatel be-libo”, the Ran's biographical placement, the Minchas Chinuch's composition history, and the Radbaz's peshat-not-kabbalah framing all give the sefer serious scholarly texture.

3.Where It's Weak or Could Be Sharper

Section X (mechiras chametz) is a half-shiur. The piece names it as one of the most fascinating topics in all of hilchos Pesach, delivers most of the setup (economic background, tavern-keeper Jews, the heter for schnapps versus for cookies), and then says twice that this is “for another shiur.” For the sefer edition, either commit to the discussion (name the machlokes, cite the two sides, offer the Rav's hachra'ah) or compress the tease down to two sentences. As currently structured the section is neither a self-contained excursus nor a full treatment.

Section VIII (“The Search Within”) and Section IX (“Yachatz”) share one idea between them. Both sections say: Pesach is the season of admitting we are not whole and doing the honest work of finding what we've hidden. Consider folding into one section — the yachatz observation is a strong close for the “search within” point and gives it a concrete ma'aseh-anchor rather than a second independent riff.

The Tosafos/Ran section (IV) presents the two ta'amim as parallel and moves on. The alert reader wants to know: does the difference generate a nafka mina? (It does — a person who can honestly achieve bittul; the halachic status of a house where the chametz is inaccessible; whether mechiras chametz is more comfortable on Tosafos than on the Ran.) A single paragraph at the end of Section IV noting even one such nafka mina would sharpen the whole apparatus.

The opening callout (Reb Itzele Charif anecdote) is warmly told but sits as a stand-alone miltza d'bedichusa disconnected from Section I's serious opening on hachanah. Consider either weaving it into Section I proper (the wife's “my father sold that chametz to a goy gamur” is actually a nice ironic bridge to the mechiras chametz excursion later) or moving to a footnote/appendix.

Section II's list of three layers (achilah, hana'ah, bal yeraeh) is presented cleanly but the section stops short of naming what makes the third layer structurally unique. The paragraph immediately below the list does the work (“basar v'chalav can sit in your house”), but the payoff sentence — “chametz alone the Torah demands you remove from your domain” — should be the topic sentence of the section, not the third paragraph.

4.Factual / Citation Concerns

The Reb Itzele Charif identification (fn13) conflates two distinct figures. Reb Itzele Charif of Slonim is Rabbi Yitzchak Yaakov Rabinowitz — but that identification is itself contested; the more common attribution of Reb Itzele Charif of Slonim is to Rabbi Yitzchak Yaakov Rabinowitz of the late nineteenth century, whose primary rabbanus was in Ponevezh, not Slonim. The footnote as written (“properly Rabbi Yitzchak Yaakov Rabinowitz of Ponevezh / Slonim”) muddles the geography. A brief clarification — or simply attributing the anecdote to “Reb Itzele Charif of Slonim (as popularly told)” — is cleaner.

The Ran citation (fn5) is given as Chidushei ha-Ran Pesachim 2a. The Ran's primary discussion of bittul/bedikah is in his chidushim on Pesachim, but the classic locus of the “shema yochal” reasoning is actually in the Ran on the Rif (early pages of the Rif in Pesachim), not exclusively in the chidushim. Consider expanding the citation to “Ran on the Rif, opening of Pesachim; cf. Chidushei ha-Ran.”

The Radbaz citation (fn9) is given as “Shu"t ha-Radbaz” without a specific siman. The Radbaz's shu"t is a large corpus and chametz-as-yetzer hara is not obviously tied to a single well-known teshuvah. Some chassidishe and mussar sources attribute the reading to the Radbaz more generally (or to his Metzudos David-style commentary work) without a pinned teshuvah. Before sefer publication, either pin the siman or hedge to “a reading transmitted in the name of the Radbaz.”

The Chasam Sofer citation (fn8) is given as “pirush al ha-Haggadah / Drashos Chasam Sofer on Pesach.” This is the second time in the sefer that a Chasam Sofer-on-Haggadah chiddush is cited without a specific siman or edition. A pinned edition (Machon Chasam Sofer? standard Drashos?) and page would strengthen the note.

The Minchas Chinuch siman (fn6) is given as mitzvah 9. Verify — the Minchas Chinuch's chakirah on tashbisu is standardly cited at mitzvah 9 in most editions, but the three nafka minos are developed across several sub-sections; a note pointing to the sub-section (“ק. i-iii” or similar) would help the sefer reader who wants to look it up.

The Rambam's birth date for the Minchas Chinuch author is given in the body of Section V as 1801–1874 with the aside that his birth and death fell on the same day of the year. Verify — the standard birth date for Rabbi Yosef Babad is 1801, and the death year is 1874, but the “same day of the year” claim is a pious biographical siman that circulates without a single primary source. Consider hedging with “said to have fallen on the same day of the year.”

The historical claim about mechiras chametz (fn12) names the Beis Meir, Chok Yaakov, Pnei Yehoshua, and Shulchan Aruch ha-Rav as the developers. Verify chronology — the Chok Yaakov (Rabbi Yaakov Reischer, d. 1733) predates the Pnei Yehoshua (Rabbi Yaakov Yehoshua Falk, d. 1756) and both predate the Shulchan Aruch ha-Rav. Naming them in chronological order rather than alphabetical would strengthen the historical argument. Also: the earlier Bach (d. 1640) already discusses mechiras chametz, so the piece's claim that “in earlier generations there was no such practice” is a slight overstatement.

5.Small Polish Suggestions

  • Footnote numbering in body order. The reader encounters footnotes in the sequence 13, 1, 4, 3, 2, 7, 5, 6, 11, 8, 10, 9, 12 — the notes were laid out in shiur-delivery order rather than reading order. Before sefer publication, renumber in the order the reader meets them.
  • The header date-line reads “Recorded 6 April 2025 (ח' ניסן תשפ"ה)” but the summary header gives the date as ו' ניסן. Verify which is correct. (6 April 2025 corresponds to ח' ניסן; the summary appears to be off by two days.)
  • The lead paragraph is long — a single sentence running to nearly 130 words that lists every section topic. Break into two sentences or convert the second half to a short bulleted preview.
  • “בון מויט” (Yiddish/German for “bon mot”) appears in the opening callout and in fn13 — the correct spelling would render as “bon mots” (French, singular bon mot). Either italicize as French or replace with “witticisms.” The Hebrew-alphabet rendering is unnecessary and unusual.
  • “גֶעשְמַקדִיק” (a gushmakdik roll, Section IV/Ran) — register-warm in the shiur, will read as jargon in the printed sefer. Consider “a tempting chametz roll” with a transliteration note.
  • “קרעטשמער” (Section X) — the standard transliteration is krechtsmer or karczmarz (Polish). Add a one-line gloss (“innkeeper / tavern-lessee”) on first mention.
  • “בל יראה is a סייג against accidental אכילת חמץ” (Section II) is a nice compression but is not quite right on the Tosafos' reasoning — the Torah's bal yeraeh is not chazal's seyag; the Tosafos argue that bal yeraeh is the Torah's own preemption, and the rabbinic bedikah is a further seyag. Tighten the language.
  • The rebbe's family minhag callout in Section X presents in the third person (“The Rebbe's family minhag: I do not sell chametz gamur...”) which is jarring. Either fully third-person (“The Rav does not sell chametz gamur...”) or fully first-person, but not both.

6.Bottom Line

A well-built erev Pesach piece whose structural achievement is the lomdish-to-avodah pivot: Section VI delivers the Minchas Chinuch's three nafka minos cleanly, and Section VII loops the Radbaz/Chasam Sofer avodah reading back onto the first nafka mina (the gluten-free Jew becomes the paradigm rather than the curiosity). Weakest spots: Section X (mechiras chametz) is a half-shiur that promises a full one and should either commit or compress; Sections VIII and IX share one idea and should be folded; the Tosafos/Ran parallel in Section IV should generate at least one nafka mina; the Reb Itzele Charif citation muddles Ponevezh/Slonim; the Radbaz and Chasam Sofer citations need pinned simanim; and the footnote apparatus needs body-order renumbering. The date in the header may be off by two days (ח' vs ו' ניסן) — verify against the recording. Fix those and this is a strong sefer entry.
