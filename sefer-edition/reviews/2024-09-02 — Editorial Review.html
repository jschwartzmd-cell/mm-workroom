2 September 2024 — אֱמוּנָה בְּבִיאַת הַמָּשִׁיחַ — Belief, Anticipation, and the Limits of Both

Independent review · read fresh, no exposure to the writing process · 23 August 2026

1.Overall Read

This is a serious, well-organized piece of Torah writing on a difficult topic given under difficult circumstances. The dedication frame — six chatufim murdered the day before, the contrast between American Elul optimism and Israeli mourning — is real and unforced, and it grounds what could easily have drifted into abstract theological survey. The architecture is logical: Rambam's twelfth ikar and its two components; Tanach sources; the Rishonim who counted differently; the Marc Shapiro controversy; the difficult Gemara of R' Hillel; Brisk vs. Chofetz Chaim on the meaning of chikui; halachic ripples that reveal the Rambam himself does not push chikui to its operational maximum; and a practical closing. The dual-layer footnote system is doing exactly what it should.

The piece is strongest in Section VIII (Brisk vs. Chofetz Chaim) and Section IX (the halachic ripples), which together form a genuine argument rather than a survey: the Rambam requires daily chikui, but the Rambam's own halachah on kohanim and wine shows that chikui is doctrinal rather than maximally operational, which in turn vindicates the Chofetz Chaim's reading against the pashkevil-writers. That is a real thesis and the piece earns it. Weakest are Section X (the practical list, which is generic) and a scattering of historical claims that need tightening.

2.What Works

  • The dedication and framing. The opening does not perform mourning; it names the fact of it, dedicates the shiur, and moves. The contrast — parents this morning sending children to yeshiva ketana; families in Israel burying loved ones — earns the topic without exploiting it. This is the right tone for a document that will outlive the news cycle it was written in.
  • Section VIII (Brisk vs. Chofetz Chaim) is the heart of the piece and it works. The Brisker Rav's line — "When Mashiach comes, I'll answer that question too" — is placed at exactly the right moment, and the Chofetz Chaim's counter-reading (absence of yeush rather than positive daily certainty) is stated clearly enough that a reader could take either side. The Reb Chaim Brisker distinction between the am ha'aretz and the person missing an ikar is a genuinely useful addition.
  • Section IX (Halachic Ripples) is the piece's most original contribution. The move from emunah to halachah via the kohanim-and-wine sugya, and the conclusion that Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi and the Rambam himself refuse to make chikui maximally operational, is a real argument that reframes the earlier debate. The parah adumah / pregnant-women thread and the nazir neder case round it out well.
  • The Marc Shapiro treatment (Section VI) is admirably measured. The Rebbe's position — the academic mapping is real; our community's working reality is the Rambam's framework — is stated without triumphalism and without dismissiveness. This is exactly how such a controversy should be handled in a written document that will be read by people from more than one kehillah.
  • The R' Hillel sugya (Section VII) is handled with the right degree of nerve. Naming that an Amora denied a personal Mashiach, then walking through Rashi and "shari leih marei l'Hillel", then landing on the pre-psak / post-psak distinction, is a compact and honest treatment of a passage many popular works skip.
  • The dual-layer footnotes continue to serve the document well. The academic-style flag on footnote 12 (Immanuel of Rome / contested Yigdal authorship) is exactly the right way to handle the contested attribution — transparent without cluttering the body text.

3.Where It's Weak or Could Be Sharper

Section X (“The Daily Practice”) is generic and belongs to a different genre. After the analytic force of Sections VIII–IX, the piece drops into a bulleted list of practical takeaways that could appear in any hashkafah pamphlet: daily consciousness, kavanah in brachos, avoiding yeush, avoiding date-setting, acting in alignment. Each item is true and none is memorable. Either compress the whole section to a single paragraph, or use it to do something the previous sections did not — e.g., name specifically what the Chofetz Chaim's baseline looks like in a person's morning, or what the Brisker maximum looks like in a person who actually holds by it. The current list flattens the very distinction the piece just spent two sections drawing.

The closing (Section XI) is close to the mark but not quite there. The return to the six chatufim is right; the pivot to sending children to yeshiva while families sit shiva is right. But the very last line — "the questions of how, when, and in what form are for next time" — is the wrong exit. It reads like a shiur outline, not the end of a written piece. Cut that line; end on the Chavakuk pasuk quoted just above it, or on the sentence about Klal Yisrael having carried this emunah for nineteen hundred years. The bracket back to the dedication is otherwise gracefully done and should be allowed to close cleanly.

Section II's “Portugal 1492” claim about Ani Ma'amin is doing more work than the evidence supports. The document says the printed Ani Ma'amin text "first appeared" in a Portuguese siddur of 1492, then hedges with "some historians have wondered" about the Expulsion timing. The claim as stated is difficult to source cleanly — the earliest printed siddurim are generally Italian (Soncino, late 1480s), and the specific attribution of Ani Ma'amin's first printing to Portugal 1492 is a piece of oral tradition more than settled bibliographic fact. Either cite a specific bibliographic source in the footnote or soften the body text to "is often said to have first appeared" and flag the attribution as traditional rather than documented.

The transition from Section V (Rishonim disagreement) into Section VI (Marc Shapiro) is abrupt. Section V ends with the Maharshal and Arizal declining to say Yigdal, characterized as a liturgical rather than doctrinal dissent. Section VI opens with Shapiro as if he were the next natural step, but he is a different kind of interlocutor entirely — an academic scholar, not a Rishon or Acharon with liturgical standing. A single transitional sentence acknowledging the shift in register (from within-tradition dissent to academic reappraisal) would keep the reader oriented.

Section VIII's headers are cluttered. There are four sub-headers in one section: "The Brisker Position — Rav Moshe Feinstein and the Brisker Rav," "The Brisker Rav on Daily Cheishbon," "The Chofetz Chaim's Position," "The Pashkevil Against Reb Aharon Leib Shteinman," "Reb Chaim Brisker's Distinction." That is one section header and five sub-headers — too many. Fold "Daily Cheishbon" into the preceding Brisker paragraph, and consider merging "Reb Chaim Brisker's Distinction" into the Chofetz Chaim discussion as its natural counter-move.

4.Factual / Citation Concerns

“One of fourteen Hillels in the Talmud” (Section VII) is a specific numerical claim that should either be sourced or softened. The count of Hillels named in Chazal is not a well-known standardized figure, and the parenthetical suggestion that this R' Hillel is "likely a grandson of Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi" is one identification among several — some Rishonim identify him as the brother of R' Yehudah Nesi'ah (which would make him a grandson of Rebbi), others as a different figure entirely. Either cite the source for both claims in the footnote or soften to "one of several Hillels in the Talmud" and "identified by some Rishonim as a grandson of Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi."

Yeshayahu 11 as the seventh-day-of-Pesach haftarah in Eretz Yisrael. The standard haftarah for the seventh day of Pesach (both in and out of Eretz Yisrael) is Shmuel Beis 22 (David's song of thanksgiving). The Yeshayahu 10:32–12:6 haftarah — which contains the "chotter mi'gezah Yishai" passage — is the haftarah for the eighth day of Pesach in chutz la'aretz, and for the last day (which in Eretz Yisrael is the seventh) in some rites. The claim as written is imprecise. Either verify against a luach and rephrase, or drop the calendrical detail and simply note that Yeshayahu 11 is read as a haftarah on the last day of Pesach and was adopted by the Chief Rabbinate for Yom Ha'atzmaut.

The pashkevil against Rav Aharon Leib Shteinman. The document states that the pashkevilim labeled Rav Shteinman a koifer in Bias HaMashiach specifically for transmitting the Chofetz Chaim's reading of chikui. The pashkevil campaign against Rav Shteinman is well-documented, but its stated grievances were primarily around chinuch and army questions; the specific charge of denying emunah b'Bias HaMashiach may be a distinct or lesser episode. Worth verifying against the pashkevil texts themselves before letting this stand as historical assertion.

Marc Shapiro described as “Harvard-trained, currently a professor.” Shapiro's Ph.D. is from Harvard; he is a professor at the University of Scranton. The description is accurate but slightly vague — if the point of naming credentials at all is to signal that this is not a fringe figure, name the institution.

Footnote 19 cites Ta'anis 17a and Sanhedrin 22b together for the kohanim-and-wine sugya. The primary sugya is Ta'anis 17a–b; the Sanhedrin 22b reference is thinner and, if included, should be introduced as "cf." with a note on what it adds. As currently written it reads as if the two are parallel primary sources, which they are not.

Footnote 16 (Rav Moshe on daily chikui) is a "general reference" without a siman. Given that this teshuvah is doing important argumentative work in the body — it is the pillar of the Brisker side — a specific Yoreh De'ah/Orach Chaim volume and siman should be pinned. The same applies to footnote 17 (Brisker Rav) and footnote 18 (Chofetz Chaim). At least one of the three should be nailed down; the "general reference" hedge is acceptable once, not three times in the same section.

5.Small Polish Suggestions

  • The Rambam quote in Section II — "all of our emunah into thirteen tenets of faith" — is presented in English inside curly quotes as though translated from a fixed formulation. If the phrasing is a paraphrase (which it appears to be), drop the quotation marks and italicize as summary; if it is a translation of a specific Rambam sentence, cite the sentence in the footnote.
  • Section IV's Yeshayahu blockquote uses ה' for the Divine Name, which is correct; the Malachi blockquote also does. Consistent. Good.
  • “Rabbi Akiva applied it briefly to Bar Kochba before withdrawing the application” (Section IV, and again in footnote 7). The historical record on Rabbi Akiva actually retracting the identification is thin — the classic account is R' Yochanan ben Torta's rebuke ("Akiva, grass will grow from your cheeks and Mashiach still will not have come"). Whether Rabbi Akiva himself retracted, versus was rebuked and the identification simply failed with Bar Kochba's death, is a separate question. Softening to "before Bar Kochba's revolt collapsed" or "before events proved otherwise" is more defensible.
  • Section IX opens: "If the belief in imminent Mashiach were taken to its full halachic conclusion, life would change in concrete ways." Consider making this the section's thesis sentence in bold or italics — it is the pivot of the piece's argument and currently reads as a mild scene-setter.
  • The Bilam subsection (Section IV) is one paragraph long and sits under its own h3. Either promote it by adding a second paragraph (the Rambam's citation of it in Hilchos Melachim is noted in the footnote and would fit well in the body), or fold it into the preceding Chavakuk discussion so the header structure is not carrying such a light load.
  • “Reb Chaim Brisker's Distinction” sub-header uses the Anglicized "Reb"; elsewhere the document uses "Rav" (Rav Moshe, Rav Aharon Leib, the Brisker Rav). Standardize to one register.
  • Footnote 22 (Shaagas Aryeh) is a "general reference" for a specific kashya that is quoted almost verbatim in the body. If the teshuvah is identifiable (this is the well-known Shaagas Aryeh on the nazir case), the specific siman should be cited.

6.Bottom Line

A serious and structurally accomplished piece whose real argument lives in Sections VIII–IX — the Rambam requires daily chikui but does not push it to operational maximum, which is the ground on which the Chofetz Chaim's reading stands against the pashkevil-writers; fix the generic Section X, trim the final line of Section XI, verify the Ani Ma'amin/Portugal-1492 and Yeshayahu-11-haftarah claims, and pin at least one of the three Brisk/Chofetz-Chaim teshuvah citations, and this becomes a strong sefer-quality entry on a topic that most popular writing handles far more clumsily.
