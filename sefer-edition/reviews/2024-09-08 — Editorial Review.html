8 September 2024 — אָז יָשִׁיר, יִשְׁתַבַּח, and the Throne of Shlomo HaMelech

Independent review · read fresh, no exposure to the writing process · 23 August 2026

1.Overall Read

This is one of the more architecturally satisfying pieces in the series. The shiur has a natural three-part shape — a personal tribute to the Skulener Rebbe, a halachic-historical treatment of Az Yashir and Yishtabach, and a midrashic excursion into Shlomo's throne — and the reworked text lets each part breathe without letting the seams show. The dual-layer footnote apparatus is applied consistently across twenty citations. The "three fifteens" motif in Section V (Mechilta → Yishtabach → Avraham-to-Shlomo yichus) is a real editorial achievement: it gives the whole document a numerological spine that carries the reader from the historical sections into the Shlomo material without contrivance.

The piece is strongest in Sections II–V (the halachic-historical arc from the Geonim through R' Moshe ben Kalonymos to the Aruch HaShulchan's קושְיָא) and in Section VIII (the throne narrative). It is weakest at Section IX, where the Avraham-acrostic passage contains a textual problem serious enough to require correction, and at Section XII, whose closing "the question deserves the recognition it merits" is the kind of shiur-room open ending that reads flat on the page. One structural bracket is missing: the Skulener Rebbe opens the document and is never returned to.

2.What Works

  • The Skulener tribute (Section I) is the best opening in recent shiurim. The two chosen details — the whispered instruction to switch to lashon ha'kodesh for the Mir בַחוּרִים, and the five-or-six-minute Asher Yatzar from the bungalow — do more theological work than any framing paragraph could. They also implicitly justify the whole series' focus on tefillah: this is what genuine davening looks like.
  • The Italian-origin history in Section III is confidently told. R' Moshe ben Kalonymos as the bridge from post-חוּרבָּן Roman practice to Ashkenaz, with the corroborating Mahzor Vitry תְּשוּבָה, gives the daily Az Yashir a documented lineage that most siddur-history treatments never bother to trace. The parenthetical noting the same פַּיְטָן composed Kol Mekadesh Shevi'i and Menuchah v'Simchah is the kind of quiet payoff that rewards close readers.
  • The Aruch HaShulchan / Zohar exchange (Section IV) is well-staged: the קושְיָא is sharpened before the resolution is delivered, and the AhS's own admission ("I had no resolution until I found that the Zohar HaKadosh asked the same question") is quoted in a way that makes the reader feel the relief of the answer.
  • The "three fifteens" (Section V) is the numerological spine of the document. Listing all fifteen generations from Avraham through Shlomo is a small choice that pays off enormously — the reader can count for themselves, which turns the claim from assertion into demonstration.
  • The throne narrative (Section VIII) is handled with the right balance of תַּנַ"ךic restraint and מִדְרָשic color. The halachic sidebar noting that the roaring lions did not constitute בֵּית דִּין is an intelligent parenthetical — it anticipates the sharp reader's objection before they raise it. The Pharaoh Necho / נכה etymology is one of those מִדְרָשic gems that most treatments skip.
  • The Tosfos בְּרָכָה הַֻּסְמוּכָה לַחֲבֶרתָּ&#u5D4; in Section X is the piece's cleanest single teaching. It answers a structural question (why no opening Baruch) with an answer that has severe practical נַפְקַ"מ (why the Mishnah Berurah makes you repeat from Baruch She'amar if you spoke). Text and consequence line up perfectly.

3.Where It's Weak or Could Be Sharper

The Skulener bracket never closes. Section I opens with the Rebbe and dedicates the learning לְּעִליֻי נִשְׁמָתָם; the closing Section XIV is a "synthesis" paragraph that speaks in the abstract about "two thousand years of layered mesorah." The Rebbe who davened Asher Yatzar for six minutes belongs in that closing — a single sentence bracketing back would transform the ending. As it stands, the tribute reads as a self-contained obituary rather than as a frame around the shiur.

Section IX (the Avraham acrostic) is the weakest single passage in the document — and the problem is textual rather than editorial. See Section 4 below for the specific issue.

Section XII (Shir HaMaalos on Yamim Noraim) closes on a shrug. The final line — "the question deserves the recognition it merits" — is the kind of thing that lands in a live shiur, where the tone of voice carries the meaning, and dies on the page, where the reader is left asking what the recognition is. Either commit to the halachic answer offered in the bullet above (divrei tefillah are not hefsek) as the resolution, or explicitly flag it as an ongoing tension and name what the tension is — but do not leave the paragraph hanging.

Section XIII (standing and sitting) reads as a stub. Four bullet points on posture, unmotivated by anything in the preceding argument, appear immediately before the synthesis. Either integrate the halachic point into the earlier Tosfos / hefsek discussion (where posture is arguably related to the בְּרָכָה-as-unit reading), cut it, or expand it into a fuller treatment. As placed it looks like a stray note that survived the rework.

The Charlemagne / Napoleon paragraph in Section VIII, though charming, is unsourced. "The painting depicting that visit" is too vague for a document that is otherwise scrupulous about citations. Either name the painting (Ingres? David?) and location (Aachen), or drop the visual detail and simply state that Napoleon reportedly declined to sit. As written, an alert reader will pause on it.

4.Factual / Citation Concerns

The Avraham-acrostic blockquote in Section IX contains phrases that are not in Yishtabach. The quoted text reads אֵל הַהוֹדָאוֹת, אֲדוֹן הַנִּפְלָאוֹת, בּוֹרֵא כָּל הַנְּשָׁמוֹת, רִבּוֹן כָּל הַמַּעֲשִׂים, הַבּוֹחֵר בְּשִׁירֵי זִמְרָה, מֶלֶך יָחִיד אֵל חֵי הָעוֹלָמִים. But the actual chasimah of Yishtabach in the standard נוּסְחָאוֹת is El HaHodaos, Adon HaNiflaos, HaBocher b'Shirei Zimrah, Melech El Chai HaOlamim — four phrases, not five. The lines Borei kol ha'neshamos and Ribon kol ha'maasim do not appear in Yishtabach; they belong to Elokai Neshamah and Nishmas respectively. The acrostic that gives אברהם must be reconstructed from the actual liturgical text (traditional readings pull the ב from b'tishbachos or a similar preceding word). The current blockquote will fail on any reader who compares it against their siddur. This is the correction most urgently needed before the piece ships.

The prose gloss "The first letters: א (Adon)" mis-identifies the first word. Even setting aside the phrases-not-in-Yishtabach problem, the first line of the blockquote begins with El (א), not Adon. If the acrostic is meant to start on Adon HaNiflaos, the reader has to know to skip El HaHodaos. The explanation needs to walk the reader through which phrase supplies each letter, in order.

The count of "eighteen occurrences of the שֵׁם הֲוָיָה" in Section VI is stated as fact ("רִישְׁוֹנִים count... eighteen times") without a footnote. Given that the whole eighteen-times-four-equals-seventy-two argument rests on this count — and the count depends on which introductory and concluding verses are included — a citation to the רִאשׁוֹן making the observation (Rokeach, Avudraham, or the מְקוּבָּלִים) would strengthen the argument considerably.

Footnote 8 (Zohar, Beshalach / Terumah) and footnote 20 (Zohar, Terumah) are both given as "general reference." Since two separate Zohar teachings are load-bearing in the argument — the reframing that binds Az Yashir to Yotzer Or, and the flame-under-the-כְּרוּבִים rebuke — at least one of these should be nailed down to a specific daf. The Aruch HaShulchan's own citation of the Zohar in OC 51 will give the exact reference for footnote 8.

Footnote 18 (Rav Chaim Vital) is under-specified. "Preserved separately from his transmission of the Arizal's teachings" is vague to the point of being unactionable for a reader trying to find the source. Either name the specific work (Sha'ar HaKavanos? Pri Etz Chaim? A separate כְּתַב יָד?) or flag the citation with the same academic-note treatment used elsewhere in the series for hard-to-pin sources.

The "Rav Avraham (Vrema) Loizban" parenthetical in Section I uses a nickname spelling that will confuse readers who know the מַרְבִּיץ תוֹרָה under a different transliteration. Confirm the standard spelling and add a bracketed clarification (yeshivah, city) so the reference is locatable.

5.Small Polish Suggestions

  • Section headings jump from Roman numerals I through XIV, but the visual weight of the sections is uneven — Section XIII (posture) is a four-bullet list, Section VIII (throne) is nearly a third of the document. Consider promoting the throne material to its own numbered part with sub-headings, or demoting the shorter sections to h3-level sub-sections of a parent heading. The current outline reads flatter than the material warrants.
  • The word "קָׁיָא" and its plural appear both italicized and un-italicized across the document. Standardize.
  • Section VII, third bullet: "The Meiri: from the Geonim." The colon-list construction is inconsistent with the preceding two bullets, which give a substantive attribution phrase. Rewrite for parallelism ("The Meiri: attributes the composition to the גְּאוֹנִים").
  • "He then danced with them, gave them חִזְּוּק, sent them off strengthened" in Section I — the third clause is redundant with the second (חִזְּוּק = strengthening). Cut either the Hebrew term or the English gloss.
  • "He also understood electricity" — the speculation is fine in a live shiur but reads oddly in a document that is otherwise carefully sourced. Consider prefacing with "some later commentators have speculated" or dropping the line and letting the "mechanical systems — gears, springs, hidden levers" sentence stand on its own.
  • The מראה מקומות section opens with the standard descriptive paragraph but does not indicate which footnotes carry the academic-flag treatment. If footnote 18 (Rav Chaim Vital) qualifies, mark it with the same styling the series uses elsewhere for sources that cannot be pinned to a specific siman.

6.Bottom Line

An architecturally strong shiur whose three parts — Skulener tribute, halachic-historical arc, and throne narrative — hold together better than most; the Avraham-acrostic blockquote in Section IX contains phrases that are not in Yishtabach and must be corrected before the piece ships, the Skulener opening needs a one-sentence bracket at the close, and Sections XII and XIII need either integration or expansion — fix those and this is one of the strongest documents in the series.
