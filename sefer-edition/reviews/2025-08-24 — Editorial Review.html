24 August 2025 — בִּרְכַת מוֹדִים and נְשִׂיאַת כַּפַּים

Independent review · read fresh, no exposure to the writing process · 5 October 2026

1.Overall Read

A twelve-section writeup (roughly 3,500 words of body plus a twenty-note Mareh Mekomos apparatus) that executes a clean two-part structure — the first half on Birkas Modim as the one tefillah that remains l'asid lavo, the second half on the long puzzle of why Ashkenazim in chutz la'aretz stopped duchening daily. The spine is strong: the Chafetz Chaim's pause (I), the Midrash that korban todah alone survives (IV), Rav Pappa's “limeimrinhu l'tarveihu” as the compositional engine of Modim D'Rabbanan (V), the Rav Elyashiv-at-102 story as the lived pshat of Modim D'Rabbanan, and then the four-way bavua of failed restorations — Gra in jail, Reb Chaim and the fire, the Baal HaTanya, and Rav Nosson Adler as the only success. The closing section (XII) binds the two halves well around the shared theme of “structures we did not invent and cannot dismantle.”

The document's hard editorial issues are five: (a) the footnote numbering in body-order runs 2, 3, 4, 1, 5, 19, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 20, 14, 15, 18, 16, 17 — a drafting artifact that will have to be re-sequenced before print; (b) nested <em> tags inside the three callout boxes produce malformed HTML that renders unpredictably across readers; (c) a lead paragraph that is a single 220-word sentence stacked with inline Hebrew; (d) “The Rebbe” used throughout the body for the maggid shiur while the header and footer identify him as “Rabbi Ephrati”; and (e) two garbled Hebrew/Yiddish strings in Section VIII that need to be resolved from the recording (“שטעט יקי” and “נשבת גמר” — see §4).

2.What Works

  • The two-part architecture. Modim (I–VI) and Nesias Kapayim (VII–XI) are separate sugyos; the writeup resists the temptation to force a false synthesis in the body and instead reserves the connecting move for the closing (XII). The structural restraint lets each half carry its own weight.
  • The Chafetz Chaim framing in Section I. “My Shemoneh Esrei is short; the Modim is long” is the kind of portable aphorism that will survive the sefer page. Its placement — immediately after the Yerushalmi's “the body knows; the mind drifts” — is a quiet editorial strength: the general principle, then the paradigmatic figure.
  • The “חיים” acronym in Section IV. Clean, mnemonic, well-set as a bulleted list, with the Tehillim 107 root (“chayim yoducha”) carried in fn4's expanded note rather than cluttering the body. A model of body-note division.
  • The Rav Elyashiv callout (end of V). The most memorable single passage in the document. The structural payoff — “That is exactly why we say Modim D'Rabbanan” — converts a lived story into halachic exposition without collapsing the story into the lesson. The attribution in fn19 is appropriately hedged (“circulates in ירושלים oral mesorah”).
  • The pattern-of-four in Section IX (Gra, Reb Chaim, Baal HaTanya, Rav Nosson Adler) is organized as parallel vignettes with escalating stakes — jail, fire, retraction, expulsion. The Aruch HaShulchan's “k'ilu bas kol yatzah” (fn11) is placed as the structural chasima on the first three before Rav Nosson Adler stands as the one exception. The sequencing is deliberate and effective.
  • The Pinsk story (callout in X) — “If you can be a רב here, I can be a כהן” — is deployed as both punchline and structural evidence for the Shaalos Margolios's shitta. The transition sentence (“The story is humorous and the underlying point is real”) lands exactly right: it tells the reader how to read what just happened without overexplaining.
  • Section VI (the halachic mistake) is tight, practical, and the right length. It names the mistake, states the pesak, hedges on the practical difficulty (“shalosha kolos lo yishtamu”), and closes with a “mitzvah l'takken” call to action. A clean model for the practical-halacha segments of this series.
  • Section XI's historical framing of the גליל minhag (Tzfat earthquake → talmidei haGra and Hassidic resettlement → preserved chutz la'aretz practice) is a satisfying historical arc, well-sourced in body-prose and with Rav Kook's “I don't care, I am duchening” as the perfect illustrative anecdote.
  • The closing (XII) genuinely binds the two halves (“prison cells and city fires” echoing “the Chafetz Chaim's pauses and Rav Elyashiv's English thank you”) without rhetorical overreach. The “be'ezrat Hashem… thank You is the only tefillah that remains” close returns the reader to the Midrash of Section IV.

3.Where It's Weak or Could Be Sharper

Footnote reference numbers appear in body-order 2, 3, 4, 1, 5, 19, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 20, 14, 15, 18, 16, 17. Fn1 (the foundational Gemara Sotah 40a) appears in Section V, after fns 2, 3, and 4 have already been used. Fn19 (Rav Elyashiv callout) appears in Section V; fn20 (Pinsk story) in Section X; both before fns 14–18. This is a drafting-order residue that will have to be fully re-sequenced in body-order (1, 2, 3, … 20) before print.

Nested <em> tags inside the three callout boxes. The Rav Elyashiv callout, the Rav Nosson Adler callout (chutzpa-of-Pressburg story), the Pinsk callout, and the 9/11 DNA callout all wrap the entire paragraph in <em> and then nest a second <em> for internal quotations. HTML does not nest italics meaningfully; most browsers will cancel the inner <em>, so the quoted lines (“Thank you very much”, “If you can be a רב”) end up visually identical to the surrounding italic frame instead of standing out. Fix: either drop the paragraph-level italics and keep the inner quotation marks in italic, or convert the inner quotations to <strong> or small-caps so they visually survive the frame.

The lead paragraph is a single sentence of roughly 220 words. It opens with a semicolon-chained list of six discrete topics; the reader has to work hard to parse where each clause begins. For a sefer-edition opener, break into three paragraphs: (a) the Modim material through the Chafetz Chaim; (b) the Rav Elyashiv story and the halachic mistake of the silent Modim; (c) the Nesias Kapayim arc. The current italic treatment also makes the long paragraph harder to read; consider removing italics or breaking into two shorter paragraphs.

“The Rebbe” in a non-Hassidic context. The body uses “the Rebbe” throughout (Section V: “From childhood the Rebbe understood it as…”; Section XI; fn14; fn17; fn20). The header and footer identify the maggid shiur as “Rabbi Ephrati”, and the shiur is on tefillah in a Litvish-register not a chassidish one. The term “the Rebbe” for a maggid shiur in this register will read as odd. Standardize: either “Rabbi Ephrati”, “the maggid shiur”, or “Rav Ephrati” — and apply consistently in the expanded notes as well.

Sections II and III are each a single short paragraph. II (what kavanah in Modim) is two paragraphs totaling under 200 words; III (bowing) is one short paragraph. These do not carry the structural weight of Section IV (five sub-elements) or Section V (four sub-headings with a long callout). Consider folding II and III into a combined “The Kavanah and the Bowing” section, or demoting both to h3s under I.

Section VII's governing question (“why do we in חו֥ץ לאָרֶץ not duchen daily?”) is restated at the end of Section VII (“How is it that we in chutz la'aretz stopped fulfilling this?”) and again at the start of VIII (“So why don't we duchen in chutz la'aretz?”). Three restatements of the same question inside two consecutive sections. Trim to one, then let the Rama's pshat and the minhag-origin material carry the second half.

Section VIII's “Difficulties With This Reason” subsection raises a sharp challenge (“are we to say יהודים in Eretz Yisrael today have an easier time with parnassah?”) and then dismisses it in two sentences (“the Rama's reason has the feel of an after-the-fact justification… but that is what we have”). The question deserves either a fuller treatment — naming the standard answers (the minhag precedes the taam; the Rama's taam is a limud zechus, not an original source) — or a cleaner acknowledgment that it is left as an open puzzle leading into IX's “failed attempts” material.

Section X's “Lakewood family” vignette (a family discovering through matzevos that they were not Kohanim) is dropped into a single sentence (“Imagine — all the pidyon haben ceremonies they had performed needed to be redone…”) without halachic unpacking. The “needed to be redone” claim is a serious halachic one (and in fact a contested one — bedi'eved status, how the earlier pidyonos are treated, etc.). Either expand into a short halachic note, or hedge the certainty (“raised the question of whether earlier pidyon haben ceremonies had been valid”).

Section XI's regional analysis introduces three distinct phenomena — (a) Galil Ashkenazim don't duchen daily; (b) Miron does because of Yerushalayim influence; (c) Kfar Hasidim is an exception — and places them under a single h2 with three h3s. The organization works but the Rav Kook anecdote (“I don't care, I am duchening”) is placed as a terminal h3 rather than integrated into the structural argument. Consider promoting Rav Kook's position to the lead of the section as the paradigm of “the Eretz Yisrael-wide chiyuv overrides regional minhag”, then letting the Haifa/Tzfat/Kfar Hasidim material fill in.

The closing paragraph of XII ends on “may מָּשׁיחַ bring us to a world where כֹּהֲנִים bless כְּלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל daily again, and where ‘thank You’ is the only tefillah that remains”. The latter clause creates a mild internal tension — if all tefillos are batel except Modim, in what sense do the Kohanim still bless Klal Yisrael? Resolve either by acknowledging that Birkas Kohanim is distinct from tefillah proper (it is avodah, as the body established in Section VII), or by softening the second clause.

4.Factual / Citation Concerns

Two garbled Hebrew/Yiddish strings in Section VIII. First, Rav Nosson Adler is said to have done unusual things in “a שטעט יקי city” — the Yiddish presumably intended is “a yekkisher shtot” (א יקישער שטאט). Reconstruct from the recording. Second, Section VIII attributes to “Rama” the principle that one may think business on Shabbos under the rubric of “dibbur is forbidden but not machshavah”, and closes with “‘נשבת גמר’ is the basis.” This string is not a recognized halachic phrase; the actual source is Yeshayahu 58:13 — “וְדִבֵּר דִָבָר” — which the Gemara (Shabbos 113a–b) reads as forbidding speech only, not thought. Fn9 already cites “דבר דבר” correctly; the body's “nishbas gemar” is a transcription error that must be reconstructed from the audio before print.

The Djerba claim (Section X: “an island in Tunisia called Djerba populated entirely by Kohanim — Ir HaKohanim”) overstates. Djerba's Jewish community (centered on the El Ghriba synagogue in Hara Sghira) has long had an unusually high concentration of Kohanim and a traditional claim of priestly descent, and the famous Hara Sghira / Dighet area is sometimes called the “village of the Kohanim” — but the island as a whole is not populated entirely by Kohanim, and the Jewish community itself has Levi'im and Yisra'elim as well. The DNA-confirmation claim should also be hedged — the Cohen Modal Haplotype (Hammer / Skorecki, 1997 and successor studies) establishes a disproportionately shared Y-chromosome signature among self-identified Kohanim globally, not a Djerba-specific confirmation. Soften to “a village in Djerba with an unusually high concentration of Kohanim, where general CMH studies have been drawn upon in later discussions.”

The 9/11 agunos beis din (fn15 and the callout in X). The primary beis din that handled the 9/11 agunos cases was the Beth Din of America under Rav Gedalia Dov Schwartz (av beis din of the RCA / Chicago Rabbinical Council) and Rav Yona Reiss; detailed published teshuvos are by Rav Michoel Broyde (Techumin 23) and others. The three names given in the writeup — Rav Shmuel Fuerst, Rav Avrohom Felder, Rav Eliyahu Levine — are prominent contemporary poskim but their joint authorship of a 400-page unpublished teshuvah on 9/11 agunos with the DNA-not-primary conclusion requires verification; the composition and conclusions as stated conflate several separate halachic responses to 9/11 (Rav Schwartz's heterim, Rav Zalman Nechemia Goldberg's analyses, the CRC rulings). Verify both the roster and the page-count before print; if the roster cannot be verified, soften to “contemporary American poskim” and cite the published Broyde teshuvah as the public anchor.

The “half the city of Volozhin burned down” story (Section IX). The Aruch HaShulchan (fn11) records attempts and providential interference, and multiple Volozhin fires are historically attested (1815, 1874, 1886, and the catastrophic 1891 closure period), but the specific pairing of Reb Chaim's attempted restoration of daily Nesias Kapayim with a specific fire the same night is a Netziv-family oral tradition more than a precisely dated event. Fn11 is appropriately careful; the body (“That night, half the city of Volozhin burned down”) is more specific than the sources support. Consider softening (“soon afterward, a devastating fire struck the town”).

The Rav Nosson Adler “expelled from Frankfurt” framing (Section IX callout). Rav Nosson Adler (1741–1800) did face communal cherem-adjacent opposition in Frankfurt over his Hassidic-leaning nusach changes and private minyan, and he did leave Frankfurt for a period (eventually returning), but “they expelled him from the city” compresses a more complex sequence (voluntary departure, return, continued tension). The Chasam Sofer's departure from Frankfurt as a young talmid is also more complex than the body suggests — he studied with Rav Nosson Adler in Frankfurt and later followed him to Boskowitz / Prostitz, eventually reaching Pressburg via Mattersdorf. Tighten the biographical arc or soften to “left Frankfurt under communal pressure”.

The Shulchan Aruch brings the Midrash on eternal Modim “l'halacha” (Section IV). The SA reference needs a siman-and-se'if pointer; fn4 cites Vayikra Rabbah 9:7 for the Midrash itself but does not pin the SA citation. The Mishnah Berurah on the opening of Hilchos Tefillah and the Tur both reference the Modim-forever tradition; the direct SA body-text citation is harder to localize and should be verified (likely Orach Chaim 51 area, or a Rama gloss) before print.

The Chasam Sofer left Frankfurt “as a young child” (Section IX callout). The Chasam Sofer was born in Frankfurt in 1762 and left to study with Rav Nosson Adler and Rav Pinchas Horowitz at age 13–15 (not “as a young child”). Tighten.

The Chidushei Rav Noach author (fn16 and Section XI). Rav Noach Shimonowitz was mashgiach and later rosh yeshiva figure associated with Kfar Hasidim / Yeshivas Knesses Chizkiyahu in Rechasim; verify the attribution of “instituted daily Nesias Kapayim” at the yeshiva level versus the beis midrash level, and the sefer name and edition.

The three families with Baal Shem Tov–attributed yichus (fn18 and Section X). The traditional triad is usually given as Rapoport (Rapaport / “rav of Porto”), Katz (Kohen Tzedek), and Horowitz (sometimes Hurwitz / Ish Horowitz) — not Margolios. The body hedges (“some say Margolios”), which is fine, but the standard version with Horowitz should be given first and Margolios as the variant.

The Rav Elyashiv open-heart surgery date and age (fn19). Rav Elyashiv's major cardiac intervention was in April 2012 at age 101 (he was born April 10, 1910 and died July 18, 2012); the “at age 102” framing is close but slightly over. The famous foreign surgeon flown in was Dr. Chaim Lotan (an Israeli, head of cardiology at Hadassah) in some accounts; a visiting American surgeon in others. Verify before print.

5.Small Polish Suggestions

  • Hebrew density in body prose. The lead and several sections (notably VII and VIII) run inline Hebrew-English-Hebrew-English at a rate that makes the English spine hard to track (e.g., “only the אשכנזים in חו֥ץ לאָרֶץ reserve the practice for יום טוב”). For a sefer edition, consider adopting a convention where the first use of each term gives Hebrew-plus-transliteration-plus-gloss and subsequent uses settle to one form.
  • “Duchen” as English verb. The body conjugates duchen as “duchen daily”, “duchened daily”, “they duchen”, “we don't duchen”. Standardize whether to italicize or not, and whether “duchening” / “duchened” are acceptable (the Lichtenstein-published-shiurim convention is yes; the Soloveitchik-convention is no, use “perform Nesias Kapayim”).
  • “Reworked 3 May 2026” in the header is a drafting marker that will not want to appear in the printed sefer. Convert to a colophon at the end, or drop entirely.
  • The “נצי"ב whose yahrzeit fell on Shabbos or Friday in this season” parenthetical (Section IX, Reb Chaim subsection) is a live-shiur dating cue that doesn't belong in print. Drop, or convert to “the Netziv” without the date reference.
  • “Rebbe said / Rebbe brings” throughout the expanded notes (see fns 14, 17, 20) — harmonize with the body-level fix on “the Rebbe” vs “Rabbi Ephrati”.
  • Transliteration drift. “Modim” appears both in Hebrew (מודים) and in transliteration; “Shemoneh Esrei” likewise. The header mixes “Birkat Modim” (Sefardic) with “Nesias Kapayim” (Ashkenazic). Standardize to one transliteration register.
  • “The Goldene Medina” (Pinsk callout) is a glossable Yiddishism that most readers will know, but a one-time inline gloss on first use (“the Goldene Medina — the ‘golden land,’ America”) will serve the sefer edition.
  • The “איך בין הכהן!” line (Pinsk callout) should carry a transliteration or gloss (“I am the kohen!”) for readers who do not read Yiddish fluently.
  • Section numbering runs to XII — a comfortable count — but II and III could fold into I without loss (see §3), bringing the printed count to a cleaner ten.
  • The “דוכן” / “duchen” toggling in Section XI (“they don't duchen during the week” → “Miron duchen daily”) reads as a copy-edit inconsistency. Pick one.
  • The “נשיאת כפים as עבודה” argument in Section VII is one paragraph but carries a lot of weight for the mincha-exclusion pshat. Consider promoting to its own section or expanding the Mishnah Tamid material in body-prose rather than reserving to fn8.
  • Fn12 (the Baal HaTanya sicha) explicitly states “precise citation not specified in the shiur”. For the sefer edition, locate the sicha in Likkutei Sichos or Sichos Kodesh and give a volume/page reference.

6.Bottom Line

A sefer-worthy two-part shiur with a clean architectural spine — the Chafetz Chaim's pause, the Midrash-of-eternal-Modim, Rav Elyashiv at 102 learning “thank you”, and then the four-way bavua of failed restorations culminating in Rav Nosson Adler. Three hard editorial fixes before print: (a) renumber the footnotes in body-order (currently 2, 3, 4, 1, 5, 19, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 20, 14, 15, 18, 16, 17); (b) resolve the nested <em> tags inside the four callout boxes; (c) reconstruct the two garbled strings in Section VIII (“שטעט יקי” and “נשבת גמר”) from the audio. Verify three factual anchors — the Djerba “entirely of Kohanim” claim, the 9/11 beis din roster, and Rav Elyashiv's age at the surgery. Standardize “the Rebbe” / “Rabbi Ephrati”, break the single-sentence lead into three paragraphs, and consider folding II–III into I to tighten the structure to ten sections. Fix these and this is one of the strongest two-sugya pairings in the tefillah series.
