Mareh Mekamos · Shiur Review

Baruch She'amar and Mizmor L'Todah

Shiur of 11 August 2024 (Zayin Av)
Content-based review · reviewer read the transcript, summary, and finished write-up · 10 August 2026

I. The Shiur in One Paragraph

This shiur covers two connected topics from the opening of Pesukei d'Zimra: the strange origin and halachic status of Baruch She'amar, and the meaning and significance of Mizmor L'Todah. On Baruch She'amar, the Rav surveys the tradition of the petek min hashamayim (note from Heaven) as recorded in the Tur, contrasts it with the remarkable alternative attribution to the Savoraim offered by Rabbeinu Shlomo MiGermaiza (Rashi's contemporary and friend), and traces the downstream halachic question raised by the Pri Chadash — how we may recite a brachah with Sheim u'Malchus that does not appear anywhere in the Talmud — and its resolution by the Pri Megadim. He then surveys the structural chiddush of the Aruch HaShulchan (the opening phrase of Baruch She'amar is a haktamah, not the brachah proper), the Magen Avraham's leniency for answering amen, and the inner symbolism of the brachah (ten Baruchs / ten utterances of Creation; 87 words = gematria of paz). On Mizmor L'Todah, the Rav establishes it as our daily substitute for the korban todah, explores its numerical symmetry with that offering (forty words = forty loaves), explains its omission on Shabbos, Erev Pesach, and Erev Yom Kippur, and ends with the largely forgotten Shulchan Aruch ruling to sing it with a melody — illustrated through a contemporary account of miraculous healing attributed to reinstating that practice. Throughout, the Rav weaves in historical narrative: the tragedy of Zayin Av, the Seville pogrom, the First Crusade martyrdom of Rabbeinu Shlomo MiGermaiza, the obscure Savoraic period, and the Dardaim of Yemen.

II. What Was Strongest

The most genuinely intellectually compelling material in this shiur is the double treatment of Baruch She'amar's origin — and the way those two views interact with the subsequent halachic discussion.

The petek min hashamayim tradition is well-known. But the Rav's introduction of Rabbeinu Shlomo MiGermaiza's Savoraim attribution is striking for two reasons. First, it is genuinely obscure: most who learn Baruch She'amar regularly have never heard it. Second, it sharpens the Pri Chadash's question in a way that the more common presentation does not: if the Anshei Knesset HaGedolah received a heavenly note, one can argue that the brachah predates all rabbinic categories entirely, and the Pri Megadim's resolution ("an ancient kabbalah predating the Talmud") flows naturally. But if the authors were the Savoraim — who came after the Talmud was sealed — then the question bites harder. The Savoraim unambiguously had no authority to compose brachos with Sheim u'Malchus. The Rav acknowledges this tension, though he does not fully press it.

Second strongest: the Mizmor L'Todah material. The forty-word / forty-loaf parallel is tight and memorable, and the case for daily hoda'ah — that we owe thanks every day because every day carries hidden miracles — is made with genuine warmth. The connection back to Birkas HaGomel (the same four categories, now democratized to cover everyone every morning) is a good piece of lomdus quietly embedded in the structure of the shiur.

Third: the Savoraic period discussion. It is genuinely rare for a beis medrash shiur to pause and illuminate those two hundred years between the sealing of the Talmud and the Geonim. The Ritva on Kiddushin is the right anchor for this discussion, and the observation that we have almost no independent Savoraic legacy — except, perhaps, Baruch She'amar — makes the attribution feel momentous even if unverifiable.

III. What Was Weaker — Points Worth Pushing Back On

The Pri Chadash's question is underresolved in the Savoraim direction. The Rav correctly notes that the Savoraim attribution sharpens the Pri Chadash's objection. But the offered resolution — "they were transmitting an older kabbalah, not creating something new" — applies cleanly to the Anshei Knesset HaGedolah version of the story, not the Savoraim version. The Anshei Knesset HaGedolah had full authority to institute brachos; if they received a heavenly note, there is nothing halakhically problematic about their enactment. But the Savoraim, by the Rav's own description, had no such authority — their role was editorial and transmissive, not legislative. If they authored Baruch She'amar, the Pri Chadash's problem has no clean resolution, and the brachah rests on weaker ground than the shiur suggests. A thorough treatment would acknowledge that Rabbeinu Shlomo's view, taken seriously, creates an enduring halachic difficulty with no fully satisfying answer in the Acharonim.
The miracle story as halachic argument. The Bnei Brak story of the ba'al tefillah whose cancer disappeared after reinstating the singing of Mizmor L'Todah is compelling as aggadah. But it functions rhetorically as the climactic argument for a halachic ruling — one that stands perfectly well on its own from the Shulchan Aruch, the Meiri, and the Mahari Abuhav. The shiur hedges appropriately ("I don't believe stories either"), but the very fact that the story ends the shiur means a listener absorbs its emotional weight as the primary reason to take the ruling seriously. The halachah deserves to stand on its own sources; the story, if told, belongs as an appendix, not as the closing argument. A talmid chochom listening would note the inversion.
The Dardaim digression. The mention of the Dardaim — the Yemenite anti-Zohar faction — is interesting, but the shiur introduces them abruptly, without completing the thought. Their excommunication by the Badatz is mentioned without context: what was the excommunication over, is it still operative, and what does it mean for their personal status? Once raised, a serious talmid expects these questions to be addressed. Either develop the point or leave it aside. As presented, it plants a question and walks away.

IV. Halachic and Lomdish Observations

The Aruch HaShulchan's haktamah theory may be the key to reconciling the two origin views. If the formal brachah of Baruch She'amar begins only at "Baruch Atah Hashem Elokeinu Melech HaOlam, hakeil ha'av harachaman…" — and the opening lines ("Baruch she'amar v'haya ha'olam…") are merely a poetic preamble — then it becomes possible that the Savoraim authored only the preamble, and the brachah body ("Baruch Atah Hashem") is older and of broader authority. This would partially reconcile Rabbeinu Shlomo's attribution with the petek tradition: the Anshei Knesset HaGedolah instituted the brachah body; the Savoraim composed the lyrical haktamah that introduces it. The shiur does not make this connection, but it is latent in the material.
The Magen Avraham's leniency has a logical dependency on the origin theory. The Magen Avraham permits answering amen during Baruch She'amar specifically because of its unique, non-Talmudic status — it operates by different rules than standard brachos. But this leniency is logically tied to the Anshei Knesset HaGedolah / heavenly-note theory. If the brachah was authored by the Savoraim with uncertain standing, the Magen Avraham's rationale may not apply: a brachah of questionable status is not the same as a uniquely authoritative one. The shiur does not explore this dependency.
The future annulment of all shiros except Mizmor L'Todah — and what this means for daily davening. The Shulchan Aruch's statement that all songs will be annulled in the future except Mizmor L'Todah (כל השירות עתידות ליבטל חוץ ממזמור לתודה) is not merely a statement about eschatology. The Meiri and others use it to establish that Mizmor L'Todah occupies a qualitative level above all other praise — not just higher than other liturgical passages, but standing alone in the era of ultimate redemption, when gratitude will be the only praise that remains. This connects directly to the well-known midrash that in the messianic era all prayers will cease except for hoda'ah. The shiur gestures at the singing halachah but does not develop this eschatological dimension, which would significantly deepen the listener's sense of what the mizmor is.
The forty loaves of the korban todah — why forty? The shiur notes the forty-word / forty-loaf parallel but does not explain why the korban todah required forty loaves in the first place (ten each of four kinds: chametz, matzah, chalos, rekikim). This is not incidental: the korban todah was unique in requiring the largest accompaniment of loaves of any individual offering. Some explain this is because hoda'ah is qualitatively the greatest form of avodah — hence the amplified accompaniment. That lomdus would tie together the numerical parallel, the future survival of Mizmor L'Todah alone, and the daily practice the shiur is advocating.

V. Follow-Up Questions Worth Asking the Rav

1 If we follow Rabbeinu Shlomo MiGermaiza that Baruch She'amar was authored by the Savoraim — and if the Savoraim had no standing to institute brachos with Sheim u'Malchus — does that affect the bedieved ruling? In other words: if someone, following this shitah, did not recite Baruch She'amar at all, would he still have fulfilled his obligation of Pesukei d'Zimra? How do the poskim rule when both the obligation and its framing brachah are in dispute simultaneously?
2 The Aruch HaShulchan's structural distinction between the haktamah and the brachah body — does any posek rule practically differently for hefsek during the opening lines versus the body of Baruch She'amar? The shiur mentions the theoretical point but does not bring a practical ruling. Is there a posek who explicitly permits talking between "Baruch she'amar" and "Baruch Atah Hashem" while prohibiting interruption once the brachah body begins?
3 The Magen Avraham's leniency to answer amen during Baruch She'amar — does the Magen Avraham extend this to the entire brachah body (i.e., even after "Baruch Atah Hashem…")? Or only to the haktamah? The shiur presents the leniency without specifying its scope, and the haktamah theory suggests these may be distinct questions.
4 Mizmor L'Todah on Tisha B'Av: the shiur mentions that the Luach Eretz Yisrael by Rav Tukachinsky and the Hinkis luach address the question but does not give a bottom line. What is the Rav's own practice and psak? The machloikes is significant because Tisha B'Av is simultaneously a day when we do not say Tachanun and a fast day — the chametz issue that drives the Erev Pesach omission does not apply, but there may be other considerations.
5 The Ba'al HaTanya's claim that the Rambam knew Kabbalah but deliberately concealed it — where does this appear in the Alter Rebbe's writings? Is this a specific statement in Tanya or the Iggeres HaKodesh, or is it found in one of the Hemshechim? And: does this position have any weight against the Dardaim who use the Rambam's apparent silence on Kabbalah as a halachic argument, or would the Alter Rebbe agree that even if the Rambam knew, the practical halachah may differ?

VI. Bottom Line

A rich and genuinely learned shiur whose intellectual high points — the Savoraim attribution of Baruch She'amar, the Aruch HaShulchan's structural chiddush, and the campaign to revive the singing of Mizmor L'Todah — reward careful attention; its main weaknesses are the unresolved tension between the Savoraim view and the Pri Chadash's question, and the rhetorical over-reliance on a miracle story to close material that the Shulchan Aruch and Rishonim already establish on their own authority.
