29 September 2024 — סְלִיחוֹת — Chesed v'Emes Before Yom HaDin

Independent review · read fresh, no exposure to the writing process · 23 August 2026

1.Overall Read

This is a tightly built, emotionally charged shiur-rework that succeeds because its structure mirrors its subject: nine short movements walking the reader from the Brisker Rav's mashal to the concrete kabbalos for the four days ahead. At roughly 2,000 words of body text plus fifteen footnotes, it is shorter than the Ketores piece and better paced for it — nothing overstays. The historical framing (first-night Slichos, Motzaei Shabbos, reservists being called up as the Rebbe spoke) is used with restraint: it anchors Section VII rather than saturating the whole document. The dual-layer footnote system continues to work well.

The piece is strongest in Sections I, V, and VII — the Brisker mashal, the Leket Yosher chiddush, and the moment-in-time closing. It is weakest in the citation apparatus (three footnotes are effectively unpinned) and in a couple of places where the transcription slips into shorthand that a reader coming to this cold will not parse. There are no chronological or factual errors of the "Franz Joseph" magnitude, but there are several loose citations that need tightening before this can sit alongside the Ketores piece in the sefer.

2.What Works

  • Section I (the Brisker Rav's mashal) is the strongest opening in any of the pieces I have reviewed so far. The three-tier structure — Yid, balagulah, horse — is developed with concrete stakes (thirty days of fear; loss of horse and wagon; no awareness at all) and lands the application without over-explaining. The line "A Yid is not made to be a horse" is the emotional hinge of the entire piece; it earns its weight.
  • Section II's pivot from source to demand ("If reciting the thirteen middos is the master key, why have we been saying them year after year…") is exactly the move a serious reader needs. The answer — ya'asu lefanai ka'seder ha'zeh, not a password but an instruction — is theologically clean and gives the rest of the shiur its argumentative spine.
  • Section V (Leket Yosher / Malveh Hashem) is the intellectual high point. The Rambam's din Torah venue rule → Eved loveh l'ish malveh → tzedakah as a loan to Hashem → therefore we as malveh can insist on the Beis Din HaGadol shel Rachamim is a striking chain, and the piece handles the audacity of it correctly (paragraph on "the language is daring; we would not formulate it on our own"). This is the kind of chiddush a sefer wants at its center.
  • The kafin v'ani v'lo yada reading in Section VI is the freshest single insight in the piece. Turning the closing refrain Rachmana d'anei le'aniyei aneinan into a plea that Hashem see the poverty we cannot ourselves feel is a strong reframing of a familiar line, and the historical situation (galus, hester panim) grounds it without preachiness.
  • Section VII (the moment we are in) uses the reservists-being-called-up detail with restraint. The Rebbe's line — "Had we known on Erev Simchas Torah what would happen the next day, we would have davened differently" — is the right note to end on. It is not sentimental; it is diagnostic.
  • Section VIII (Practical Kabbalos) is the right structural choice. Most reworks end on an exhortation; this one ends on a checklist grouped under the four rubrics the shiur developed (Emes, Chesed, Tefillah, Achdus). It gives the reader something to do on Sunday morning.

3.Where It's Weak or Could Be Sharper

The Brisker Rav pizmon quotation in Section I is not quite the pizmon. The italicized line "Klal Yisrael Yidden tremble with fear from the upcoming Yom HaDin" is a paraphrase in English of what is presumably a Hebrew or Yiddish line from the first-night Slichos. As it stands, the reader cannot tell whether this is the Brisker Rav's own gloss, the pizmon itself, or the transcriber's summary. Either quote the Hebrew (with translation) or attribute it explicitly as a paraphrase — "the pizmon's opening, in paraphrase," or similar. A sefer reader will notice the seam.

Section III (Tanna D'Bei Eliyahu) is the thinnest section. It compresses to two short paragraphs and does not develop the b'agudah achas teaching beyond stating it. Given that achdus reappears in the Practical Kabbalos as its own rubric, the section should either (a) grow by a paragraph — specifically, what does tzedakah b'guf look like operationally, and what is the textual anchor for connecting it to the Tanna D'Bei Eliyahu passage — or (b) fold into Section IV. As placed, it reads as a way-station between the Gemara Rosh HaShanah and the Tehillim pasuk rather than a section that earns its own numeral.

Section IV's Chasam Sofer gematria arrives without its arithmetic. The parenthetical "(with one extra letter, but the connection holds)" is the kind of hedge that a reader trusts a shiur to skate past but a sefer to nail down. Either give the actual numeric values (chesed = 72, emes = 441, sum = 513; slichos = 504; explain the one-letter kollel convention) or omit the gematria and keep only the thematic point. The current formulation reads as if the writer knows the arithmetic doesn't quite work and is asking the reader to overlook it.

The transition from Section IV to Section V is abrupt. Section IV ends on chesed as "practical action" in a single one-sentence paragraph. Section V then opens with the Alter Rebbe's letter without a bridge. A single connecting sentence — "The koach of that practical action is captured in a startling formulation from the Alter Rebbe" — would keep the reader on rails.

Section IX (Conclusion) is short and largely a restatement. The final two paragraphs do the job but do not bracket back to the Brisker Rav's mashal. A one-line return — "Not the horse, not merely the balagulah, but the Yid: arriving at Yom HaDin with thirty days of preparation compressed into four, with chesed and emes in hand" — would close the arc that Section I opened. This is the same closing-bracket problem the Ketores review flagged; consider it a house pattern to watch for.

4.Factual / Citation Concerns

Tanna D'Bei Eliyahu framing in Section III. The text describes the compilation as "teachings transmitted by Eliyahu HaNavi in his daily chavrusa with Rav Anan." This is the traditional attribution (Kesuvos 106a on Rav Anan's relationship with Eliyahu), but stated flatly it will read to a critical reader as ignoring the substantial scholarly and rabbinic discussion of the work's actual redaction. Either flag it as the traditional attribution ("in the traditional attribution…") or move the biographical framing to the footnote and keep the body text on the teaching itself. Footnote 5 currently cites Tanna D'Bei Eliyahu Rabbah 23 with a "cf. Zuta 21" — both references should be verified against a printed edition; the b'agudah achas teaching's exact location varies by edition and I would not stake a sefer on the current siman numbers without a check.

Footnote 4 (Shabbos 133b) for mah hu rachum. The locus classicus for mah hu…af atah in the imitatio Dei formulation is Shabbos 133b on zeh Keli v'anveihu, but the specific wording מָה הוּא רַחוּם — אַף אַתָּה רַחוּם is more commonly cited from Sotah 14a (on acharei Hashem Elokeichem teileichu) and the Sifri on Eikev. Verify which sugya is intended; if the point is the general principle, Sotah 14a is the safer primary reference and Shabbos 133b the secondary. The Rambam cross-reference (Hilchos De'os 1:6) is correct.

Footnote 7 (Chasam Sofer gematria) is effectively unpinned — "general reference…precise location varies by edition." This is the same problem the Ketores review flagged for the Chasam Sofer quip: an unpinned Chasam Sofer citation belongs either with a concrete siman or with an explicit "in the tradition attributed to the Chasam Sofer" flag in the body text. As placed, the body text says "the Chasam Sofer notes" — which implies a locatable source that the footnote then quietly concedes it cannot produce.

Footnote 15 (Megillah, kafin v'ani v'lo yada) is unpinned. "General reference; the sugya on the ani who is unaware of his impoverishment" is a working note, not a mareh mekomos. The phrase כָּפִין וְעָנִי וְלֹא יָדַע should be traceable to a specific sugya (Megillah 7b on Purim gifts is a candidate; verify). This is the single reading in the shiur that a reader is most likely to want to look up, and it is the least locatable. Fix before print.

Footnote 12 (Leket Yosher) needs a volume/siman. The Leket Yosher is a specific two-volume work (Yoreh Deah and Orach Chaim, ed. Freimann); the chiddush on malveh/loveh and the Beis Din HaGadol shel Rachamim should be locatable to a page. Given that Section V is the intellectual centerpiece of the piece, this citation carries the most weight and deserves the tightest pinning.

Footnote 13 (Rambam Hilchos Sanhedrin 6:7). Verify. The rule that the malveh can compel the venue and drag the loveh to the Beis Din HaGadol is discussed in Hilchos Sanhedrin ch. 6, but the exact halacha number should be confirmed; the parallel in Hilchos Malveh v'Loveh is worth locating specifically rather than cross-referencing generically.

5.Small Polish Suggestions

  • The italicized-term density is high — Yid, balagulah, ferd, sus, knas, parnassah, davens all appear in the first three paragraphs. The shiur register justifies this, but consider whether ferd and sus together (both meaning "horse," one Yiddish and one Hebrew) is necessary in Section I; one would carry the point.
  • "Aleph-Beis-Lamed-Vav-Lamed" in Section I's first bullet is presumably Elul spelled out. As presented, a reader unfamiliar with the transcription convention will not parse it. Either write Elul plainly or add a parenthetical gloss on first use.
  • "We have only four days of Slichos this year (Ashkenazic communities; Sephardim already had the entire month)." The parenthetical is slightly off — Sephardim begin Slichos on Rosh Chodesh Elul, so at first-night-of-Ashkenazic-Slichos they have had roughly four weeks, not "the entire month." Tighten to "Sephardim have been saying Slichos since Rosh Chodesh Elul" or similar.
  • Section V refers to "R' Yoyzpa Estraicher." The standard transliterations are "Yosef ben Moshe" (his given name) and "Ostreicher" or "Ostraicher" (family designation); "Yoyzpa" is the Yiddish diminutive and "Estraicher" is a phonetic variant. For a sefer, standardize to "R' Yosef (Yoyzpa) Ostreicher" on first mention. Also: the Terumas HaDeshen's biographical parenthetical ("fled Germany during a redifus, settled in Austria, and opened a yeshivah there") is good historical detail but interrupts the halachic argument; consider moving to the footnote.
  • Section VII: "As the Rebbe spoke, his phone showed a text" — the phrasing "the Rebbe" appears here for the first time in the body text (elsewhere it is "Rabbi Ephrati"). Standardize. If the honorific "the Rebbe" is chosen for the closing section for tonal reasons, that is defensible, but it should be a deliberate choice, not a slip.
  • The heb-title in the header has nikkud on every word; the body text switches between vocalized and unvocalized Hebrew. For a sefer, decide one convention (probably: vocalized for titles and blockquotes, unvocalized for inline terms) and apply it consistently.
  • The closing footer line says "personal asides have been omitted." Given the Rebbe's phone-text moment in Section VII is a personal aside preserved deliberately, the footer's blanket claim is slightly inaccurate. Tighten to "extraneous personal asides omitted; the closing moment-in-time preserved."

6.Bottom Line

A tightly built, emotionally grounded Slichos shiur whose Brisker mashal opening and Leket Yosher chiddush carry it; the citation apparatus is the main weakness (three footnotes — Chasam Sofer, Megillah, Leket Yosher — are unpinned in ways a sefer cannot afford), Section III should either grow or fold, and a one-line bracket back to the horse-Yid-balagulah image in Section IX would close the arc — fix the citations and this belongs in the sefer as-is.
