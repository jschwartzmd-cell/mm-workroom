2024-07-04 — The Kotel HaMa'aravi: History, Sanctity, and Halacha

Content-based review · reviewer read the transcript, summary, and finished write-up · 10 August 2026

1.The Shiur in One Paragraph

This is a broad survey shiur that takes the Kotel HaMa'aravi as its subject and works through three concentric circles: history, theology, and halacha. The Rav begins with the physical reality of the wall — Tukachinsky's stratigraphy of five distinct building eras from Shlomo HaMelech through the Arabs of 1924 — then descends underground with Wilson's excavations, and then ascends to the central lomdish question: what exactly is this wall the retaining wall of? The Radvaz's minority view (it is the wall of the Azarah) is set against the overwhelming majority consensus (it is the outer wall of Har HaBayis), and the halachic stakes — whether anyone may stand in the plaza at all — are made concrete by describing the Brisker Rav's practice of praying from far behind the gate. From there the shiur moves to the Midrash's guarantee that the Shechinah never departed, the three-century burial of the wall and its rediscovery, and lands finally on two clusters of practical halacha: the kvitlach/touching question (with the Avnei Nezer's svara, the chillul argument, and minhag Yisrael as the operative heter), and Rav Moshe Feinstein's ruling against removing stones. The arc is history → sanctity → boundary debate → practical consequence, and it holds together well.

2.What Was Strongest

  • The three-centuries-of-burial proved by negative evidence. The argument from Binyamin of Tudela and Petachiah is suggestive. But the clincher is Rabbeinu Eshtori HaParchi — a Rishon, not a casual traveler, who dedicated seven-plus years to systematically recording every halachic and geographic detail of Eretz Yisrael, described the gates of Har HaBayis, and said nothing at all about the Kotel. A man who couldn't miss something that important if it were there. That silence is more probative than any direct testimony, and the Rav deploys it well. It also carries an unstated pathos: the wall was there all along, swallowed by rubble, waiting.
  • The Tukachinsky biography. The story of the ten-year-old orphan making his way to Eretz Yisrael alone, the dateline controversy, and especially the dust-in-the-apartment story ("on this sand walked Avraham, Yitzchak, v'Yaakov — how can you just wipe it away?") make the research human. Tukachinsky comes across as a man in whom love of Eretz Yisrael and halachic rigor were one thing, not two. That framing earns him intellectual credibility: his willingness to engage secular archaeology is not despite his hashkafos but an expression of them.
  • The Radvaz and the Brisker Rav, paired. Abstract machloches become concrete when you name the person who lives by the stringent view and describe exactly where he stands during tefilah — behind the gate, elevated, davening with kavanah but not approaching. The Rav doesn't ridicule this; he treats it as a live position deserving respect. That's the right tone.
  • Rav Moshe's teshuvah on the stones. The pragmatic framing ("millions of Jews would line up — within weeks, no Kotel would remain") is not merely a crowd-control argument; it is a derash on the idea that Hakadosh Baruch Hu left the wall for it to remain, not to be carried home pebble by pebble. That is a genuinely theological point, and Rav Moshe's aghast reaction to the question is memorable.
  • The Avnei Nezer's svara. The elegance of the limitation — bi'ah b'miktzas applies only when the entry-point is physically large enough to admit a full body — is the kind of precisely calibrated lomdish move that makes you want to go learn the teshuvah itself. It raises more questions than it settles, and that is a sign of a real chiddush.

3.What Was Weaker or Worth Pushing Back On

The mountain-height question is opened and abandoned. The Rav raises the puzzle at the outset: Yeshayahu says Har HaBayis will be established at the top of the mountains, Chazal say Eretz Yisrael is higher than all other lands, yet you walk down to get to the Kotel. He says "we'll deal with that in a moment" — and then never does. In a written piece this kind of open thread damages the argument; in a live shiur it is a small wound. But it is a real wound. The resolution is not obvious: one answer is that Jerusalem's topography was altered by the Roman leveling of the city; another is that the pasuk speaks eschatologically rather than literally about current topography; a third invokes the spiritual-elevation interpretation of "gevoha." Any of these would have completed the thought.

The Shechinah distinction is asserted rather than argued. The Rav says forthrightly that he does not know what he is talking about when it comes to the difference between the essential Shechinah and the radiating emanation. The honesty is charming, but it leaves the resolution suspended. The Achron invoked in the transcript is the Orach LeNer (in Binyan Tziyon), but no text is read and no mechanism is explained. A careful listener is left with a feeling that the theological problem has been waved away rather than answered. The distinction is real and defensible — the Acharonim do elaborate it — but the shiur doesn't earn it.

Minhag Yisrael as a heter for a possible d'oraita. The Rav presents minhag Yisrael as the "strongest" heter for touching the Kotel and placing kvitlach. Rav Ovadia Yosef's "im einan nevi'im, bnei nevi'im hen" is powerful — and probably correct. But a careful learner should press: minhag Yisrael is the great equalizer for rabbinic prohibitions and for safek; is it by itself sufficient to permit what may be a d'oraita of kares (entering Har HaBayis while tamei)? The cases where minhag overrides a biblical issur are few and contested. The honest answer is probably that the Avnei Nezer's svara, taken together with the chillul argument and the minhag, form a cumulative case rather than any single heter being decisive — but the shiur presents them as independent options rather than a converging defense.

4.Halachic / Lomdish Observations

The Avnei Nezer's limitation on bi'ah b'miktzas is contested. The svara — that the body-sized-opening condition limits the rule — is not universally accepted. The Minchas Chinuch and others read the Gemara in Zevachim as applying bi'ah b'miktzas regardless of the size of the opening; the principle derives from the language אל המקדש לא תבא, and "entry" is determined by direction and intent, not by whether a full body could follow. If the Avnei Nezer's reading fails on the Gemara, the entire svara falls, and the kvitlach practice is back to resting on minhag alone. This debate is worth flagging for anyone who wants to learn the sugya.

The Ra'avad/Rambam dispute was mentioned but dropped. The shiur notes in passing that according to the Ra'avad, bi'ah b'miktzas carries only makkas mardus (rabbinic), not kares (biblical). If the Ra'avad is followed, the entire scaffolding of the heter question looks different: there is no biblical issur to justify, only a rabbinic one, for which minhag is more than adequate. This point deserves more development — not because the Ra'avad is necessarily correct, but because knowing which tier of issur you are dealing with changes the weight of every heter.

The pritzim v'chiluleha argument needs sharper limiting principle. The shiur says most poskim reject extending the Avodah Zarah Gemara (that invaders' violation strips kedushah) to the Kotel, on the grounds that the Kotel remained standing. But this needs a more precise articulation: the Gemara says that vessels and stones taken out and desecrated lose their kedushah; it does not say a structure that survived in place loses it. The wall was never "u'va'u bah" in the sense of being removed and profaned. That distinction is the actual sevara, and stating it clearly would strengthen the dismissal of the chillul argument.

The Radvaz's date and the geometry question. The shiur says archaeological measurements prove geometrically that the wall cannot be the wall of the Azarah. This is asserted, not demonstrated. A listener who wants to verify would need to know: given the recorded dimensions of Har HaBayis (500 amot by 500 amot, as in the Mishnah in Midos), where exactly does the Azarah fall within that space, and where does the wall we see stand relative to it? The claim is almost certainly correct — the numbers do work out — but the shiur treats the conclusion as a given rather than walking through even one cheshbon.

5.Follow-up Questions Worth Asking the Rav

Q1. The Rav opened with the puzzle of going down to the Kotel when the pasuk says Har HaBayis is on top of the mountains. He said "we'll deal with that in a moment" and never returned to it. What is the actual resolution — geological, eschatological, or spiritual?
Q2. On the two-level Shechinah distinction (essential presence vs. emanating light): the Orach LeNer in Binyan Tziyon is mentioned briefly. Is there a fuller written treatment of this distinction, and does any Achron actually explain the mechanism by which the emanation (as opposed to the Presence itself) could persist at a site where the Presence had withdrawn?
Q3. On the Avnei Nezer's svara limiting bi'ah b'miktzas to body-sized openings: does the Rav hold that this svara is correct on the sugya in Zevachim? How does he respond to the Minchas Chinuch or other poskim who reject the size condition?
Q4. The Radvaz is described as the first to take a definitive position on the wall's identity. Were there no Rishonim who addressed the boundary question before the wall was rediscovered in the 15th century — even theoretically, based on the measurements in Midos or Middos?
Q5. On the modern ascent of Har HaBayis: the shiur says the overwhelming majority of poskim forbid it even after mikveh. Is there a teshuvah specifically from Rav Moshe Feinstein or the Chazon Ish on ascending (as opposed to the general question of Har HaBayis status), and what exactly is the grounds for the prohibition if a person has immersed — is it the additional tumah of tumas meis that mikveh alone doesn't address?

6.Bottom Line

A well-crafted survey shiur that earns its length. The historical arc — from Ottoman-era legend through Wilson's excavations through three centuries of burial — is vivid and well-paced. The Tukachinsky material is the unexpected gem: a man whose love for Eretz Yisrael and rigorous halakha formed one integrated personality, and whose willingness to engage archaeology made his conclusions trustworthy on both sides of the aisle. The halachic material is real but stays at the level of orientation rather than deep analysis; the kvitlach sugya in particular deserves a full shiur of its own. The two main weaknesses are the abandoned mountain-height question (left conspicuously open) and the Shechinah distinction (asserted but not earned). Neither is fatal. A talmid chochom who sat through this shiur would leave with an accurate map of the terrain and a productive set of questions — which is precisely what a good survey shiur should accomplish.
