Mareh Mekamos · Independent Editorial Review

Baruch She'amar and Mizmor L'Todah

Write-up dated 11 August 2024 (Zayin Av)
Independent review · read fresh, no exposure to the writing process · 10 August 2026

I. Overall Read

This is a well-constructed piece of Torah prose — scholarly, substantive, and legible to an educated lay reader without talking down to a serious learner. The dual-layer footnote structure works well: the short citation suffices for quick lookup; the expanded note provides the context that transforms a reference list into a genuine study tool. The HTML formatting is clean, the callout boxes are used judiciously, and the flow from Baruch She'amar to Mizmor L'Todah feels organic rather than forced. The historical passages — Zayin Av, the Kehillos Shum, the Savoraic period — are genuinely illuminating and not merely decorative. For a write-up of an oral shiur, this is a high-quality rework.

II. What Works

  • Structure. The eight-section layout with numbered headings gives the reader clear wayposts. Nothing feels buried or orphaned. The movement from origin (Section II) → halachic question (III) → status (IV) → symbolism (V) → Mizmor L'Todah (VII–VIII) is logical and satisfying.
  • The Savoraim passage. The digression on the Savoraic period — who they were, when they lived, what they did to the text of the Gemara — is genuinely useful. The Ritva on Kiddushin as supporting evidence is well chosen. This material appears rarely in popular Torah literature.
  • Rabbeinu Shlomo MiGermaiza's biography. The write-up correctly places him as Rashi's contemporary and friend, ties his martyrdom to the First Crusade (1096), and connects the Kehillos Shum to the broader Sephardic-Ashkenazic story. This is precisely the kind of historical depth that makes Mareh Mekamos write-ups distinctive.
  • Mizmor L'Todah — the forty-word parallel. Tight, precise, and memorable. The connection between the forty loaves of the korban todah and the forty words of the mizmor is explained cleanly without over-elaboration.
  • The Shulchan Aruch singing halachah and the Bnei Brak account. An engaging and practical close — the ruling is established from primary sources (Mechaber, Meiri, Mahari Abuhav) before the story is told, so the story illustrates rather than proves. The hedge ("to be received with the appropriate measure of reverence and discernment") is well-placed.

III. Where It Is Weak

The Pri Chadash resolution is incomplete. Section III presents the Pri Chadash's question (how can we say a brachah not in Chazal?) and the Pri Megadim's answer (pre-Mishnaic kabbalah). But the write-up does not make clear that this resolution applies to the Anshei Knesset HaGedolah version of events, and not obviously to the Savoraim version offered by Rabbeinu Shlomo. The final sentence of that passage — "this is a real and continuing difficulty in the Acharonim" — is correct but too brief. A reader who has followed the argument carefully will be left with an unresolved tension that the write-up treats as settled. The sefer edition should either elaborate the Acharonic discussion or openly acknowledge the unresolved difficulty.
The Dardaim callout is a loose end. The Dardaim are introduced, their excommunication by the Badatz is mentioned, and the passage closes with the Ba'al HaTanya's counter-claim that the Rambam knew Kabbalah. But no source is cited for that claim, and the practical implications for the Dardaim's status are not addressed. Excommunication raises serious questions in halacha (kiddushin, gittin, communal obligations) that a reader familiar with the laws of cherem will not ignore. The callout box should either be expanded with the Ba'al HaTanya's source and the contemporary status of the excommunication, or trimmed to remove the cherem mention entirely.
Section VI (Hodu and Nusach) to Section VII (Mizmor L'Todah) transition is abrupt. The Hodu discussion ends with the sentence "Everything else was added later," and Section VII opens immediately with "Mizmor L'Todah (Tehillim 100) is recited daily after Baruch She'amar." There is no bridging sentence or transitional paragraph. A sefer reader who is following the argument about what belongs inside the Baruch She'amar / Yishtabach frame will want to know where Mizmor L'Todah falls on that spectrum before the material on its significance begins.
The phrasing "the Rebbe relayed an account from his brother-in-law" is presenter-voice and needs to go. This is the only place where the oral shiur's first-person framing appears directly in the prose. For a sefer edition the phrase should be rendered in third person without reference to the oral delivery context.

IV. Factual and Citation Concerns

Concern: "The Or Zarua (the grandson of the Ramban — distinct from the famous Or Zarua of Rabbeinu Yitzchak of Vienna)." This is the write-up's most significant factual problem. There is no well-known sefer called "Or Zarua" written by the Ramban's grandson. The famous Or Zarua is emphatically Rabbeinu Yitzchak of Vienna — a first-generation Ashkenazic Rishon, entirely unrelated to the Ramban. The Tur in OC 51 cites the petek min hashamayim tradition from "the Geonim" or from unnamed earlier sources; the Beis Yosef traces the chain of transmission more carefully. The "grandson of the Ramban" appears to have entered the shiur as an oral approximation that was then literalized in the write-up. Until the correct source is positively identified, the footnote in the book edition should be revised to note the uncertainty and cite the Tur's actual language directly, without an attribution that cannot be verified.
Concern: The "achlu v'kirkeru" citation (fn. 1). Footnote 1 reads: "The Gemara elaborates the three days of 'eating, drinking, and reveling' from Zayin Av to Tisha B'Av…" and cites Gittin 57a "regarding the Romans." The Gittin 57a reference describes the Roman destruction; using it to source Babylonian behavior (Nevuzaradan, Nevuchadnetzar) is a category slip. The actual Talmudic discussion of Nevuzaradan's three days in the Beis HaMikdash is in different passages (cf. Gittin 57b, or the Midrash on Yirmiyahu / Eichah Rabbah). The source for this specific phrase applied to Nevuzaradan's forces should be verified and the footnote corrected.
Concern: The Mishnah Berurah's unusual phrasing. The write-up renders the Chofetz Chaim's unusual hedge as "yesh li lishav'ev" — a phrase that does not correspond to any standard locution in Hebrew or Yiddish. The transcript records the Rav saying "yesh li s'yashav," which also does not match standard Mishnah Berurah language. The actual phrase in Mishnah Berurah OC 51 regarding hefsek between Baruch She'amar and Yishtabach should be checked against the primary text; the current rendition in the write-up should be verified and corrected if needed.
Minor concern: The Ba'al HaTanya on the Rambam and Kabbalah. The claim that the Alter Rebbe wrote that "the Rambam certainly knew the Zohar and Kabbalah but deliberately concealed it" appears in the callout box without citation. This is a significant and contested historical claim — some researchers specifically dispute it. A sefer edition should provide the precise source (Iggeres HaKodesh? Likutei Torah? Elsewhere?) and acknowledge that this view is the Alter Rebbe's, not a universally accepted historical conclusion.

V. Small Polish

  • The date line reads "Reworked 30 April 2026" — this is a relic of the original write-up date. The sefer edition should update accordingly.
  • In the lead paragraph: "The strange origin of Baruch She'amar; the halachic implications of its non-Talmudic source; and the daily korban todah we offer through Mizmor L'Todah." — "we offer" is first-person. Consider: "…the daily korban todah embodied in Mizmor L'Todah."
  • Section V heading "The Inner Structure and Symbolism of Baruch She'amar" — fits awkwardly alongside Section IV's "The Halachic Status of Baruch She'amar." Consider combining or reordering to: (IV) Symbolism → (V) Status, so the structural and symbolic material prepares for the halachic discussion rather than following it.
  • The phrase "Siddur Rashi" in fn. 14: it is debated among scholars whether the Siddur HaRashi is genuinely Rashi's composition or a student compilation. A footnote acknowledging this scholarly note would be appropriate.
  • Footnote 6 refers to "daf 2a–3b" for the Ritva's Savoraim attribution in Kiddushin. Standard Vilna Shas references should specify amudim clearly; "the opening sugya through amud 3b" is more precise and avoids ambiguity.
✦ ✦ ✦

VI. Bottom Line

A genuinely strong write-up — substantive, well-cited, and historically rich — that needs one structural correction (the unresolved Savoraim tension in Section III), one significant factual check (the "Or Zarua, grandson of the Ramban" attribution), and one voice correction ("the Rebbe relayed") before it is ready for the sefer edition.
