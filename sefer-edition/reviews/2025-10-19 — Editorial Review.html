Mareh Mekamos · Independent Editorial Review

19 October 2025 — Siyum שמונה עשרה: יהיו לרצון, אלוקי נצור, and the מחלוקת of Pressburg

Independent review · read fresh, from the finished HTML only · 12 October 2026

1.Overall Read

A fourteen-section writeup (roughly 4,100 words of body plus a twenty-two-note Mareh Mekomos apparatus of about 1,600 words) on the close of Shemoneh Esrei. The spine is clear for the first two-thirds: the Oseh HaShalom / HaMevarech question (I), the book Chut HaMeshulash (II), the Pressburg split (III), the placement of Yihyu Leratzon against Elokai Netzor (IV), the history of Elokai Netzor and Mar b. Ravina (V–VII). The last third (VIII–XIV) is looser: lashon hara, kavanah, when to lengthen, the verse of one’s name, biblical names, the name as the soul, and reward and punishment, in an order that is partly thematic and partly the order in which they came up. The document’s hard editorial issues are six: (a) a genealogy in Section II that contradicts itself and contradicts its own footnote; (b) a gematria in Section I that does not compute; (c) the voice of oral delivery throughout (“last time we addressed,” “I forgot to mention,” “I personally practiced”); (d) a near-duplicate pair of passages (XI and XIII) with an unrelated section (XII) wedged between them; (e) an apparatus that still carries working notes to the editor (“verify,” “should be cross-referenced”) and several notes that contradict the body; and (f) Section VIII, which announces two questions, answers one, and attributes both to a person never otherwise introduced.

2.What Works

  • The argument of Sections IV–VII. The sequence is a model of how to set a halachic dispute for a general reader: the ambiguity of the verb in Yihyu Leratzon, the Gemara’s question, David’s eighteen chapters (with the nineteen-chapter wrinkle handled in one parenthesis), the Rashba, the Vilna Gaon’s objection, the Mishnah Berurah’s “both,” and then the history of Elokai Netzor that explains why the question exists at all.
  • Section VI as a piece of construction. The Worms legend about Rashi’s mother, which the historian “proved” never happened, is used to introduce the Gemara it came from, which in turn leads to the glass at the wedding and to the prayer itself. Legend, text, custom, liturgy in four short steps; the best passage in the document.
  • The Yom Kippur Minchah callout (after IV) is exactly the right use of a callout: a real aside, short, with a stated practical answer.
  • The two kavanah paragraphs in IX (words said without kavanah; the Radak’s thoughts beyond words) are the most portable material in the writeup. The Hong Kong joke is placed so that it illustrates the point and is not the point.
  • The name-verse section (XI) is real source-history. It moves from the claimed source (the Shelah) to the admission that the origin is obscure, to the printed record (the parenthetical Rashi, the complaint of Shem Ephraim, the first siddur appearance, the Yaavetz). That is how such a custom should be traced.
  • Footnote hygiene. The twenty-two references run in body order from 1 to 22 with matching back-links, the short/expanded format in the notes is consistent, and the notes do the work of the scholarly apparatus without cluttering the body.
  • The irony paragraph in III. “We all want shalom — and that is what they fought about” is short, earned, and stops where it should.

3.Where It’s Weak or Could Be Sharper

Section II’s genealogy contradicts itself. The text says a grandchild of the Chasam Sofer wrote a biography of three generations: “his grandfather Rabbi Akiva Eiger, his father the Chasam Sofer, and his own father the Ksav Sofer.” A man cannot have the Chasam Sofer as his father and the Ksav Sofer as his father; and if he is the Chasam Sofer’s grandchild, the Chasam Sofer is his grandfather. Footnote 1 then calls the author the grandson of the Ksav Sofer and great-grandson of the Chasam Sofer, which is a third version. Settle the relationships once, in the body, and make the note agree.

The voice is the voice of a live shiur. “Last time we addressed…,” “I forgot to mention one fascinating fact,” “We discussed at length,” “Even in 2025 we cannot forget this,” “The past year, when we were in Worms with HaRav zt”l,” “(I personally practiced this minhag in my youth…),” “Perhaps he was a Litvak,” “(I have actually seen a copy of that siddur.)” For a sefer edition these should be recast in the third person or dropped; the ethnic joke and the dated “2025” in particular do not belong. The notes slip between “we” (body) and “the shiur” (notes) within the same document.

Section VIII announces two questions and leaves the first dangling. “We left off with two questions raised by Rabbeinu Baruch” — no such person has appeared and nothing says who he is. The first question (why this prayer was standardized) is never answered; the second (why lashon hara) is answered in one paragraph that would sit better inside Section VII, where Elokai Netzor Leshoni MeRa is introduced. As it stands, Section VIII interrupts the move from the text of the prayer (VII) to its kavanos (IX). Fold the lashon hara answer into VII; state the first question as an open one and say so.

Sections XI and XIII repeat each other, and XII sits between them. XI says a person in this world is identified by looks, career, vocation and name, and in the next world only by name. XIII says it again in nearly the same words, then adds the Arizal. The Bnei Yissaschar callout belongs with the Arizal material. Section XII (biblical names, Nimrod and Yishmael) interrupts that line. Merge XIII into XI, and place XII after them; consider whether XII can answer the question it raises (which biblical names are appropriate) or should say plainly that it leaves it open.

Section III has a missing bridge. It opens on the Pressburg controversy over nusach, runs through five paragraphs on the Chassidic revolution (posture, the cherem, nusach, the Chofetz Chaim, Nusach HaAri), and only in its final sub-section returns to Oseh against HaMevarech. The reader has to wait several hundred words to learn why the revolution is here. One sentence at the top saying so would do it. Also, “the Chasam Sofer and others adjusted certain things in response” is unexplained: adjusted what, in which direction?

The statement of what Mar b. Ravina prayed is unclear. Section VII gives “Elokai netzor leshoni merah… ad kol machshevosam” with an ellipsis in the middle of a quotation and then says the printed nusach differs, that Nusach Sefard “keeps that explicit,” and that “that is what one is supposed to have in kavanah.” “That” has no antecedent. Either give the Gemara’s wording and say plainly how the siddur differs, or say less.

Section IV’s sentence on Hashem Sefasai Tiftach is hard to follow. “Just as we are not mafsik between geulah and tefillah — and Hashem Sefasai Tiftach is not considered a hefsek but rather part and parcel of Shemoneh Esrei — the same applies to Yihyu Leratzon.” A reader who does not already know that geulah must be joined to tefillah will not understand why a verse before the Amidah could be a problem. One clause of explanation is needed.

Section VII repeats Section IV. IV says that after Yihyu Leratzon it is “as if you have already taken three steps back”; VII says it again in the same words. Say it once, in IV, and let VII refer to it.

The concluding paragraph of XIV is a synthesis that the body never builds. It ties kavanah, the Hebrew name and lashon hara into one “deeper backdrop” for the close of Shemoneh Esrei; XIV itself is about reward and punishment, and its relation to the siyum is not argued. Section XIV also speaks of “a tradition — I am calling it a tradition because we do not know the exact makor,” while XI speaks of “a tradition” of the soul forgetting its name. Are these one tradition or two? As written the reader cannot tell.

The lead paragraph covers one third of the document. It names the close of Shemoneh Esrei, the nusach question and Elokai Netzor; it does not name Mar b. Ravina, the verse of one’s name, lashon hara, or the soul and its name, which are half of what follows.

4.Factual / Citation Concerns

The gematria in Section I does not compute. The text says HaShalom equals Sandalfon. The standard values give HaShalom = 5+300+30+6+40 = 381 and Sandalfon = 60+50+4+30+80+6+50 = 280. The next sentence says oseh equals HaShalom, which does hold (70+6+300+5 = 381), so the whole argument depends on the first claim being right. Another angel whose name totals 381 is Safriel (60+80+200+10+1+30), the angel usually associated with recording; check the intended name against the source.

Where is chut hameshulash in the Gemara? The body says Kesuvos; footnote 2 says the principal location is Bava Metzia 85a and treats Kesuvos as the lecturer’s error. The verse (Koheles 4:12) is quoted in the body as “chut hameshulash lo yinatek,” a shortened form of the verse as given in footnote 1. Decide the locus, quote the verse in full once, and make body and notes agree.

Footnote 3 cites three loci for the placement of Yihyu Leratzon (Berachos 4b, 9b, 31a). The eighteen-chapters argument is elsewhere in the notes tied to 9b; 31a appears again in footnote 10 for the wedding story, and footnote 8 puts the prayer of Mar b. Ravina at 16b–17a. The note also says 4b is “foundational for the placement,” which is not obviously so. Verify and narrow.

Footnote 4 sends the Rashba to Berachos 31, but the prayer of Mar b. Ravina is at 17a (footnote 8). A dispute about whether Yihyu Leratzon goes before the supplications is naturally found where the supplications are recorded. Check whether 31 is right, and whether the position is the Rashba’s own or one he reports.

Footnote 5 names an authority without a work. The Rema MiPano (“in his liturgical writings”) is credited with the Gra’s view; no title is given. The better-known Rema (R. Moshe Isserles) has a gloss on Orach Chaim 122, which may be what was meant. Footnote 6 says the Mishnah Berurah “acknowledges” the arrangement is terei desisrei; that characterization looks like the lecturer’s.

Footnote 9 contradicts the body on geography. The body says Mechoza is “near present-day Mosul”; the note says it “was a Babylonian Jewish center on the Tigris, not ‘near Mosul’” and that the detail “should be verified.” Resolve in the body. The same note quotes the Mishnah as hareoh makom shenaaseh lo nes and then the body quotes the blessing in the first person for Mar; check the actual formulas, which differ for the community and for an individual.

Footnote 10 gives zecher lechurban to Tosafos. Check whether that wording is Tosafos’ own or a later explanation of the custom (the note also cites the Rema in Even HaEzer 65). The body asserts it as Tosafos’ statement.

Footnote 17 is an unresolved working note. It says the Radak’s Michlol is a grammatical work, so the citation “should be verified”; that the second title “is a less commonly cited title; verify the printed source”; and then gives a history of the custom in “kabbalistic-influenced works.” Yet the body states the earliest reference as if it were established. Either find the source or drop the claim. Footnote 18 does the same: the body says the first appearance in a siddur is Amsterdam 1699, while the note says the data “appear to” indicate this and must be “cross-referenced against the JNUL/NLI.”

Footnote 20 is internally inconsistent. The short citation is Zohar, Parashas Haazinu, while the expanded note gives Zohar volume II, Parashas Tazria 32b. Tazria is in the Leviticus volume, not volume II. The body cites only Haazinu.

Footnote 16 gives Ephraim Zalman Margolioth dates (1762–1828) and calls him a “bibliographer” though the body calls him a “prolific author”; and says he documents interpolations “especially in Shoftim and Shmuel” where the body names only Shoftim 20. Footnotes 7, 15 and 22 also close with “verify.”

Unsourced claims in the body. The Chofetz Chaim’s remark about the Baal Shem Tov and the maskilim (III) carries no note; neither does the statement that the cherem against the Chassidim describes how they prayed; nor the Worms historian (“Yehuda Gevera” looks like a variant spelling; name and standing should be checked). The claim that “in earlier times nusach Ashkenaz said Oseh HaShalom the entire year” (I) rests wholly on Chut HaMeshulash via footnote 1; the note should say so.

5.Small Polish Suggestions

  • Hebrew density. The body runs Hebrew and English together at a rate that makes the English spine hard to follow, with bare Hebrew words mixed with spans for quoted phrases. For a sefer edition, adopt one convention: transliterated term first, Hebrew in parentheses, and nothing in Hebrew alone in running text.
  • The aynikel and similar glosses. “Aynikelach,” “Oberland yidden,” “Oiseh-niks” and “HaMevarech-niks” each want a one-time gloss (“grandchildren,” “Jews from Upper Hungary” if that is meant, “partisans of…”).
  • Header and footer. “Reworked from the original shiur” (header) and “Reworked from a shiur delivered by Rabbi Ephrati” (footer) duplicate each other, and “Hebrew terminology preserved in Hebrew script throughout” is the opposite of what a reader of the English needs. Drop the first; revise the second.
  • The blockquote chain (Chasam Sofer → Ksav Sofer → Shevet Sofer → Daas Sofer) lists the names of the books as though they were the names of the men. Give both once.
  • Section numbering. Fourteen sections plus a siyum for a document of this length is many; with the folds above (VIII into VII, XIII into XI) it is twelve.
  • Footnote anchors sometimes follow the punctuation and sometimes precede it (compare footnotes 4 and 6). Standardize.
  • The final siyum pivots in its last sentence to “may our names be called in shamayim with honor,” which echoes XI to XIII and not the shiur as a whole. If it stays, mark it as an editorial coda.

6.Bottom Line

A good core with an uneven second half. The argument about Yihyu Leratzon and Elokai Netzor, and the Mar b. Ravina passage that explains it, are the strongest sustained writing in the document. Before this goes into a sefer: (a) repair the genealogy in Section II and its note; (b) correct or verify the gematria; (c) recast the oral-delivery voice (“last time we,” “I personally,” “2025”) and drop the ethnic joke; (d) fold VIII into VII and XIII into XI, and place XII after them; (e) clear the apparatus of working notes and resolve the places where the notes contradict the body (Kesuvos/Bava Metzia; Mosul/Tigris; the Zohar locus; the Rashba’s page); (f) adopt a single convention for Hebrew in running text. Fix these and this is a strong treatment of the close of the Amidah.
