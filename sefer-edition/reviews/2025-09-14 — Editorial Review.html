Mareh Mekamos · Independent Editorial Review

14 September 2025 — מכניסי רחמים and the Question of Intermediaries

Independent review · read fresh, no exposure to the transcript, the summary or the writing process · 12 October 2026

1.Overall Read

A twelve-section write-up (roughly 1,800 words of body plus a seventeen-note Mareh Mekomos apparatus of about 1,500 words) on one sharp question: may we say Machnisei Rachamim, given that it appears to address angels? The architecture is the right one. The problem is stated in three authorities (Rambam, Yerushalmi, Ramban); the responses follow in a clear order (the Maharal and the Chasam Sofer, the Maharit Asad, the Litvish abstention); a natural objection is raised (what of going to a tzaddik?); and three answers close the argument. The pivot in Section IX, from “may we ask angels?” to “why may we ask a sage?”, is the best moment in the piece. The Chasam Sofer falling into nefilas apayim while the congregation sings is a memorable image.

The hard editorial issues are six: (a) note 5 contradicts the body it supports: the body says the Gemara in Shabbos 12b “forbids asking malachim”, while the passage the note quotes concerns praying in Aramaic and forbids nothing; (b) the Maharal’s change is called a “command” in the body and a “declaration” in note 8, and the grammar (a third-person form) does not obviously command anyone; (c) the opening hook, Sim Shalom and the angel Safriel, is dropped and never returns; (d) the piyyut itself is never quoted or described, so a reader unfamiliar with the text cannot see why it looks like a request to angels; (e) five block quotations of oral speech are set as if they were located texts (the Ramban, the Chasam Sofer, Rav Moshe’s father, R. Yaakov bar Yakar, the Mahari Bruna) with no Hebrew and no source; and (f) live-shiur residue (“Last night”, “we noted”, “Reworked 3 May 2026”, “Special Selichos-Season Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati”) that will date badly in a sefer.

2.What Works

  • The problem is stated cleanly. Sections III–V take three authorities, each in a short paragraph with a clear claim, in ascending order of directness (principle, Talmudic metaphor, explicit call for removal). A reader meets the difficulty at full strength before any solution.
  • The king-of-flesh-and-blood image (IV). “Every single one of us has direct access” is the portable core of the whole sugya, and the sentence “This appears to negate Machnisei Rachamim outright” is a good handoff.
  • The Chasam Sofer’s practice (VI). Falling into nefilas apayim and finishing his tachanun exactly as the congregation finishes, so no one notices, is the most vivid and most instructive detail in the document: it shows how to hold a private view without imposing it. Its pairing with the Maharit Asad (VII) carries the practical lesson of the piece.
  • The three answers (X) are well sequenced. The first dissolves the question (the phrase never meant angels); the second distinguishes the cases (no ta’us with a tzaddik); the third reframes the act (humility, not petition). Each answer is a different kind of move, and Section XI’s synthesis says so.
  • The Litvish section (VIII). The detail that Rav Moshe Feinstein’s father was a misnaged serving as rav of the chassidim is an arresting piece of social history, and it is used economically.
  • Note discipline. The notes are honest about what they could not pin (“verify exact siman”), and the references are numbered 1–17 in strict body order with matching back-links, which is clean.
  • The closing returns to the Yamim Noraim and to “direct connection” with the Ribbono shel Olam, echoing Section IV without overreach.

3.Where It’s Weak or Could Be Sharper

The piyyut is never quoted. The title and every section assume the reader knows what Machnisei Rachamim says, when it is said, and why it reads as a request. A two-line quotation of the opening (the Hebrew and a translation), with one sentence on when it is recited, would let the reader see the difficulty for himself. Section II (“Machnisei Rachamim Itself”) promises exactly this and delivers a single short paragraph about the previous night’s Selichos instead.

The Safriel hook is abandoned. Section I opens with the old Ashkenazi ending “oseh ha-shalom” and the gematria that supposedly invokes the angel Safriel, and uses it to pose the question. After Section I neither Safriel nor Oseh HaShalom returns, and no answer is checked against that example. Either resolve it in Section XI (does any of the three answers cover oseh ha-shalom?) or cut the opening to a single sentence. A reader will otherwise assume the example was forgotten.

Block quotations of speech. Five passages are set as indented blockquotes: the Ramban (“Do not say Machnisei Rachamim. Remove it from your machzor…”), the Chasam Sofer (“I did not want to change the nusach…”), Rav Moshe’s father, R. Yaakov bar Yakar and the Mahari Bruna; the Maharit Asad is quoted in the same manner inline. They are in modern colloquial English, with no Hebrew original, and in the first person. A sefer reader will take them for located quotations. Either supply the Hebrew and the source, or recast as reported speech (“the Ramban urges that it be removed from the machzor”), and say once, in a note, that the wording is a paraphrase.

Request versus command versus declaration. Section VI says the Maharal’s one-letter change (הַכְנִיסוּ → יַכְנִיסוּ) turns a request into a command to the angels. Note 8 calls it “third-person imperfect (‘they shall bring in’)” and “a declaration of how Hashem uses these angels.” A third-person future is not a command to the persons addressed, and a “declaration” is a third thing again. The text must choose, and explain why the change removes the problem (presumably because the sentence is no longer an address to the angels at all).

Note 7 and the body disagree. The body (VI) says the Chasam Sofer “brings the Maharal’s answer” and that his personal practice was to continue his tachanun while the congregation sang; the note says he “cites the Ramban and Maharal” and “records his own personal practice of replacing the piyyut with extended tachanun.” Whether he cites the Ramban matters, because Section V builds the problem on the Ramban. Reconcile.

The “halachic distinction” (Answer 2) is the weakest answer and is mislabelled. It is not a halachic distinction but a rationale (the risk of mistaken belief). It opens with “tzaddikim in death are called alive”, which at best explains why one might address a deceased tzaddik and says nothing about why a living one is different from an angel; and the actual reasoning (no ta’us) is stated in one paragraph with no source. “Some Rishonim” are not named. Retitle it, tie the two halves together, and say who holds it.

Section II is too thin to stand alone. A single paragraph of three sentences. Fold it into Section I (or into a new opening that quotes the piyyut).

Section VIII asserts without sources. “Because of this sugya, the Gra and Reb Chaim of Volozhin did not say Barchuni LeShalom on Friday night” is stated as fact with no note, and the parenthetical on Rav Dovid Feinstein (“common in those days”) generalizes from one case. Note 12 describes the father as “identified with the Litvish stringent line that included the Gra and Reb Chaim Volozhiner”, which is an inference stated as fact.

The lead is a single sentence of 114 words with nine inline Hebrew terms. For a sefer-edition opener break it into two or three sentences, or three short paragraphs: (a) the question; (b) the authorities; (c) the three answers.

A footnote reference sits inside a heading (Section VI, the Maharal subhead carries [8]) and another inside a blockquote ([9]). Move both into running text so they survive a table of contents and a re-flow.

4.Factual / Citation Concerns

Note 2 (Rambam). The note places the sin of דור אנוש at Hilchos Avodah Zarah 2:1–2; the generation of Enosh is, as far as I know, the opening halachah of the first chapter. It also glosses Yesodei HaTorah 1:6 as the mitzvah of knowing God “as One”, where the verse base for knowing His existence and the prohibition of another god is the usual content of that halachah. Neither note gives the line that the body actually relies on (“only to Him is it appropriate to pray”). Please check all three loci.

Note 5 (Shabbos 12b). The text quoted (a statement of R. Yochanan that the ministering angels do not recognize Aramaic) cannot support a prohibition of asking angels, and the note’s own sentence concedes that it “assumes some role for malachim in carrying tefillos.” Either the body is mis-cited, or the note belongs elsewhere. See Section 3.

Note 4 (Toras Hashem Temimah). Treated as the locus classicus for the Ramban’s position, with a structure (“around Tehillim 19”) and an edition (Chavel) but no page. The claim in Section V that it “runs four to five hours when read aloud” has no support in the notes. Note 6 says the Ramban “explicitly criticizes the piyyut” and “calls for its removal”; if that is documented, cite it; if it is reported, say so.

Note 9 (Rav Amram Gaon). The heading calls the Seder Rav Amram “earliest extant Ashkenazi-style seder tefillos”; it is a Babylonian geonic work of the 9th century, and the “Ashkenazi-style” label is anachronistic. The note then says Machnisei Rachamim “appears in early printed editions and in the manuscript tradition”, which does not establish that it is in Rav Amram’s own seder, the very claim the Chasam Sofer blockquote relies on.

Note 11 (Maharit Asad). “Doing so is itself a chillul Hashem against the holiness of minhag Yisrael” is much stronger than the body (“unacceptable”) and has no source. Note 10 gives his dates and seat, and says the teshuvah is in Orach Chaim without a siman.

Note 13 (Taanis 23b). “חוני המעגל and the request to chachamim for tefillah on behalf of others (Taanis 23)”: as far as I know, the Choni stories of the sleeper and the rain circle are not on 23b; and neither is a case of going to a sage for a sick person. Cite the passage you mean, or drop it and keep Bava Basra 116a, which is on point.

Note 14 (the Nimukei Yosef). The note’s own hedge undercuts the body: the body states, with a date (“the late 1300s”), that R. Yosef ibn Chaviv records the French minhag; the note admits it may be a different Rishon and gives different dates (“late 14th to mid-15th century”, “late 15th century”). Resolve the identity before print and use one date range.

Note 15 (R. Yaakov bar Yakar). The heading says “10th–11th century” while the text gives a death date of 1064 (11th century); it names Worms as his seat (the usual association is with Mainz; please verify); and it says Rashi “cites him repeatedly as mori ha-zaken” and that his commentary is quoted in Siddur Rashi and Machzor Vitri, with no references. The central claim, that he read machnisei as tzaddikim, is placed “in the manuscript tradition” with no location.

Note 16 (Berachos 18b). The principle that tzaddikim are called living is accurate, but the note claims it distinguishes requests to tzaddikim from requests to angels; the passage itself says nothing about prayer. Mark the application as the lecturer’s.

Note 17 (Mahari Bruna). “A talmid of the Terumas HaDeshen” is a biographical claim that should be checked; the passage containing the shiflus reading has no siman. The Hebrew word “בתחפיסה” in Answer 3 is not an identifiable word and is almost certainly a mis-transcription; resolve it from the audio.

Dates and numbers that are right and need no change: the Chasam Sofer (1762–1839), the Maharal (c. 1520–1609), Rav Moshe Feinstein (1895–1986), the gematria (381), and the Hebrew year of the Selichos (תשפ"ו, the year ahead) are consistent.

5.Small Polish Suggestions

  • Hebrew density. Almost every noun is a Hebrew inline span, with no transliteration, so the English spine is hard to read aloud and impossible for a reader without Hebrew. Adopt the series convention: transliteration first, Hebrew in parentheses on first use, then one form.
  • Naming. “Reb Hershi”, “Rav Dovid Feinstein”, “Reb Chaim of Volozhin”, “Rav Moshe Feinstein” and “רב חיים מוולוז'ין” mix Reb and Rav; standardize. “יהודים” and “Jews” also alternate.
  • “Last night” and the header. Replace with the date; delete “Reworked 3 May 2026” and the “Special Selichos-season shiur” kicker, or move to a colophon.
  • Rav Dovid Feinstein. The parenthesis (“interestingly, Rav Dovid was a misnaged… common in those days”) interrupts the sentence; move to its own sentence and drop “common”.
  • The footer repeats the lead almost word for word; reduce to one line.
  • Terms of art. Kefirah, ta’us, shiflus and nefilas apayim are used without gloss; one-time glosses would serve a general reader.
  • The conclusion (XI) restates all positions and ends with what “does not stand”; it never says what a worshipper should do tonight. Consider a final, practical sentence in the voice of the sources (“the minhag stands; the public denunciation does not”).
  • Transliteration drift. “Machnisei” in the title but “מכניסי” in nearly every sentence; pick one for running text.

6.Bottom Line

A clear, well-ordered treatment of a delicate question, with a strong spine (the problem, the authorities, the three answers) and two memorable details (the king who needs no courtiers; the Chasam Sofer silently lengthening his tachanun). It needs five fixes before print: quote the piyyut and cut or resolve the Safriel opening; reconcile note 5 with the body, note 7 with Section VI, and note 8 with the word “command”; recast the block quotations as reported speech or supply their sources; resolve the unsourced or hedged identifications (the Nimukei Yosef author, R. Yaakov bar Yakar’s seat, the Mahari Bruna’s teacher, the Choni passage, the Enosh halachah); and clean the live-shiur residue and the unintelligible Hebrew word in Answer 3. With these, it can stand as a short, sharp chapter in the tefillah series.
