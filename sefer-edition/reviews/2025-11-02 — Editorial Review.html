2 November 2025 — שיעור על תחנון: the Confession, the Fall on the Face, and the Argument over Skipping

Independent review · read fresh, no exposure to the transcript, the summary or the writing process · 18 October 2026

1.Overall Read

A thirteen-section write-up (about 4,300 words of body plus an eighteen-note Mareh Mekomos apparatus) on tachanun: why a vidui precedes it, where nefilas apayim comes from, the long narrative of R. Eliezer and Imma Shalom, and the Chassidic custom of skipping tachanun, ending with Rav Moshe Feinstein and the Chazon Ish on how to behave when others skip. The architecture is sound and the storytelling is strong: the Rambam and the sage who would not confess, the oven of Achnai, Imma Shalom at the door, the Radoshitzer’s 5,000 tachanunim and the Chazon Ish saying tachanun alone and then thanking the baal tefillah are the kind of passages a reader remembers. The last story gives the whole piece its moral.

The hard editorial issues are seven: (a) the body and the notes disagree on who fell on their faces in Korach and on whether the Tur mentions the vidui; (b) several arithmetic and calendar statements cannot be right as written (“the thirtieth day, not the thirty-first”; 70–80 tachanunim a day for five weeks reaching 5,000; “three or four hundred years ago” for the Arizal); (c) the chronology of the oven episode is confused (Rabbi Yochanan ben Zakkai is made to act, then R. Gamliel is said to have succeeded him); (d) the argument that nefilas apayim is a reshus does not follow from the story as told; (e) garbled or unclear Hebrew and Yiddish terms (נורא עלימה מלך, מלמדי זלמן, קבלת עולים, יורד, the visitor’s retort); (f) unresolved “verify” flags in five notes; and (g) live-shiur residue (“Mrs. Weissman wrote me this morning,” “I grew up in Bnei Brak,” “I am the biggest advocate…”, the dedication callout) that will date badly in a sefer.

2.What Works

  • The opening question. To whom is ואמרו אמן said, at the end of עושה שלום? is a clean hook, and the answer (the angels, with an imagined resounding amen) is memorable. It is a pity that it is not tied to what follows.
  • The Zohar and the mekatrigim (II). The picture of the accusers pouncing the moment the prayer ends, and the worshipper “stealing their words” so that the accuser stands in the corner with nothing to say, is the most vivid explanation of why a vidui stands before nefilas apayim.
  • Collective responsibility, then the Rambam story (III). The move from “I did not steal” to “we are one body” and then to the sage who refused to confess what he had not done is well sequenced, and the Rambam’s turn (anger is idolatry; you must also confess your refusal to confess) lands.
  • Imma Shalom (VI). The narrative is paced well: the oven, the signs, the voice from heaven, the ban, the sister, the daily distraction, the day she erred, the shofar. Setting out Imma Shalom’s predicament (a brother who is the nasi, a husband who is the greatest of the generation) gives the reader a reason to care.
  • The moral framing (VII). “We want to be like Imma Shalom, not like the rest of the world” is the right caution after a story about a prayer that kills.
  • The practical turn (XII–XIII). Rav Moshe’s “don’t make a fight” followed by the Chazon Ish’s silent practice is a strong pairing, and the closing sentence (“say it quietly, on your own”) is the best line in the document.
  • Note discipline. The references are numbered 1–18 in body order with matching back-links, and several notes (fn 3, 11, 14, 15, 18) are honest that they have not found the exact place.

3.Where It’s Weak or Could Be Sharper

The opening question is orphaned. Section I asks to whom ואמרו אמן is addressed and answers in one paragraph, mentions that “we discussed the three steps last week,” and then never returns to it. A reader without last week’s shiur does not know what the three steps are. Either state them in a clause or drop the reference, and either tie the question to tachanun (the prayer is over; what comes next?) or present it explicitly as a preface.

Body and notes disagree on Korach. Section V says that in the story of קרח kal Yisrael “fell on their faces” and says א-ל אלוקי הרוחות; note 7 says that משה and אהרן fall and speak. As far as I know the verse (Bamidbar 16:22) has Moshe and Aharon fall. The body should follow the text, or say that the lecturer read it so.

The Tur is both cited and not cited. Section II says “the טור and שלחן ערוך do not even mention this systematically,” and in the next sentence “the טור notes that some קהילות had the מנהג”; note 2 says the same of the Tur in OC 131 and adds a reference to “למנצח/ארץ ישראל distinctions” that is not explained anywhere. The text must decide whether the Tur mentions it, and where.

“Three or four hundred years ago.” The Arizal (האריז״ל) died in 1572 (as far as I know), so “not more than three or four hundred years ago” is too short; note 2 says “17th century.” Give the Arizal’s dates and say when the custom spread.

“Thirtieth day, not the thirty-first.” A Hebrew month has at most thirty days, so there is no “thirty-first.” As far as I know the Gemara says that she confused a full month with a deficient one, so that she took the day for Rosh Chodesh when it was not. Restate the error in those terms.

The chronology of the oven episode. Section VI opens with R. Yochanan ben Zakkai “running Klal Yisrael” and needing to make a public statement that the authority of Chazal was intact, and then tells the dispute over the oven as that statement. A few paragraphs later it says that R. Gamliel “had become the נשיא after R. Yochanan ben Zakkai,” and it is R. Gamliel who leads the ban. The two cannot both be the setting. Either say the dispute took place in R. Gamliel’s time, as the lecturer’s illustration of the same principle, or drop R. Yochanan ben Zakkai from the narrative.

The oven. The text says it was “broken down and reconnected with cement.” As far as I know the Mishnah (Kelim 5:10) has the oven cut into rings with sand between them. The reasoning attributed to R. Eliezer (“once broken down, the act of reconnecting does not produce a new כלי”) should be marked as the lecturer’s explanation.

R. Akiva. “R. Akiva, who was a תלמיד of R. Eliezer, joined with the חכמים” undersells what I recall from the Gemara, where R. Akiva is the one who goes to tell his teacher. Check.

Reshus, not chiyuv. The argument in Section VIII is that, were nefilas apayim an obligation, R. Eliezer “would never have listened to his wife.” But in the story he does not listen to her: she distracts him, and the one day she fails to do so he falls. The inference is that he did not insist on saying it, not that he obeyed her. Rewrite so that the reader can follow the logic, and say who draws the inference (the ראשונים, unnamed).

The Chofetz Chaim digression. The paragraph on when not to listen to a wife is in the first person (“I am the biggest advocate of listening to your wife”), is unsourced (note 11 says “verify”), and its supports (Rashi on “שמע בקולה” and Bava Metzia 59a) do not establish the principle: the former concerns Sarah’s gift of prophecy, I believe, and the latter the value of a wife’s counsel. Recast as reported, in the third person, with an honest note.

The Beis Yosef proof. “The בית יוסף brings a ראיה from this גמרא… The אחרונים ask exactly what proof — perhaps it could equally have been after מנחה — but משמע from the גמרא that her interruption was after שחרית” is compressed to the point of unintelligibility. What proof, for what point, and why would Mincha be relevant? A sentence of explanation is needed.

The visitor’s retort and the “יורד.” In Section X the man replies that תחנון is a foreign word, followed by “ברוך שם כבוד מלכותו לעולם ועד — חס ושלום from תחנון!”, which is not a sentence. And a “יורד” ordinarily means an emigrant from Israel; here a man who has lost his wealth is meant (יורד מנכסיו). Fix both.

Arithmetic. The man says תחנון seventy or eighty times a day for five weeks “until he completed the five thousand.” Thirty-five days at seventy to eighty is roughly 2,450 to 2,800, not 5,000. Either the figures are off or the number of weeks; the text should say which is the storyteller’s.

The Sanzer’s יארצייט. “כ״א אדר, the יארצייט of the נורא עלימה מלך” is not a name. Note 14 offers the Noam Elimelech (R. Elimelech of Lizhensk); if that is what is meant, say so in the body.

The fast on a יארצייט. “The Litvaks do; אשכנזים who follow the שלחן ערוך maintain it” sets Litvaks against Ashkenazim, which they are not. And “The מלמדי זלמן would fast on a יארצייט; his grandson, the הייליגע רב זלמן מלצר” has no antecedent for “his.” Rewrite or remove.

“קבלת עולים.” In Section XII, תחנון comes before אשרי and לציון “because of קבלת עולים, which we will learn as we continue.” I do not recognize the term and the promised explanation is not in the document. Either explain or drop it.

Section XI is a list, with a source that does not fit. The paragraph that gives the exemptions ends with the Beis Yosef on the dispute between Brisk and Vilna on a bris and Mincha; the same attribution appears in note 12, and note 16 says it is “recorded in the פוסקים ad loc.” Brisk and Vilna are not, as far as I know, named in the Beis Yosef. And the list in note 16 (Tu B’Av, Tu BiShvat, Shushan Purim, 14–15 Adar I, Erev Rosh Hashanah) goes beyond the body without saying so.

The callout on ר’ דוד. It makes extraordinary claims (that Neil Armstrong would not have survived; that a company of forty thousand began to hire שומרי שבת) with no source other than a message the lecturer received, and records a heter to hear the Megillah by telephone. A sefer should present this as a dedication in the lecturer’s voice (“as reported to him by the family”), not as established fact, and should keep the caution (“do not learn from that heter”) with it.

The conclusion (סיכום) restates the thesis in the editor’s voice (“the very greatest of our גדולים”) and is weaker than the Chazon Ish ending it follows. Consider ending on the Chazon Ish and putting the summary first.

4.Factual / Citation Concerns

Note 1 (Zohar). The loci are “III, Parshas Balak 222a” and I 58b. I believe Balak ends well before 222a in the standard pagination, and I 58b would fall in Noach; neither is plausible as the place for the accusers or for the shortening of days. The same note cites “Shaar HaKavanos, Drushei Nefilas Apayim.” Check all three.

Note 2 (Tur and Shulchan Aruch 131–134). See above. The notes on the “למנצח/ארץ ישראל distinctions” and “the 17th century” are unsupported.

Note 3 (Chida). The story is attributed to the Chida “in his commentary tradition” with a list of three possible works. The dates 1724–1806 are right. Resolve the work before print.

Note 4 (Shabbos 105b). The quotation “אמר ר’ אבין: כל הכועס — כאילו עובד עבודה זרה” does not match my recollection of Shabbos 105b, where R. Avin interprets Tehillim 81:10 (the strange god within a man is the evil inclination) and a baraisa speaks of one who tears garments in anger. The exact wording of “anyone who becomes angry is as if he worships idols” is, as far as I know, in the Rambam (Hilchos De’os 2). The note then says Chazal read the verse as “the foreign god within the angry person himself”; and a Zohar I 27b is cited with no content. Check.

Note 5 (Yesod VeShoresh HaAvodah). The dates 1730–1794 for R. Alexander Ziskind should be checked; I recall a later birth year. The note says the midrash appears “in his treatment of nefilas apayim” without a place. Nechemiah 9:33 is a correct reference for the verse.

Note 6 (Devarim 9:18, 9:25). Both verses are the retelling of the same forty days after the calf; “other crisis moments” is vague.

Note 8 (Bava Metzia 59b). A good summary, but it should say what is in the Gemara and what is the lecturer’s addition (R. Yochanan ben Zakkai; the cement; the calendar error; the sages’ apology).

Note 9 (Devarim 30:12; Shemos 23:2). The note attributes the reading by אחרי רבים להטות to R. Yehoshua; in the Gemara, as I recall, it is R. Yirmeya who explains it.

Note 10 (Ritva). It gives the Ritva’s dates (“c. 1260–1330”) and says the Beis Yosef cites him; the body attributes the reshus to the Rishonim, the note to the Ritva. Reconcile.

Note 11 (Chofetz Chaim). See above.

Notes 12 and 16 (Beis Yosef 131). Note 16 begins “Same סימן as fn 12 above”; the two should be merged or distinguished. See above on Brisk and Vilna.

Note 13 (Minchas Elazar). R. Chaim Elazar Shapira of Munkatch (1868–1937) is right as far as I know. “One of the most quoted פוסקים in Hungarian and חסידי circles” is an unsupported superlative. No siman is given for the critique.

Note 14 (Divrei Chaim). R. Chaim Halberstam of Sanz (1797–1876) is right. The statement that the Satmar Rebbe followed Sanz in his rulings is the lecturer’s.

Note 15 (Radoshitzer). R. Yissachar Ber (1765–1843) and the Chozeh of Lublin are right as far as I know, but the identification of the Radoshitzer of the story is the editor’s; and R. Shlomo Zevin’s Sippurei Chasidim is offered as a place to look, not as a source.

Note 17 (Igros Moshe). No volume or siman; a paraphrase set in quotation marks in the body (“I do not know from these things. Don’t make a fight. Don’t start screaming”) reads as a located quotation. Recast as reported speech.

Note 18 (Chazon Ish). The dates 1878–1953 are right. The story is from oral tradition; the suggestion of Pe’er HaDor and Maaseh Ish is not a citation.

Dates and numbers that are right and need no change: the Chida (1724–1806), the Sanzer (1797–1876), the Radoshitzer (1765–1843), Rav Moshe Feinstein (1895–1986), the Chazon Ish (1878–1953), the Minchas Elazar (1868–1937), as far as I know.

5.Small Polish Suggestions

  • Hebrew density. Almost every noun is a Hebrew inline span, with Yiddish (הייליגע, שטיקל, ברענפן, לאכיש) and English in one sentence. Adopt the series convention: transliteration first, Hebrew in parentheses on first use, then one form.
  • Live cues. “Today’s שיעור,” “wrote me this morning,” “Before we begin,” “Let me share a powerful story,” “I grew up in בני ברק,” “as we continue.” Move to the third person or delete.
  • Spelling and naming. “Reb Meyer,” “ר’ זריאל דוד,” “רבצין,” “רבצין / Rebbetzin” and “Rav Moshe” vs. “ר’ משה” alternate; אריז״ל is spelled with three different punctuation styles.
  • Quotation marks on oral speech. The Rambam, the visitor to the Radoshitzer, Rav Moshe, and the sage are in quotation marks and blockquotes in modern colloquial English; mark as reported.
  • Terms of art (mekatrigim, nefilas apayim, hilula, baal tefillah) are used without gloss on first occurrence.
  • The header reads “Sunday Morning שיעור on תפילה” and the footer repeats “Hebrew terminology preserved in Hebrew script”; delete both production lines.
  • Humour. “Lived happily ever after” (X) and “the Sanzer would take out a stick” (IX) are in the lecturer’s voice; in print, keep the point and tone it down.

6.Bottom Line

A strong narrative piece whose best parts (the accusers who are left with nothing to say, Imma Shalom, the Chazon Ish) carry it. It needs seven fixes before print: reconcile the body with the notes on Korach and the Tur; repair the calendar, arithmetic and chronology (“thirty-first,” five weeks and 5,000, R. Yochanan ben Zakkai and R. Gamliel, the Arizal’s dates); explain the reshus argument; replace the unclear terms (נורא עלימה מלך, מלמדי זלמן, קבלת עולים, יורד); resolve or honestly hedge the five “verify” notes and the doubtful loci (Shabbos 105b, the Zohar, the Beis Yosef); recast oral speech as reported; and strip the live-shiur residue and the family dedication callout to a short dedication. With these, it can stand as a chapter in the tefillah series.
