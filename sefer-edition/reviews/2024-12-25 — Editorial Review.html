Mareh Mekamos · Independent Editorial Review

25 December 2024 — כֵּיצַד נַעֲשִׂיוּ הַחַשְׁמוֹנָאים לִמְלָכִים

Independent review · read fresh, no exposure to the writing process · 23 August 2026

1.Overall Read

This is a strong Chanukah shiur that carries a genuinely important kushya — how Kohanim came to sit on a throne reserved for Beis David — through a substantial historical arc and lands on a real machloikes between the Ramban and Rav Yaakov Kamenetsky. The pacing is well-judged: the historical setup (Alexander through Antiochus) is long enough to give the reader real orientation, and the halachic payoff (Sections XIII–XV) actually delivers on the question posed at the start. The dual-layer mareh mekomos apparatus continues to be the format's most impressive feature. At roughly 4,500 words plus twenty-one footnotes it is on the long side, but for a piece that has to carry a reader through Greek history, priestly civil war, and a Ramban/Rambam dispute in one sitting, the length is earned.

The piece is strongest in Sections XIII–XV, where the Ramban/Rav Yaakov confrontation is set out cleanly and the tzivui-vs.-havtachah framing is genuinely illuminating. It is weakest in the middle historical stretch (Sections V–VII), where several factual dates and identifications drift, and in Section XVI, where the Chasam Sofer / Vilna Gaon / Sefas Emes triptych is compressed to the point of feeling appended rather than integrated. A cluster of dating slips (Alexander's chronology, the "110–115 years" figure) and one garbled identification (the Tzedukim as "students of the early Tana'im") need correction. The closing (Section XVII) is a single line and is the weakest structural element in the document.

2.What Works

  • The Ramban / Rav Yaakov confrontation (Sections XIII–XV) is the intellectual heart of the piece and it lands cleanly. The move from the Ramban's stark reading (it was a sin; Hordus was the punishment) to Rav Yaakov's structural reading (Bayis Sheni was never meant to be the full geulah; the Sanhedrin's silence is the tell) is exactly the shape a sefer-quality treatment should take: two gedolim reading the same historical fact through opposite yesodos. The yishuv Eretz Yisrael parallel in Section XV as a second instance of the same tzivui/havtachah axis is a first-rate structural observation and should be preserved in the sefer edition exactly as written.
  • The Yochanan Kohen Gadol / Mattisyahu irony (Section IX) is beautifully handled. Framing Mattisyahu's heroism against the fact that his father was the Tzeduki of the Berachos 29a sugya gives the rebellion a genealogical drama that reads as more than pageant. The Al ta'amin b'atzmecha hook is used with restraint.
  • The Rav Yaakov answer for why Bayis Sheni is the tekufah of gezeiros and takanos (fortification for the coming galus) is a genuine yesod that reframes the entire era. This should be flagged in a callout box or subheading in the sefer edition — it is easy to lose in the flow.
  • The Rabbi's stubborn pshat on Maccabi (Mattisyahu Ben Yochanan Kohen, not the acronym on the flag) is exactly the kind of small, contrarian textual note that gives the shiur its personality. The parenthetical (the questioner pushing back; the Rabbi holding his ground) should be kept in the sefer edition, not smoothed away.
  • The Sefas Emes's reading of the missing Maseches Chanukah — that Chanukah is the essence of Torah she'b'al peh, and so must remain unwritten — is the strongest single line in the closing section. It rescues Section XVI from feeling like a grab-bag.

3.Where It's Weak or Could Be Sharper

Section XVII ("Closing") is one paragraph and a greeting. After a document that has traveled from Yaakov Avinu's blessing through Alexander, Antiochus, Mattisyahu, Hordus, the Ramban, and Rav Yaakov, the reader deserves a closing that returns to the opening kushya and states what the reader now knows that they did not know at the start. The current ending ("more next week, freilche Chanukah") is a shiur-room signoff, not a written-document closing. A three-paragraph coda that (a) restates the two answers, (b) names the axis between them, and (c) draws one mussar-lesson from the Chashmonaim's fate would complete the piece.

The two Rambam citations in Sections XII and XV read as if from different documents. In XII the Rambam is invoked as the source who describes the Chashmonaim as Kohanim "who have no business being kings" — a phrasing that actually belongs to the Ramban's critical voice. In XV the same Rambam is (correctly) shown praising them. A reader who does not already know the Rambam's position will experience this as contradiction. Section XII needs one sentence marking the framing as the kushya-voice, not the Rambam's own; Section XV then reads as resolution rather than reversal.

Section XVI (why no Maseches Chanukah) is compressed to the point of feeling tacked on. Three separate traditions (Chasam Sofer via Chut Ha'Meshulash; Vilna Gaon on the lost masechtos; Sefas Emes on Torah she'b'al peh) each deserve their own beat. As currently written, the reader is rushed through them without being told why they belong in this shiur rather than in a general Chanukah collection. A one-sentence bridge at the head of the section — "If the Chashmonaim's malchus was itself contested, one might ask why the yom tov they gave us has no maseches at all" — would tie it back to the main yesod.

The Sefarim Chitzonim / Megillas Antiochus stretch (Section VI) is a digression that could be shortened. The bibliographic note on Maccabim I & II — including the aside about Protestants removing them from their canon — is charming in a shiur setting but reads as sourcing housekeeping in a written document. Move most of it to the footnote apparatus (fn7 and fn8 already carry the weight) and keep the body text focused on the historical narrative it is serving.

4.Factual / Citation Concerns

Alexander's dates and age at accession are off. The document places Alexander's rise at "approximately 320 BCE" and puts him "at seventeen or eighteen" when he ascended the throne. In fact Alexander became king in 336 BCE at the age of twenty, and his conquest of Eretz Yisrael is conventionally placed at 332 BCE. He died in 323 BCE. The "320 BCE" number is post-Alexander (roughly the beginning of the Diadochi wars) and does not fit "an individual rises to power." Corrected phrasing: "approximately 336–332 BCE" for the rise and the conquest of the region; "at the age of twenty" for the accession.

"110–115 years after Alexander" of peaceful coexistence does not work arithmetically. Alexander's conquest of the region: c. 332 BCE. Antiochus IV's persecution: c. 168–167 BCE. That is roughly 165 years, not 110–115. If the Rabbi meant "from the death of Alexander (323 BCE) to the accession of Antiochus IV (175 BCE)," that is ~148 years. Either figure should be substituted; 110–115 does not correspond to any standard reckoning of the interval.

The identification of Tzedukim as "students of the early Tana'im" (Section III) is garbled. The classical mesorah (Avos d'Rabbi Nasan 5:2) is that Tzaddok and Baisus were talmidim of Antignos Ish Socho — one specific tanna, not "the early Tana'im" generally — and that they (or their followers) misread his teaching on avdus mei'ahavah and broke away. As currently written the sentence reads as though the Tzedukim were a legitimate scholastic line descending from the Tana'im, which inverts the historical relationship. Rewrite: "talmidim of Antignos Ish Socho whose followers broke from the mesorah."

Menelaus as "Jason's brother, also a Kohen" (Section VII) is not the standard historical reading. In 2 Maccabees (4:23–29), Menelaus is the brother of Simon (a Benjaminite temple official), not of Jason, and his own priestly credentials are disputed — several sources treat him as not from the legitimate Kehunah line at all. Josephus differs and does make him Jason's brother. This is a place where the sources genuinely disagree and the document should acknowledge it rather than assert one reading flat. A brief hedge ("According to Josephus, Menelaus was Jason's brother; 2 Maccabees identifies him differently") preserves accuracy without derailing the narrative.

Antiochus IV's death by shipboard flight (Section X, "Antiochus HaBoreach") is the Megillas Antiochus tradition, not the historical consensus. The document flags this parenthetically ("secular historians say he died of disease") — good. But the standard record (Polybius; 1 Maccabees 6; 2 Maccabees 9) places his death on the Persian campaign in Elam, not by ship. The flag should be strengthened: "The mesorah in Megillas Antiochus gives an account at variance with the ancient historians, who record his death from illness on the Persian campaign."

"Rav Saadia Gaon in his Geon Eikvah" (Section VI, fn8) is a title I cannot identify. Rav Saadia's known works include Emunos v'Deos, the Siddur, the Tafsir, and various peirushim. Geon Eikvah may be a mishearing (of "Geon Yaakov", perhaps, or of a Judeo-Arabic title). The reference should either be pinned to a specific extant work by Rav Saadia or attributed more broadly ("Rav Saadia Gaon, in the introduction to his siddur, attests...").

Fn22 is referenced in Section XVI but there is no fn21-to-fn22 bridge in the body. The footnote numbering jumps: fn21 (Sefas Emes) appears in Section XVI, then fn22 appears mid-paragraph in the Chasam Sofer subsection, then the narrative continues. Read straight through, the reader hits the fn22 marker before understanding what it is documenting. Either move the fn22 marker to a cleaner anchor point (immediately after "the Reish Galusa being in Bavel") or renumber so the sequence tracks the text order.

5.Small Polish Suggestions

  • "B'lashon the Rabbi" appears twice in Section II and reads awkwardly — the Yiddish/Hebrew fragment plus English article is an ungrammatical hybrid. Replace with "in the Rabbi's phrase" or simply "as the Rabbi put it."
  • "Tekufah moshikra" (Section XIV) is a spelling I cannot place. If the intended term is meshanas or arai (temporary), or mishnah (secondary), it should be regularized. If it is a genuine phrase from Rav Yaakov, it should carry a footnote pointing to where he uses it.
  • "Alexander Yannai (Alexander Janus)" in Section XII — the standard Latin/English is Jannaeus, not Janus (which is a different Roman deity entirely). This is a one-character fix but a visible one.
  • "L'derech ha'kavah" in Section XIV — unclear phrase. If l'derech gavo'ah ("on the elevated path") is intended, it should be spelled out; if a different phrase, it should be regularized.
  • Section VIII's "davar acher" for pig is used three times in close succession, then Section VIII closes with the number "40,000–80,000 killed" without a citation. Josephus and the Megillas Antiochus traditions give different figures; a footnote naming the source range would strengthen the paragraph.
  • The Rambam phrase in fn11 (וְגָבְרוּ בְּנֵי חַשְׁמוֹנַאי הַכֹּהֲנִים הַגְּדוֹלִים) is quoted in the footnote but not in the body. Given that this lashon is exactly the phrase Section XV turns on (the Rambam's praise: gavru, gedolim), it deserves to appear in the body text as a blockquote in Section XV. It is the strongest single piece of textual evidence for the Rabbi's reading and the document currently hides it in the apparatus.
  • The Chashmona'is-on-the-rooftop passage (Section XIII, "The Last Chashmona'is") is given as an English blockquote without the Aramaic. The Bava Basra 3b lashon (כל דאמר מבית חשמונאי קאתינא עבדא הוא) is short and quotable; adding it above the translation would match the format used elsewhere.
  • The lead paragraph identifies Rav Yaakov's position as "hora'as sha'ah" in bold at the start, which slightly over-simplifies. Rav Yaakov's actual yesod is the deeper structural one (Bayis Sheni was never meant to be the full geulah); hora'as sha'ah is one consequence. A one-word fix in the lead ("a structural reading rooted in hora'as sha'ah") would prime the reader more accurately.

6.Bottom Line

A well-structured piece with a real halachic payoff in the Ramban/Rav Yaakov confrontation and a first-rate structural insight in the tzivui/havtachah parallel to yishuv Eretz Yisrael; the middle-section date slips (Alexander's chronology, the "110–115 years" figure) and the garbled Tzedukim identification need correction, the two Rambam voices in Sections XII and XV need to be reconciled with one bridging sentence, and the one-line Section XVII closing needs to be replaced with a proper coda that returns to the opening kushya — make those fixes and this is sefer-ready.
