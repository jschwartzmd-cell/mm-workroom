4 May 2025 — אתָה קָדוֹשׁ, the כּוזִָרי, and אתָה חוֹנֵן

Independent review · read fresh, no exposure to the writing process · 7 September 2026

1.Overall Read

This is a broad, generously-scoped piece that behaves less like a shiur on one berachah and more like a mini-portrait of an entire liturgical zone: the third berachah, its nusach history, its adjacent halachos, its Yamim Nora’im variant, and the opening berachah of the bakashos that immediately follows. The organizing move is a long-form excursus on the Kuzari — author, text, historical setting, Cairo Geniza corroboration, modern adaptations — from which the piece extracts a single dialectical reading of why Atah Kadosh follows Gevuros. That reading is the piece's spine and it holds.

The strongest stretches are the compact lexical unit in Section IV on kadosh-as-muvdal (the Kadesh Barnea / Kadesh Naftali geographic observation is a genuine miniature); the Section VII payoff on the Kuzari's immanence-vs-transcendence reading of the first three berachos; and the Section X nusach-history on “lo dor va-dor,” which is a first-rate piece of source-driven historical writing that will surprise most readers. The weakest is Section XV, a modern-adaptations tail (David Nieto and Isaac Breuer) that has drifted a full six sections downstream from the Kuzari discussion and reads as an orphan. There are a handful of copy-edit issues, one factual over-reach on Ashkenazi genetics, one nested-em HTML bug in the Rav Yonah of Vizhnitz callout, and one pasuk mis-quote in the Section II block-quote that should be caught before print. The dual-layer footnote apparatus is again a highlight.

2.What Works

  • Section IV, and specifically the Kadesh Barnea / Kadesh Naftali observation. This is the piece's small jewel. The lexical claim kadosh=muvdal is anchored in a concrete geographic fact of Tanach place-names sitting on regional borders. It turns an abstract translation into something a reader can visualize. The paragraph is short, unadorned, and lands.
  • Section VII, the Kuzari's dialectical reading. The move from Gevuros's intimate rofei cholim / matir asurim to Atah Kadosh's corrective is presented as a real dialectic (“Yes — He is close. And He is also infinitely separate.”). This is the piece's structural payoff and it justifies the long Kuzari setup in Sections V and VI.
  • Section X, the nusach-history on “lo dor va-dor.” A genuinely surprising historical finding, presented cleanly: the Rambam's Seder Tefillah (Sephardi Rishon) preserves the “lo dor va-dor” formulation that now sits in nusach Ashkenaz; the Orchos Chaim and Kolbo corroborate; and the change to “Atah Kadosh v'Shimcha Kadosh” is a later structural takkanah to secure three mentions of kadosh for a yachid without a Kedushah. Every step earns the next. This is sefer-quality writing.
  • The dual-layer footnote apparatus. The expanded notes on Kuzari (fn6), the Cairo Geniza and Ibn Shaprut (fn7), the Rambam's Seder Tefillah (fn9), and Nieto/Breuer (fn11) all give the sefer real scholarly texture. The academic-flagged note on Rav Avli Posweller (fn16) is exactly the right register: it names the chiddush, cites the Chayei Adam as the vehicle, gives the Mishnah Berurah's counter, and closes with the observation that the chiddush circulates as a lomdishe pre-Rosh Hashanah dvar Torah. That last sentence is the kind of framing that lets a reader know the halachic status without being told.
  • The Rav Avli Posweller section is proportioned correctly. One paragraph for the chiddush, one paragraph for the parallel to Ya'aleh ve-Yavo, one paragraph for the tarti de-sasri rejection. Clean, does not overstay.
  • The closing callout on the Rav's two grandfathers. Presented in a callout at the right moment (immediately after the “lost da’as” paragraph), lets the personal detail carry the argument without narration. This is exactly what a sefer needs from a shiur ending — a specific face to hang the bakashah on.

3.Where It's Weak or Could Be Sharper

Section XV (Modern Adaptations of the Kuzari) is a downstream orphan. It sits six sections after the main Kuzari excursus (Sections V-VI), after Atah Chonen, after the grandfathers callout. By the time the reader arrives, we have moved emotionally into the closing register; returning to David Nieto and Isaac Breuer breaks the arc. Fold this material into fn11 (already partly there) or into a paragraph at the end of Section VI, and let the piece close on the grandfathers.

Sections V and VI together run long. The Kuzari setup is genuinely interesting, but eight paragraphs of biographical, historical, and manuscript background before we hear the Kuzari's actual reading of the berachah is a lot. A reader who came for a shiur on Atah Kadosh can feel the wait. Compress by moving the Arabic title, the “דָבָר קָטָן” letter, and the Lunel correspondents-to-Rambam anecdote into fn6, which already exists.

The transition between Sections VII and VIII is not marked. Section VII ends with the Kuzari's point — He is infinitely separate; do not collapse the distance. Section VIII opens with “Beneath the Kuzari's point lies another” and pivots to the Yid's own koach for kedushah. These are in real tension — one says He is far; keep the distance, the other says we can be like Him. The piece treats the second as extending the first; it partially answers it. A one-sentence bridge acknowledging the tension (“Yet the same berachah that insists on the distance also asserts our capacity to close it”) would let the reader hold both.

The Rizhiner paragraph (in Section VIII) presents his reading of “talmud lomar” as if it were a plausible reading of Rashi. It is not; Rashi's drasha unambiguously reads “talmud lomar” in the standard midrashic sense (the pasuk teaches to say...) to shut down the “yachol kamoni” suggestion. The Rizhiner's inversion is a chassidishe re-hearing, not a philological alternative. Fn13 correctly softens this (“precise published source not given in the shiur”); the body text could pick up the same hedge with a phrase like “The Rizhiner hears — against the drasha's plain reading —...”.

The Section XI heading (Rav Avli Posweller) reads as a digression at this point in the piece. The chiddush is halachically real but the piece is not otherwise a pre-Yamim Nora’im shiur. Either add one sentence at the top of Section XI framing why it belongs here (“the same halachic architecture — three mentions of kadosh, first-unit sealing amen — carries into the seasonal substitution of ha-Melech ha-Kadosh”) or move Sections XI to a companion pre-Rosh Hashanah piece.

4.Factual / Citation Concerns

The block-quote in Section II is not the Gemara's pasuk-verbatim. The text quotes “הָבוּ לַה’ בְּנֵי אֵלִים, הָבוּ לַה’ כָּבוֹד וָעֹז.” The pasuk in Tehillim 29:1–2 actually runs “הָבוּ לַה’ בְּנֵי אֵלִים, הָבוּ לַה’ כָּבוֹד וָעֹז. הָבוּ לַה’ כְּבוֹד שְׁמוֹ.” The piece then bullets the “הָבוּ לַה’ כָּבוֹד שְׁמוֹ” segment as if it appears in the quoted pasuk, which it does not. Fix: extend the block-quote through pasuk 2b, or split the quote in two.

The “Ashkenazi Jews descend from converted Khazars” aside overstates a fringe scholarly position. Section VI, second parenthetical: “Some historians argue that a portion of Ashkenazi Jewry descends from the converted Khazars.” This is the Arthur Koestler / Shlomo Sand / Eran Elhaik thesis, which has been substantially rejected by mainstream population-genetics studies of the last fifteen years (Behar et al. 2010; Costa et al. 2013; Xue et al. 2017). Ashkenazi Jews descend principally from a Middle Eastern founder population with substantial European admixture, primarily southern-European. The Khazar-origin theory is a live cultural discussion, but the “some historians argue” framing implies more scholarly weight than it currently has. Fix: soften to “A once-popular theory held that... population-genetics research over the last two decades has substantially narrowed this claim”, or drop.

The Section VII paraphrase attributed to the Kuzari is not textually pinned. The reading (“Yes — He is close. And He is also infinitely separate.”) is presented as if it comes from a specific ma’amar in the Kuzari. No ma’amar or siman is cited in either the body or the footnotes. The Kuzari's treatment of prayer and the divine names appears principally in Ma’amar III; specific paragraphs on the third berachah would need pinning. Either add a footnote (“Cf. Kuzari III:17–19”, verified) or reframe the paragraph as “the Kuzari's broader argument implies” rather than “the Kuzari says.”

The “three letters” Cairo Geniza claim needs a small hedge. Fn7 correctly names the Khazar Correspondence and correctly credits Schechter's recovery. But the actual documentary trail is more complex: the “Schechter Letter” is a distinct Cambridge Geniza fragment (T-S Misc. 35.38), the “King Joseph Reply” and “Ibn Shaprut Letter” are separately transmitted (partly through non-Geniza sources). The body text's “three letters recovered by Schechter” compresses this; the more accurate framing is “Geniza fragments together with related manuscripts confirm the Khazar Correspondence.”

Rav Avli Posweller's dates in fn16 (c. 1764–1836). Standard sources give his death in 1836 (Sivan) in Vilna. Birth date is sometimes given as c. 1764, sometimes c. 1750. The c. hedge in the footnote is correct; worth double-checking against the Chayei Adam's own biographical note before print.

The Section IX / X siman-references in fn14. Fn14 attributes the Rama's late-arrival pesak to OC 109:1. The actual Rama on the late-arrival / amen-after-Ha-Kel-ha-Kadosh sits at OC 109:1 in most editions, but the Kriyas Shema-interruption pesak (mentioned in the same footnote as a “parallel”) belongs at OC 66:3 and needs a separate citation. Split the note.

5.Small Polish Suggestions

  • HTML bug in the Section VIII Rav Yonah of Vizhnitz callout. The paragraph opens with <em> and contains a nested <em> around “I do not understand...”. The nested <em> will render as un-italicized (browsers cancel italics inside italics). Rebalance the tags — either drop the outer <em> and use it selectively, or use a different mechanism (a callout class already provides visual weight; <em> is redundant).
  • Section II block-quote — extend to full pasuk 2 or split. See factcheck #1 above.
  • Section V heading “Author and תְּקוּפָה” uses a mixed-register Hebrew word (tekufah) in an English heading. Elsewhere the piece writes “Modern Adaptations of the כּוזִָרי” which reads more naturally. Consider “Author and Setting” or “Author and Historical Context.”
  • Section VI parenthetical about Ibn Shaprut's street. “A street in אֶרֶץ יִשְׂרָאֵל is named” is used in the shiur to establish familiarity; in the sefer version it is a room-specific joke that will confuse readers outside Eretz Yisrael. Drop or move to a footnote.
  • Section VI, “the Rebbe only learned this recently” (the Kadesh Barnea geography) is charming in the room, awkward in the sefer. Consider “A detail worth noting” or simply drop the frame.
  • Section IX heading “בְּרַכַת הַקָדוֹשׁ” should probably be “בְּרַכַת הָא-ל הַקָדוֹשׁ” for consistency with the rest of the piece, which uses “הָא-ל הַקָדוֹשׁ” as the chasima name.
  • The “Rebbe” vs “the Rav” voice. The piece uses both. In the Zohar paragraph (Section III) the speaker is “the Rebbe”; in the closing callout (Section XIV) it is “the Rebbe.” The Kuzari passages use “the Rav.” Since the shiur is by Rabbi Ephrati, and the header calls him “Rabbi Ephrati,” either “the Rav” or third-person unnamed is more consistent than the occasional “Rebbe.”
  • Section X, “לְדֹֹר וָדֹֹר” vs “לְדֹֹר וָדֹֹר”. The standard is “לְדֹֹר וָדֹֹר”; the piece is consistent, good.
  • Lead paragraph runs to a single 100+ word sentence. Would read easier as three shorter sentences or as a lead followed by a bulleted map of the sections.

6.Bottom Line

A broad, generously-scoped piece whose spine — the Kuzari's dialectical reading of why Atah Kadosh must follow Gevuros — holds; Section IV's Kadesh Barnea observation and Section X's “lo dor va-dor” nusach-history are the two strongest pieces of writing; Section XV (Nieto/Breuer modern adaptations) is a downstream orphan and should be folded into fn11 or moved earlier; Sections V and VI on the Kuzari's history run long and can be compressed by pushing detail into fn6; the Section II block-quote is missing the second half of pasuk 2; the Ashkenazi-Khazar aside overstates a fringe genetic thesis and needs softening; the Section VII “Kuzari says” paraphrase needs a pinned ma’amar or a reframing; one HTML nested-em bug in the Vizhnitz callout — fix those and this is a strong entry that will make readers put down the sefer and go daven Atah Chonen with a full heart.
