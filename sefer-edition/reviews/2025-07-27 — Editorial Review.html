Mareh Mekamos · Independent Editorial Review

27 July 2025 — תְּפִלַּת נַחֵם and מוֹדִים

Independent review · read fresh, no exposure to the writing process · 5 October 2026

1.Overall Read

A two-movement chapter in the Sunday-morning tefillah series, running to roughly 3,100 words across twelve numbered sections with a sixteen-note Mareh Mekomos (מראה מקומות) apparatus. Movement one (I–VIII) is Tefillas Nachem (תפילת נחם): the once-a-year chasimah (חתימה), the Yerushalmi (ירושלמי) source and Rav Muna’s (רב מונא) past/future rule, the Rif (רי"ף) placement in Birkas Yerushalayim, the forgotten-Nachem rules, why only at Minchah (מנחה), and the date problem of the churban (חורבן). Movement two (IX–XI) is Modim (מודים): the bow, the spine-and-snake Gemara, the double sense of modim, and the hidden prayer for children in “l’dor va-dor” (לדור ודור). The spine is sound and the hinge sentence at IX (“Returning to our regular seder”) is honest about the seam.

The structural problems are three. First, the footnote references run out of order in the body: 16, 1, 2, 4, 3, 6, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 15, 11, 12, 14, 13. For a printed sefer this has to read 1–16 in body order. Second, Section VIII (the emotional reading of the nusach) arrives after the heaviest lomdus in the chapter and reads as an afterthought; it belongs next to Section IV, where the nusach is first defended. Third, several load-bearing halachic claims in Sections V and VII carry no footnote anchor at all, so the apparatus does not cover the argument it is meant to support.

2.What Works

  • The bear-and-honey opening (I). The riddle, the mashal, and the nimshal (“Suddenly we wake up”) give the chapter a reason to exist: the once-a-year bracha as a cure for autopilot tefillah. It is the right door into the material and it is paid off in the closing section.
  • The Rav Muna rule (II). The blockquote — past in Modim, future in Retzeh — followed by the two worked examples (Al HaNisim, Yaaleh V’Yavo), is the cleanest piece of structure in the chapter. A reader can carry it out and use it.
  • The no-repetition rule (III). The Yaaleh V’Yavo/Nachem contrast through the presence or absence of Mussaf is stated in two bullets and needs nothing more.
  • The Nusach section (IV). The refusal to soften “Yerushalayim charavah” is the strongest paragraph in the chapter, and it grants the real yishuv and the real Torah in Eretz Yisrael before refusing the edit. That concession is what makes the refusal credible.
  • The spine-and-snake pivot (IX). “Why a Snake?” is the chapter’s best conceptual move: the nachash as the original kafui tov, the bow as the body’s refusal to be one. It is short and it lands.
  • The callouts (VI, XI). The post-midday chair story and the two l’dor va-dor stories are the warmest material in the piece, and the callout format sets them apart from the halachic spine so a reader scanning for halacha can skip them.
  • The lead paragraph maps both movements in one breath and names the chapter’s two or three real chiddushim.

3.Where It’s Weak or Could Be Sharper

Footnote order. Body order is 16, 1, 2, 4, 3, 6, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 15, 11, 12, 14, 13. Note 16 (the Rabbi Yonasan Eibeschutz bear story) is first in the body, notes 4/3 and 6/5 are swapped, 15 jumps in ahead of 11, and 14/13 are swapped. Renumber in body order before print and re-sequence the Mareh Mekomos list to match.

Section VIII is stranded. The reading of “Yerushalayim ka-almanah” (ירושלים כאלמנה) and “al kein Tziyon be-mar tivkeh” (על כן ציון במר תבכה) is the emotional case for why nobody may touch the nusach, which is exactly what Section IV argues. Placed after the lomdus of Section VII it reads as a coda and the reader has to re-warm to it. Move it into IV, or at minimum open it with a sentence that ties it back to IV.

The Rebbi Yochanan l’shitaso argument (VII) does not quite close. The text says: if fire is the arrow, the damage at the destination is the shooter’s act; so the primary damage on the tenth is also his act; so the fast should mark the tenth. But on an arrow model the act is the shot, and the shot was on the ninth — which is what the Sages already hold. The paragraph then ends with “We hold techilas ha-pur’anus is the primary moment; the ninth,” a clipped sentence that never says why the arrow model does not simply support the Sages. As written the reader cannot tell what Rebbi Yochanan gains from mishum chitzav. Either spell out the step or mark the connection explicitly as a suggested tzeiruf (צירוף) rather than a settled one. (The heading already calls it a tzeiruf; fn9 and fn10 then state it as fact.)

Section V (Why Only at Minchah?) has no footnote anchors. The Rosh, the Mechaber/Rama split, and the Rama’s reason are the halachic core of the section and none carries a reference number; the only cite is fn6, which sits in Section III. Anchor each of the three in place. Likewise the machlokes rishonim in II (“past or future? Retzeh or Modim?”) names no rishon and has no footnote at all.

Two stretches of internal inconsistency in VI. (a) The Gemara account says that on the seventh, eighth and ninth they had not yet set fire, then that on the ninth they ignited it — so the ninth is both before and after the fire. (b) The fire is set “after midday — toward sunset.” Midday and sunset are hours apart, and the whole halachic point (the Rama’s reason in V) depends on which one it is. Pick the precise formulation and use it in V and VI alike.

The anonymous acharon in XI. The entire section XI hangs on “One acharon: the bracha for children is hidden in l’dor va-dor.” An unnamed authority for the chapter’s most distinctive chiddush is a weak footing in a sefer. Either name him or label the point plainly as the speaker’s reading. A reader will also want the preceding step — where else might the bracha for children have been expected, and why is it not there? — and the text jumps straight to the answer.

Voice drift. The body alternates “the Rebbe,” “Rabbi Ephrati,” and the impersonal; callouts are in first-person-reported italics (“The Rebbe shares a family joke…”). Settle one voice. Also decide whether the speaker’s own back trouble (IX) and the family anecdote about his brother (VI) belong in the print edition at all; both are the sort of aside that works in the room and reads differently on a page.

The closing coda (XII) is lovely but is clearly the editor’s synthesis (“the entire arc of tefillah is the same arc: churban answered by nechamah…”), not a continuation of anything in XI. It should be marked as such, or trimmed to two sentences.

4.Factual / Citation Concerns

The Yerushalmi source and the “Rav Muna rule” (fn1). The cite is Yerushalmi Berachos 4:3, with Rav Muna described as “an amora who served as rosh yeshiva in Tzippori” and Rav Avdimi d’min Tzippori as his talmid. The biographical details have no note and sound over-specific; the names (Muna/Mana, Avdimi/Avdima) are spelled in a way that suggests transliteration drift. Also worth a check: whether the rule is given as Rav Muna’s own or cited by him in the name of an earlier amora. Send to the fact-checker.

Rav Saadia Gaon (fn2). The note says Rav Saadia wrote that despite Bavel’s silence “there is no loss in saying it,” and that the pesak “ultimately enters the Shulchan Aruch as a binding minhag.” Both halves smell: the quoted phrase is given with no siddur page, and the later Shulchan Aruch sentence is vague (the Shulchan Aruch is not a single voice here — Section V itself splits Mechaber from Rama). Verify what Rav Saadia actually says and where.

Who says forgotten-Nachem goes in Shema Koleinu (II/III, fn5). The body and the note credit “the Tur and the Taz” together. That is an unusual pairing — the Tur is a rishon and the Taz an acharon writing on the Shulchan Aruch — and the note states the Tur “rules” this. Check whether the Tur says it at all or whether it is the Taz’s own reasoning.

The date narrative (VI, fn7–fn8). Three smells. (1) VI says Nevuchadnezzar burned the Beis HaMikdash per Yirmeyahu, while the very next subsection says Nevuzaradan did; the pesukim are normally cited with Nevuzaradan. (2) The quoted phrase “אוכלין ושותין ומקרקרין” is presented as the Gemara’s language in fn8; it reads like the speaker’s paraphrase and should be checked against Taanis 29a before it is quoted. (3) fn8 says the sugya “grounds” the custom of restraint into the tenth until midday; that is a separate halacha with its own source, and the note should say where.

The bone-and-snake Tosafos (fn11–fn12). fn11 puts a quotation in the Gemara’s mouth (“מי שאינו כורע במודים, שדרתו נעשית נחש לאחר שבע שנים”) and calls it a drasha; the wording is a paraphrase and the form may be that of a baraisa. fn12 then has Tosafos propose the luz-bone reading and reject it — an unusual move for Tosafos to make on a Gemara of this kind. Confirm that the luz material belongs to the Tosafos proper and not to a later gloss or to a different commentator.

“As the rishonim and acharonim say” (IV). The statement that the third Beis HaMikdash descends in fire from shamayim is presented as the consensus. It is a well-known live machlokes (descending versus built by Mashiach), and the next paragraph’s “acharonim debate” assumes it. Soften “as the rishonim and acharonim say” to “in the view of many” or add a note naming the two sides. Note also that IV quotes “ובאש אתה עתיד לבנותו” (masculine) while VIII quotes “ובו אש אתה עתיד לבנותה” — two different texts in one chapter; settle the received nusach and use it in both.

The Mashiach-born midday kula (VI callout). The relief after midday is explained by a mesorah that Mashiach was born on Tisha B’Av, with the aside “whatever that means precisely.” No midrash is named, and the stated link to the permission to sit on a chair is strong for something this thinly sourced. Needs a source or a softer verb.

The date-specific rule (VI). “No meat or wine from Sunday night through midday Monday” is a one-year fact (the 2025 calendar) stated as a general halacha. Restate it generally (“from the night of the tenth until midday of the tenth”) or the sentence will be wrong in any other year.

Notes 15 and 16. The Shlomo Simchi note identifies a contemporary composer and a recording circuit it cannot point to (“precise recording references not specified”), and the spelling of the name may be a rendering of a different one. The Eibeschutz note attaches a bear-and-honey maaseh to him with the hedge “circulates in oral and printed chassidus-influenced sources”; the biographical dates (1690–1764) and the Yaaros Devash attribution are standard, but nothing ties the story itself to him. Both are correctly flagged. Keep the flags; do not let either harden into a stated fact in the sefer edition.

The bowing note (fn14). The Mishnah Berurah is credited with a psak that a partial bow “that signals hachna’ah” is acceptable for those with back conditions. That is a specific and practically consequential claim and needs a page-level cite before it is printed.

5.Small Polish Suggestions

  • Nested <em> tags. Several callouts and blockquotes italicize a whole paragraph and then italicize a phrase inside it, which inverts the emphasis in most renderers (the Section IV objection, the Section VIII blockquote, both Section XI callouts). Use italics for the outer paragraph only and set inner emphasis in roman, or drop the outer italics.
  • Hebrew without transliteration. The body uses Hebrew script in place of the English or transliterated term almost throughout (“an אחרונים debate,” “ספרדים follow”), which a reader without Hebrew cannot parse. Under the series’ Hebrew-inline convention the transliteration should lead and the Hebrew follow in parentheses; do this at least on first use of each term.
  • Number agreement. “An acharonim debate” (IV) should be “a debate among the acharonim” or “an acharon’s debate.” “May we be zocheh” (XII) should be plural: “zocheh” for a group is awkward.
  • “Critical detail” (III) is the only place the chapter uses a bold stage-direction label. Fold it into the sentence or use the callout style used elsewhere.
  • “A profound reading” in the lead is an adjective the text should earn in the body instead of announcing.
  • “The Hebrew word for partying” (VI) is a stage direction to the live audience; in print, give the word or drop the gloss.
  • Heading VII (“Rebbi Yochanan’s Shitta — Why Not the Tenth?”) is clear; the subheading on the tzeiruf is the right place to state the claim precisely.
  • Section II’s subheading “Our Psak — Lirushalayim Irecha” places the Rif before the supporting Avodah Zarah cite in the note order (fn4 then fn3). Swap the references so the principle is introduced before the person who applies it.
  • Dedication. The l’iluy nishmas line is well placed in the lead; keep it where it is for the print edition.

6.Bottom Line

A warm, well-spined two-movement chapter with three real peaks — the Rav Muna-and-Rif structure, the refusal to alter the nusach, and the nachash-as-kafui tov pivot, bracketed by the bear and the child on the magna-tiles. Before it ships as a sefer chapter: (a) renumber the footnotes into body order; (b) move Section VIII up beside IV; (c) anchor the Rosh/Mechaber/Rama material in Section V with footnotes; (d) repair the ninth-and-midday contradiction in VI; (e) either spell out or label as a suggestion the Rebbi Yochanan l’shitaso connection in VII; (f) clear the fact-check list — the Yerushalmi attribution (fn1), Rav Saadia (fn2), the Tur/Taz pairing (fn5), the Nevuchadnezzar/Nevuzaradan slip, the Tosafos bone-and-snake attribution (fn12), the over-strong “third Beis HaMikdash descends” consensus, and the two nusach variants of “ba-esh… li-vnosah.”
