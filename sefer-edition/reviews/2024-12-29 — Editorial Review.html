29 December 2024 — בָּרוּךְ שֵׁם כְּבוֹד — The Whispered Line

Independent review · read fresh, no exposure to the writing process · 23 August 2026

1.Overall Read

A rich, multi-angled treatment of the second line of Shema that succeeds in the essential thing: after reading it, one understands why Baruch Shem is whispered, what the two competing rationales are, and what one should be thinking when one says it. The Rabbi has organized the material around a genuine machlokes in the rishonim and acharonim (Tzelach vs. Midrash) and then extended it out through the mekubalim, the Shiltei Giborim's startling psak, and the Vilna Gaon's beautiful vort on the wings. The arc pays off. The dual-layer mareh mekomos is again a strength; the citations are clean and the expanded notes carry real content.

The piece is strongest in its middle chapters (V through IX) and in the Vilna Gaon section (XII), which is the emotional and intellectual peak. It is weakest in its Chanukah framing — a hakdamah in Section I and a gematria stitch in Section XIII that never quite fuse with the body — and in a handful of transcript-artifact moments that need editorial cleanup. There is one garbled Aramaic phrase that must be fixed and one section header that misdescribes its own content. These are correctable.

2.What Works

  • The Tzelach / Midrash machlokes as organizing spine (Sections V–VI). The document earns its length by locating a real nafka mina: is the b'lachash a din in Krias Shema or a permanent din in the words themselves? The Bayinaer Kloyz vignette makes the Tzelach's position vivid, and the Midrash's malachim-jealousy frame is set up so cleanly that a reader can carry the distinction away.
  • The Rav Sholom Shvadron vort (Section VII) is a small classic and is presented with the right register — the Kol Nidrei-bloated vs. Motzaei Yom Kippur-clean inversion lands, and the punchline (“where the head is”) is not over-explained.
  • The Rabi Yehoshua Boaz mini-biography (Section VIII). The observation that most of Klal Yisrael's daily limud depends on the work of a man whose name they don't know is a genuine chiddush for a lay reader, and the enumeration of his three contributions to every page of Shas is memorable. This is exactly the kind of biographical detail the series should preserve.
  • The Vilna Gaon “six wings / four wings” passage (Section XII). This is the intellectual and spiritual apex of the piece. The correlation of the two folded wings with the two hidden words k'vod malchuso, and the reframe of galei k'vod malchuscha as the petition to unfurl them, is the kind of vort the reader will take away and repeat. The build — Gemara in Chagigah, then Arizal, then Gaon, then the Yom Tov tefillah, then the practical kavanah — is patient and effective.
  • The Rav Asher Weiss vort on Amen Yehei Shemei Rabbah (Section XIV) is a satisfying resolution to the emotional tension the piece has been building — the neshamah wants to scream, and the halachah gives it a licensed channel elsewhere. Excellent editorial instinct to include it.

3.Where It's Weak or Could Be Sharper

Section I (Chanukah hakdamah) sits apart from the rest of the piece and is never called back. The Shelah on bein ha'zmanim and the Bach on refyon yadayim are lovely stand-alone points, but the body of the shiur is a Hilchos Krias Shema treatment that would read identically in any week of the year. The Chanukah frame reappears only in Section XIII (the gematria) and briefly at the sign-off. Either the hakdamah should be trimmed hard (a paragraph, not a two-header opening) or the Chanukah connection needs to be threaded through the body — e.g., using the Bach's refyon yadayim as a bookend to the Section XIII Tehillim quote about chariots and horses.

The Section IX header (“The Mekubalim Disagree Sharply”) misdescribes its own content. The section does not show mekubalim disagreeing with each other; it shows the Nefesh HaChaim and the Baal HaTanya agreeing with each other and both disagreeing with the Shiltei Giborim. Retitle to something like “The Mekubalim Push Back — Baruch Shem at the Center” or “The Kabbalistic Counter-View.”

The Section X title promises a three-way distinction (“Minhag, Takanah, or Tzivui?”) that the body never delivers. The text only develops minhag vs. takanah; tzivui is never defined, never invoked, never resolved. Either drop “or Tzivui” from the header or add a sentence identifying whose position tzivui would represent (presumably the mekubalim's stronger reading) and dismissing it.

The paraphrased blockquote in Section II (“My grandfather had Yishmael; my father had Eisav…”) is presented as a quotation but has no source line and no Hebrew original. As set, the reader can't tell whether this is a translation of a specific midrash or the Rabbi's dramatization. It should either get a Hebrew original underneath (from the same Pesachim 56a sugya or Bereishis Rabbah) or be demoted from a blockquote to inline narration (“Yaakov turned to them, in effect: perhaps among you there is also a Yishmael or an Eisav?”).

The “two weeks earlier” aside in Section XI (Ben Ish Chai on covering the eyes) is transcript residue. A written document has no “two weeks ago.” Rephrase as “As noted in an earlier shiur…” or, better, drop the temporal reference entirely and just cite the Ben Ish Chai directly.

The Klozz / Kloyz spelling is inconsistent (header: “The Klozz Incident”; body: “the Bayinaer Kloyz”). Standardize to Kloyz in both places. Also verify the name of the shul: the well-known Prague institution associated with the Noda B'Yehudah was the Klaus Synagogue (Klausen-Synagoge); “Bayinaer” is unfamiliar and may be a mishearing of Beinisher, Pinkas, or simply Klaus. Worth checking.

4.Factual / Citation Concerns

The Rav Shvadron quote in Section VII contains a garbled Aramaic phrase that must be fixed. The text reads “kemtanah gedolah she'yesh li b'beis ginzi.” This is a distortion of the famous Gemara in Shabbos 10b: “matanah tovah yesh li b'beis genazai u'Shabbos shemah.” The correct form — especially if the Rabbi was echoing this lashon to describe the “great gift” of Yom Kippur — is matanah tovah / gedolah she'yesh li b'beis genazai. “Kemtanah” and “ginzi” are not Aramaic words. Fix, and consider adding a footnote to Shabbos 10b.

The gematria of 1,358 checks out (ברוך = 228, שם = 340, כבוד = 32, מלכותו = 502, לעולם = 176, ועד = 80; total 1,358), but the piece asserts the number and then does nothing with it — the connection to Chanukah is via k'vod malchuso, not via the number. Either use the number (find a Chanukah anchor that equals 1,358 — if none exists, drop the number) or, cleaner, cut the “1,358” line entirely and let the k'vod malchuso ↔ b'sheim Hashem nazkir move stand on its own. Right now the number is a claim with no consequence.

Footnote 1 (“Shelah HaKadosh, Maseches Sukkah, Ner Mitzvah”) — the Shelah's classic discussion of bein ha'zmanim on Chanukah is usually located in Maseches Chanukah (specifically Ner Mitzvah), not Maseches Sukkah. Verify and correct if needed.

Footnote 8 cites the Moshe / malachim midrash as “Devarim Rabbah 2:36.” The standard locus is Devarim Rabbah 2:36 in some editions but often cited as Devarim Rabbah Va'eschanan 2:36 or as Pesikta d'Rav Kahana. Also worth cross-referencing to Rashi on Devarim 6:4, who brings the same idea. A cf. line would tighten the citation.

Footnote 15 (Rambam, Hilchos Krias Shema 1:4) with the lashon “v'eich nahagu.” The body text (Section X) says the Rambam writes “ein nahagu.” These are two different phrases. The Rambam's actual formulation should be reproduced verbatim and both body and footnote made to match.

The name “Onyu” (Section I) as a Hungarian woman's name is worth a quick verification — the Hungarian word anyu / anyuka means “mother/mommy” and is not a proper name; the actual name may have been Ágnes, Annó, or something similar that the Rabbi rendered phonetically. A brief check with Rav Chaim Adler or his family would resolve it.

5.Small Polish Suggestions

  • “Poof” in Section II (“Suddenly — poof — nistalkah heimenu ru'ach ha'kodesh”) is a shiur-register word that reads as too casual in a sefer-quality document. Consider “abruptly” or simply cut the interjection.
  • Section IV's parenthetical about carcinogens in baked ziti (“Years later I learned it's full of carcinogens”) is charming in the shiur but sits oddly in written form. Either preserve it as an italicized aside marked as the Rabbi's voice, or trim.
  • “Nuanses” (Sections IX and fn12) is a Yiddish-English hybrid. Standardize to “nuances” or, to preserve the Yiddish register consistently, “chilukim.”
  • “Fargess fun dem” (Section IX) is untranslated Yiddish. Add a gloss (“forget about it”) either inline or in a bracket, so the reader without Yiddish isn't stopped cold.
  • Section V, the Practical Nafka Mina bullet list, has a stray closing tag confusion — the italic-close of the sentence “there is no issur. One may say it b'kol ram:” runs through the colon and into the list, so the whole list is rendered italic in the source. Fix the HTML: close the italic before the list starts.
  • The mother-name dedication in Section I ends the paragraph on a Hungarian name with no further context. Even one clause (“a survivor of…” or “whose family came from…”) would give the reader an anchor for the l'ilui nishmas.
  • Section XV closes with “a lengthy discussion in the sefarim” and moves on. If the piece is not going to enter the yichuda ila'ah/tata'ah details, a footnote pointing the interested reader to Nefesh HaChaim Sha'ar 3 pereks 11–14 (already cited in fn12) would be the courteous cross-reference.
  • The final sign-off (“A lichtige, freilche Chanukah!”) is warm and fits the Chanukah week, but it undercuts what could be a stronger structural close returning to the k'vod malchuso theme. Consider placing it above — not after — a one-sentence bracket back to Section I or the Vilna Gaon's kavanah.

6.Bottom Line

A strong, well-organized treatment whose intellectual spine (Tzelach vs. Midrash) and spiritual peak (the Vilna Gaon on the wings) more than justify its length — fix the garbled matanah tovah / beis genazai phrase in Section VII, tighten the Chanukah frame so the hakdamah and the gematria actually connect to the body, and correct the two headers that overpromise (Section IX “Disagree Sharply,” Section X “or Tzivui”), and this becomes a genuinely sefer-quality chapter.
