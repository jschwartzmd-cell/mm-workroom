1 January 2025 — מַדּוּעַ אֵין מַסֶּכֶת חֲנוּכָּה

Independent review · read fresh, no exposure to the writing process · 23 August 2026

1.Overall Read

This is one of the more ambitious pieces in the series: a six-teretz survey of a classical kushya that builds, with real momentum, toward a historically-anchored resolution from Rav Reuven Margaliyos and closes with a beautiful remez in Bava Kama on Yochanan and his sons. The structure is essentially a scholarly argument dressed as a shiur, and it earns that ambition. The Erev Zos Chanukah hakdamah is warm and properly framed, the six teirutzim are arranged in a rising order (each one addressing a weakness in the previous), and the final Bircas HaShalom drash — חנוני = יוחנן, with patur read as the tikkun of the descendants — lands as a genuine closer rather than an appended chidush.

The piece is strongest in Sections VIII (Rav Margaliyos / Tumultus Iudaicus) and XIII (the Bava Kama remez). It is weakest in the middle-numbering (Sections V and VI, where the argumentative armature briefly wobbles) and in the pileup at Sections IX–X, where two short subsections — Sefas Emes and b'sha'as ha'sakanah — feel like bolt-ons that dilute the momentum built by Section VIII. Two factual points want a second look: the chronology of the Sikariim and the length of the Bar Kochva revolt. Otherwise the historical apparatus is careful.

2.What Works

  • The six-teretz spine. The document does what a written survey ought to do — each teretz is stated, given its strongest expression, then queried. The Vilna Gaon's "it was lost" is answered by the missing cross-references. The vendetta teretz is answered by the Sforno. The Chasam Sofer's "only what the olam doesn't know" is triangulated with the Rambam on Menachos. This is the right way to arrange a kushya-survey shiur for the printed page.
  • The Rav Margaliyos section (VIII). Genuinely well constructed. The frame — a shopkeeper in Eretz Yisrael who wrote dozens of sefarim in his spare time — introduces the author properly. The Tumultus Iudaicus triple-revolt setup gives real historical scaffolding. The Alexandria section (great synagogue, Trajan, the yeshivah learning Ki Savo, the sea turning red) is dramatic without becoming lurid. The two pieces of supporting evidence — the Targum Hashivim as Roman-inherited surveillance text and the two sardiotin in Bava Kama — are precisely the right corroboration for the political-censorship thesis.
  • The Bircas HaShalom remez (Section XIII). The best sustained chidush in the piece. The three-way linkage — Yochanan's cheit of fire-outside on Yom Kippur, the chenvani's candle-outside in the mishnah, the Chashmonaim's ner Chanukah outside as tikkun — is tight and structurally satisfying. It also answers the Bnei Yissaschar's kushya from Section XII cleanly, closing a bracket the piece opened.
  • The dual-layer footnote system continues to serve well. The academic-flag notes (fn3 for the Apter Rav vort, fn6 for the reported Chasam Sofer, fn15 for Dio Cassius, fn27 for the Bircas HaShalom) are used exactly where they belong — sources whose provenance is oral, non-canonical, or contested. This is textbook.
  • The Horayos correction (fn7). The footnote quietly notes that the Rabbi cited "Chagigah" but the sugya is in Horayos. This is precisely the right way to handle in-shiur slips without embarrassment — a factual fix, calmly labeled, without editorial fuss.
  • The Erev Zos Chanukah hakdamah (Section I) is well-paced. The Bnei Yissaschar's outstretched hand, the Apter Rav on January 1st, and the Rav Yaakov Kamenetsky anecdote are three miniatures that share a theme (turnings, resolutions, last gates) without over-explaining the connection. The "79% of resolutions" aside and the "two masechtos" retort are the kind of shiur-warmth that survives translation to print.

3.Where It's Weak or Could Be Sharper

Section V's header mislabels its content. The heading reads "The Sforno's Frame — There Was No Cheit at All," but the subsection opens with two full paragraphs on the Rambam praising the Chashmonaim and on the Rambam's historical hakdamah at Hilchos Chanukah. The Sforno arrives only in the third subsection. Either restructure so the Sforno leads, or retitle Section V as something like "The Rambam and the Sforno — No Cheit at All." As it stands, a reader scanning the document by headers will miss the Rambam material entirely.

Sections IX and X pile up after the climax. The Rav Margaliyos teretz in Section VIII is the argumentative peak. Section IX (Sefas Emes — concealment as inner yesod) is thematically resonant but is essentially a one-paragraph restatement of the same idea in mystical register; Section X (b'sha'as ha'sakanah at the window) is a tangent that never quite connects back to the thesis. The piece would breathe better if these were folded — Section IX collapsed into a closing paragraph of Section VIII, Section X either dropped or moved to a footnote-length sidebar. Right now they read as leftover material that the shiur ran through before running out of time (which is likely what happened, but the written version can be tighter).

The 9/11 callout in Section VIII interrupts the Trajan climax. The Rabbi's rebbe reading the tochachah from the roof on Ocean Parkway is a genuinely moving piece — but it is inserted between the Ki Savo pesukim the Alexandria yeshivah was learning and the moment Trajan says "Here I am. That is me." and slaughters them. The most powerful sentence in the section (the sea turning red with Jewish blood) follows immediately after the callout, which blunts its impact. Move the 9/11 note to a sidebar box after the Trajan climax, or fold it into the footnote on fn18. Placement matters here.

The "Sikariim" pivot conflates two rebel groups. The document has the Sikariim surviving the Bar Kochva revolt (132–135 CE) and then relocating to Alexandria, Cyprus, and Libya to incite the Diaspora Revolt. But the Kitos War / Diaspora Revolt is dated 115–117 CE — roughly fifteen years before Bar Kochva. The narrative sequence in Section VIII inverts the actual chronology. The Rabbi's underlying point survives (Judean rebels fanning out into the Diaspora and inciting local Jewish revolt), but the specific claim that Bar Kochva survivors seeded the Kitos War is not historically defensible. A brief rewrite — "after successive failed revolts at home, Judean rebels dispersed into the diaspora" — would keep the thrust without the anachronism. (See also Factual Concerns.)

The final closing (Section XIV) is thin. "The Rabbi closed: there is much more to say, but he must run to give another shiur in Brooklyn" is faithful to what happened in the room but leaves the written document without a real close. The Bircas HaShalom remez in Section XIII is the true peak; a two-sentence coda after it — even just returning to the "outstretched hand" of the opening and observing that the tikkun of Yochanan's sons is itself a kind of last-hand acceptance — would give the piece a bracket. As it stands the reader is dismissed with a L'chaim and no synthesis.

4.Factual / Citation Concerns

Bar Kochva revolt described as "some nine years of guerrilla warfare." The revolt ran 132–135 CE — roughly three and a half years. Nine years may be a conflation with the longer Roman military response, but the plain figure is wrong. Correct to "roughly three years" or "three-and-a-half years."

Sikariim / chronology. As noted above, the Sicarii as a named faction belong to the First Revolt era (mid-first-century CE, ending at Masada c. 73 CE); the Diaspora Revolt was 115–117 CE, before Bar Kochva. The document's sequence (Bar Kochva → survivors move to Alexandria → Kitos War) reverses the actual order. Rework the narrative flow of Section VIII to keep the political-rebel theme without pinning it to this specific chronology.

Yerushalmi Sukkah — Trajan story. The ma'aseh is often cited as Sukkah 5:1 in the Yerushalmi and Sukkah 51b in the Bavli (as fn16 and fn17 have it). Historically, the massacre in Alexandria that Roman sources associate with this period was carried out under Trajan by his general Lusius Quietus (from whom the war takes its name, "Kitos"), not personally by Trajan himself. The Gemara's "Trajan" (Torianus/Turnus Rufus territory) is a talmudic naming that scholars debate; the shiur's identification is defensible on Gemara's terms but a one-line acknowledgment in fn17 that Roman sources place the massacre under Quietus, not Trajan personally, would be scholarly best-practice.

Ptolemy's mother named "Arneves." The Gemara in Megillah 9a explains the shinui of arneves to tze'iras raglayim, and the traditional explanation is that Ptolemy's wife (in some versions, mother) was named Arnevet (or the family name resembled it — the Ptolemaic dynasty descends from Lagos, "hare"). The document states "mother — from Amalek — was named Arneves." The Amalek gloss is a homiletical tradition, not a plain reading of the Gemara. Consider softening to "as tradition explains" or moving the Amalek detail into fn19.

Chasam Sofer — two teirutzim, no fixed siman. The document gives two distinct teirutzim in the name of the Chasam Sofer (Section IV, the vendetta; Section VI, the "only what's unknown"). Footnote 6 flags the first as reported by his grandson (Chut HaMeshulash); fn11 tags the second to Drashos / Toras Moshe, Parshas Mikeitz / Vayeishev. The second citation is loose — three possible parshiyos without a fixed drush number. If a specific location can be confirmed, pin it; if not, a "widely cited in the tradition of the Chasam Sofer" hedge in the body text would match how fn6 handles the first attribution.

Rabi born the day Rabi Akiva died. The chronology in Section VI ("~135 CE") echoes the well-known Gemara statement in Kiddushin 72b. The math for "350 years total" checks out roughly, but a footnote pointing to Kiddushin 72b would be worth adding for the reader who wants the source.

5.Small Polish Suggestions

  • Yiddish phonetic transliterations are heavier in this piece than in the reference document. "posh'tushe pshat," "moidi'ach," "krenkel" (twice), "muchlat diminished," "misakein" all appear in body text. Each is defensible as preserving spoken register, but the cumulative effect is that a reader without yeshivish background stumbles more often than in the Ketores review. Consider standardizing: keep one or two per section as flavor, gloss the rest.
  • "Reid Bites." The document refers to Reb Shmueli Bornstein as "known for his 'Reid Bites.'" A reader outside Lakewood will have no idea what this is. Either add a one-line gloss (short-form chaburos he distributes) or drop the reference.
  • "Tzimim" on Ocean Parkway. In the 9/11 callout, "his rebbe in Tzimim" is ambiguous — this looks like a proper noun (a yeshiva name) but is not glossed. If it's Chaim Berlin, or Torah Vodaas, or another specific institution, name it plainly.
  • "Wilanowitzer / Velver." Both names appear in Section I referring to the same source (fn3). A reader hits both without knowing they are the same person. One quick parenthetical — "the Velver (i.e., the Wilanowitzer Rebbe)" — would clarify.
  • Header for Section VII ("Teretz #4 — Megillas Taanis Already Existed") is clean; header for Section VIII ("Teretz #5 — Rav Reuven Margaliyos: The Political Reason") is clean; but Section VI is "Teretz #3 — The Chasam Sofer: Rabbeinu HaKadosh Wrote Only the Unknown" — two Chasam Sofer teirutzim both flagged, but with Teretz #3 arriving after a Section V that wasn't numbered as a teretz. If the piece is going to number, it should number consistently: consider tagging Section V explicitly as a bridging note ("Interlude — Was There a Cheit at All?") so the numbering runs cleanly 1–5 across the six actual answers.
  • Eight mentions of Chanukah. In Section XIII the "trivia" of eight mentions is delivered without a footnote. If Yossi wants this pinned for readers who go looking, a fn listing the seven "Chanukah" and one "Chashmonai" locations would be useful — and would double as a beautiful mareh mekomos mini-index.
  • The lead paragraph lists heoros from "Josh Shapp, Robert Engelbert, and Reb Shmueli Bornstein" but the body text only credits Shapp (via Bornstein) and later Engelbert (mentioned once by name, no specific heorah credited). Either drop Engelbert from the lead or add a line crediting his contribution.

6.Bottom Line

A genuinely accomplished six-teretz survey that peaks with the Rav Margaliyos political-censorship reading and closes with a beautiful Bircas HaShalom remez — tighten the Sikariim/Bar Kochva chronology, fix the Section V header, fold Sections IX–X back into the Section VIII climax, and add a two-sentence bracket-closing coda, and this is one of the strongest pieces in the series.
