Mareh Mekamos · Shiur Review

9 February 2025 — The Origins and Development of שמונה עשרה

Content-based review · reviewer read the transcript, summary, and finished write-up · 23 August 2026

1.The Shiur in One Paragraph

This shiur opens what the Rav frames as a long, multi-month study of Shemoneh Esrei by laying its historical and structural foundations. It moves through five main pieces: the classic machloikes in Berachos 26b between avos tiknum and k'neged temidim tikunum, resolved through the Gemara's own synthesis that the Avos set the times and Chazal set the structure; the three-part shape of the tefillah (shevach — bakashah — hoda'ah); three Gemara-sources for the number eighteen (the mentions of Hashem in Havu La'Hashem, the eighteen chuliyos hashidrah, and the eighteen names in Kri'as Shema); the addition of the nineteenth berachah in Yavneh by Shmuel HaKatan against the early minim, together with the famous story of his forgetting it a year later; and the Rambam's magisterial historical reconstruction in Hilchos Tefillah, tracing fixed nusach to Ezra HaSofer as a response to the linguistic collapse of the golus Bavel generation. Two "bookend" observations frame the material: an older midrash in Pirkei d'Rabi Eliezer that the malachim already recited these berachos, and the Avudraham's striking shitas yachid that word-counting is futile because nuscha'os have always varied. The Rav closes with his own long-standing question: what becomes of tefillah when the korbanos return in the days of Moshiach?

2.What Was Strongest

  • The Rambam as the organizing spine of the shiur. The most valuable move in the shiur is treating Rambam, Hilchos Tefillah perek aleph as the master text and letting everything else — the machloikes in Berachos, the Yavneh episode, the Avudraham — hang off it. The Rambam's claim that daily tefillah is d'oraisa in its three-part shape (praise, request, thanks) but d'rabbanan in its number and nusach, and that fixed nusach is a golus-instrument born of a lost lashon, is a genuinely powerful framing. It converts an intimidating structural topic into a historical narrative with a clear cause.
  • The Shmuel HaKatan sequence. The pairing of the two Gemaros — the composition scene and the forgetting scene — is handled with unusual care. The Rav does not treat the "forgetting" as an embarrassing aside; he uses it to introduce the tzaddik's different relationship to fixed tefillah, and to bring in the kohen gadol who ended his life a tzedoki as a control case against al ta'amin b'atzmecha. That is high-quality shiur architecture: an anecdote used to teach three distinct yesodos.
  • The Nefesh HaChaim as the answer to the Rishonim's kushya. The Rosh's honest admission that he has no satisfying answer — that any Jew, even a woman, can curse minim effectively — is set up beautifully, and the Nefesh HaChaim's response (that Shmuel HaKatan's anavah was the specific kli for a berachah whose every letter aligns with higher-world structures) lands with real weight. It also seeds the entire coming series: if that is what a berachah is, the word-by-word study ahead is not pilpul, it is exegesis.
  • The eighteen chuliyos + kol atzmosai teaching. The Pnei Yehoshua's point — that most mitzvos engage specific eivarim but Shemoneh Esrei uniquely mobilizes the whole body through the spine that bows — is genuinely illuminating. It also justifies (rather than merely defends) the anatomical count against the modern thirty-three-vertebrae objection: the count is functional, not descriptive.
  • The Avudraham as intellectual honesty. Naming a shitas yachid as a shitas yachid, while giving its argument full force, is exactly what a survey shiur should do. The Avudraham's reasoning (variant nuscha'os with differing word counts undermine the count-based edifice) is a real objection, and putting it on the table now — before months of counting words — is the honest move.

3.What Was Weaker or Worth Pushing Back On

The Rambam is presented but not confronted. The Rambam's reconstruction is startling and, on any careful reading, in tension with the older midrash from Pirkei d'Rabi Eliezer that the malachim were already reciting these berachos — and, more sharply, with the Gemara's own attribution to the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah as the k'neged korbanos institutors. The shiur places these three accounts side by side but does not adjudicate. Are the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah identical with "Ezra u'beis dino" for the Rambam's purposes? If so, why does the Rambam foreground the linguistic-collapse story rather than the k'neged korbanos one? A single sentence resolving this would tighten the whole section.

The "controversial chiddush from Rav Kook" is dropped in without integration. The Rebbe's own note — that he does not hold of the shitah and does not think anyone to Rav Kook's right does — is honorable, but if that is the position, it is worth asking why the chiddush is included at all. In a section titled "Tefillah in the Era of Moshiach," the operative question — will Shemoneh Esrei continue when the korbanos return — deserves the Rambam's own answer (Hilchos Melachim perek 11–12 and Hilchos Ma'aseh HaKorbanos) rather than a headline-grabbing outlier that the Rebbe himself rejects.

The "vague legend of the chacham named Yochanan or Shimon" section is the shiur's weakest moment. The historical claim that a chacham deliberately infiltrated early Christianity to engineer distinctions — possibly identified with Paul — is presented cautiously ("vague legend," "some identify") but still occupies real airtime in a shiur that is otherwise disciplined about sourcing. The reference to Siman 580 in the Shulchan Aruch as an anchor is thin. If the point of the paragraph is to explain why the community needed sharp external differentiation from the minim (and thus why Birkas HaMinim was necessary), that structural point can be made without the biographical legend, which distracts more than it illuminates.

4.Halachic / Lomdish Observations

The Rambam vs. Ramban machloikes deserves more mileage than it gets. The one-line treatment ("Rambam says d'oraisa, Ramban says d'rabbanan") conceals what is arguably the most consequential dispute in hilchos tefillah. On the Rambam, a person who davens in his own words — three elements, in order — has satisfied a chiyuv d'oraisa; on the Ramban, he has done nothing d'oraisa at all. This has direct implications for safek tefillah, for a person too sick to daven the fixed nusach, for women's obligation on the various shitos, and for the halachic weight of any chiddush that alters the fixed nusach. Any one of those applications would have made the machloikes live.

The synthesis of the two Gemara shitos is done too smoothly. The Rav accepts the Gemara's own reconciliation — Avos set the times, Chazal did the rest via asmichinhu a'korbanos — but does not address the real lomdishe question: is the zman of tefillah defined by the Avos's original institution or by the korban's zman? This matters practically: the classic dispute about plag haminchah, the outer edge of tefillas mincha, and even the zman of ma'ariv (fixed by Yaakov's pegi'ah versus the hektar chalavim v'eivarim) hinges on it. The synthesis harmonizes the two views formally but obscures the halachic edge.

The status of "moseif ohel al ohel" — adding a berachah — is left implicit. The addition of the nineteenth berachah at Yavneh raises a real question: how did Rabban Gamliel and his beis din have the koach to add to a structure whose count was itself derived from d'oraisa hints (the eighteen shemos in Kri'as Shema, the chuliyos)? Are all three Gemara-derivations of the number eighteen retrospective asmachtos, or are they binding structural claims that Chazal overrode? A brief engagement with the Chasam Sofer's or Chida's treatment of this question would have added lomdishe substance.

The Avudraham's shitah has a sharper halachic edge than the shiur registers. If word-counts are not load-bearing (because nuscha'os vary), then b'dieved a person who davens a corrupted or shortened version has still fulfilled his chiyuv as long as the matbei'a (opening and closing formulas, and the core body) is intact. On the mainstream shitah, that is less clear. This has direct application to ba'al korei-type situations, to lo yatza claims in Shemoneh Esrei, and to the halachic status of nusach-switching (Ashkenaz ↔ Sfard ↔ Edot HaMizrach), which the shiur touches only in passing.

5.Follow-up Questions Worth Asking the Rav

Q1.How does the Rambam's Ezra-based historical reconstruction relate to the Gemara's attribution to the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah and to the older midrash that the malachim already recited these berachos? Are the three accounts one integrated picture, or does the Rav hold that the Rambam is offering something distinct from — even in tension with — the Pirkei d'Rabi Eliezer tradition?
Q2.On the Rambam that daily tefillah is d'oraisa in its three-part shape but d'rabbanan in its nusach, what is the halachic status of a person today who davens the three elements in his own words but skips the fixed Shemoneh Esrei? Has he fulfilled the chiyuv d'oraisa while transgressing a chiyuv d'rabbanan? Or does the d'rabbanan tekana absorb the d'oraisa so that only the fixed nusach now discharges it?
Q3.The Rebbe raised the question of what becomes of tefillah when the korbanos return but resolved it only through the chazal of "kol hatefillos beteilos chutz mehoda'ah." Setting aside the Rav Kook chiddush, what does the Rambam himself hold? He is explicit that korbanos return; is he also explicit anywhere that fixed tefillah continues alongside them, or does he leave the question open?
Q4.The Avudraham's shitas yachid was presented as the outlier; is there any subsequent posek — the Chida, the Yaavetz, the Aruch HaShulchan — who takes the Avudraham's line seriously enough to soften the mainstream count-based approach in a practical halacha? And how does the Rav himself sit with the Avudraham's underlying observation, which does seem factually correct: that Ashkenaz and Sfard nuscha'os differ substantially in word counts?
Q5.The Nefesh HaChaim's answer — that Shmuel HaKatan's anavah made him the specific kli for a berachah tuned to higher worlds — is compelling. But it raises a question about every other berachah in Shemoneh Esrei: who among the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah is the parallel kli for Refa'einu, for Barech Aleinu, for Modim? Does the mesorah preserve any tradition of individual authorship, or are they treated as a ma'aseh beis din composed collectively?
Q6.The Rambam frames fixed nusach as a golus-response to a lost lashon. In modern Eretz Yisrael, lashon hakodesh is again spoken natively by millions. Does the underlying rationale for fixed nusach weaken? Presumably not — but on what basis? Is fixed nusach now independently justified by the Nefesh HaChaim-style structural claim, so that the original historical ta'am is superseded and no longer load-bearing?

6.Bottom Line

A strong, foundational opening shiur to what will clearly be an ambitious series. Its greatest strength is the choice of the Rambam as organizing text: it converts the intimidating question "where does Shemoneh Esrei come from?" into a coherent historical narrative from Avos, through Ezra, to Yavneh, to us. Its weaknesses are of the kind that mainly matter to a written sefer: three parallel accounts of authorship are lined up without adjudication; the Rambam vs. Ramban machloikes is under-mined for its halachic yield; and the "vague legend" and Rav Kook chiddush passages do not earn their space. The Nefesh HaChaim answer, the Pnei Yehoshua teaching on the spine, and the Avudraham's honest objection are the three pieces that will stay with a careful reader — and each of them opens a door the series will now have to walk through.
