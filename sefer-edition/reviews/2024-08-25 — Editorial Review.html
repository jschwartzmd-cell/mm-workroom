Mareh Mekamos · Independent Editorial Review

25 August 2024 — אַשְׁרֵי — A Gateway to Olam HaBa

Independent review · read fresh, no exposure to the writing process · 23 August 2026

1.Overall Read

This is a strong piece — well-paced, tightly focused on a single kapitel, and structurally alert in a way the subject demands. Ashrei is the kind of text that rewards a writer who slows down and lets structural features (missing letter, prepended pesukim, trailing Halleluyah) do theological work; the piece does exactly that, and the results are consistently satisfying. The Maharil Diskin narrative in Section VII is the most gripping stretch of storytelling in any Mareh Mekamos piece I have reviewed — a genuine set-piece with stakes, characters, and a chilling ending.

The two structural weaknesses are inherited from an earlier problem the June review already flagged: (a) the opening Section I is a floating anecdote that never connects to the topic, and (b) an ill-timed aside (“Eilav Tishma'un”) interrupts a climactic narrative moment. Both are the same class of error — charming shiur-context material that damages a written document by breaking momentum. There is also one likely factual error about the Maharil Diskin's age and one geographic epithet that may be misapplied. None are fatal; all are fixable in a light editorial pass.

2.What Works

  • The Maharil Diskin narrative (Section VII) is the piece's high point. The setup (young Rav in Brisk, resentful elder talmid chacham), the escalation (going shul-to-shul to alter the sifrei Torah), the Maharil Diskin's aghast pursuit, and the Erev Shabbos drowning at the mikveh — the whole sequence is paced expertly. It arrives just when the technical stira between siddur and Sefer Torah risks feeling academic, and gives the reader a reason to care.
  • The missing-nun / samech pairing (Section IV) is beautifully handled. Framing the structural omission as a theological statement that is immediately answered by the next verse (“yes, falls happen; and Hashem catches”) turns what could be a dry grammatical curiosity into a devotional insight. The prose earns the payoff.
  • The framing of the first prepended pasuk — “first sit, settle, focus, then say od y'hallelucha” — is one of the cleanest micro-arguments in the piece. It ties the mechanics of Pesukei d'Zimra back to the Chassidim HaRishonim and makes a structural point without straining.
  • The dual-layer footnote system continues to work well and shows further refinement here — the academic-flag box on footnote 18 (Sar M'Brisk as biographical source) is exactly the transparency the June review praised. The system is now a settled feature of the series.
  • The closing pasuk read as a plea to keep davening in olam ha'ba is a genuinely moving idea, well-placed. The follow-on paragraph (“thanking Hashem for the ability to thank Him”) extends it without over-explaining.

3.Where It's Weak or Could Be Sharper

Section I (“Reb Chaim Brisker and the Rogatchover”) does not belong where it is. It is a delightful chavrusa-in-Slutsk story, tied to the yahrtzeit mentioned in the dedication — but it has no thematic connection to Ashrei, no bridge into Section II, and the piece never returns to it. As written, it reads like a warmup that was left in when the tape started rolling. Two options: (a) cut it entirely and let the sponsorship line in the lead carry the yahrtzeit reference, or (b) rework the closing (Section VIII) to bracket back to it — e.g., noting that the same Reb Chaim Brisker whose yahrtzeit we mark today is the paradigm of the Jew who davens because he understands what he is saying, which is exactly the kavanah the Gemara's promise requires. Option (b) redeems the anecdote; option (a) tightens the piece.

The “Aside on Eilav Tishma'un” (end of Section VII) breaks the Maharil Diskin narrative at precisely the wrong moment. The reader has just absorbed the drowning at the mikveh and the grave lesson about mesorah. That is the moment to let the reader sit with what just happened — not to pivot into a secondary anecdote about a maspid's word choice and a Brisker Rav / Satmar Rav exchange over Rabbi Akiva Eiger's citation. The material is interesting on its own, but placed here it acts exactly like the June review's “Hakadosh aside” did in the ketores piece: it lands on a lesser point after a climactic one and blunts the impact. Move it to its own footnote, or lift it out into a companion sidebar, or cut. The narrative needs to be allowed to end.

Section VIII (“Closing”) is thin. Two short paragraphs, one recapping the structural points and one restating the Maharil Diskin lesson. The Maharil Diskin paragraph is fine as a takeaway, but the first paragraph reads as a table of contents. A closing that reaches back to something concrete — the Reb Chaim opening, or the “keep davening in olam ha'ba” note from Section IV — would give the piece the emotional cadence its content deserves.

Footnote 20 (Rav Schwab / Iyun Tefillah) is explicitly labeled “not directly cited in this shiur but appears in the original Mareh list.” That kind of meta-note belongs in a working document, not in a published mareh mekomos. Either integrate the content substantively (a sentence in the body about the tzitzis-holding practice, with the footnote as its citation) or cut. Orphaned cross-references invite the reader to wonder what is missing.

4.Factual / Citation Concerns

The Maharil Diskin's age at his Brisk appointment appears wrong. The piece states he “was less than thirty years old” when he became Rav of Brisk in the 1870s. R' Yehoshua Leib Diskin was born in 1818 and served in Brisk from roughly 1874–1877 — putting him in his mid-fifties, not his twenties. He was already a seasoned Rav (Lomza, Mezritch, Kovno, Shklov) before Brisk. The narrative point (elder talmid chacham resenting a newly-arrived Rav) can survive without the age claim; the age claim as stated is chronologically implausible and should be verified against Sar M'Brisk directly. If the shiur said this, it is a rare case where the rework should silently correct rather than transmit.

“Brisk was already known as Yerushalayim of Lita” (Section VII) is very likely a misapplied epithet. Yerushalayim d'Lita is the classical byname of Vilna, not Brisk. Brisk had its own titles (a Torah stronghold, the Ir Va'Em B'Yisrael in some sources) but the “Jerusalem of Lithuania” formula belongs to Vilna. Check the source; if it turns out the shiur said this, a bracketed clarification or a swap to a Brisk-specific epithet is warranted.

The Sar M'Brisk / HaSrof M'Brisk transliteration in footnote 18 is muddled. The sefer's name is השששׁרף מבריסק — “HaSaraf MiBrisk” (“The Seraph of Brisk”), not Sar (prince) and not Srof (which is not a word). Either “HaSaraf MiBrisk” or the accepted Ashkenaz pronunciation “HaSoref MiBrisk” would be correct. Author attribution to “Moshe Sheinberger” should also be verified — a title by this name is more commonly associated with other biographers of the Maharil Diskin.

“The Rebbe noted that during his years as a Rav in Miami” (Section V, Chassidic Practice of Repetition) has no antecedent — no earlier reference identifies which Rebbe, and no Rebbe has been introduced in the piece. In a written document (unlike a live shiur, where the speaker's frame of reference is understood) this needs to be named explicitly: which Rebbe, which Miami tenure. Otherwise cut the sentence and let the Vizhnitz siddur observation stand on its own.

Footnote 13 (R' Shlomo Kluger) is labeled “general reference” to Orach Chaim. This is the same kind of unpinned citation the June review flagged in footnote 1 of the ketores piece. Either narrow to a specific siman or acknowledge in the note that the position is attributed to R' Shlomo Kluger through talmidim/drashos rather than to a specific pinned text.

“Chazal moved that recitation forward into Shacharis” (Section III) as the explanation for the two Ashreis in Shacharis plus the missing one in Maariv — this is a nice framing, but it is a specific Rishonim position that should be sourced by name (Tur, Abudraham, or wherever it originates) in a footnote rather than presented as consensus.

5.Small Polish Suggestions

  • “says the Shlomo Kluger” (Section V) reads awkwardly — the definite article shouldn't sit in front of a personal name in English prose. Either “says R' Shlomo Kluger” or “the Shlomo Kluger writes” (if the intent is to use it as a title, as with “the Chazon Ish”). Pick one register and hold it.
  • Section VII's “HaSrof M'Brisk” parenthetical inside a sentence that already gives “Sar M'Brisk” is confusing — the reader gets two different-looking titles for one sefer with no signal that they are meant to be the same book. Fix the transliteration (see Factual section) and pick one primary form.
  • “machloikis” appears twice in Section VII. This is a Yiddish-inflected transliteration (as opposed to machlokes); the piece elsewhere uses more standard Hebrew transliterations. Standardize.
  • The dedication line in the lead is very dense — sponsor + iluy nishmas + additional yahrtzeit + Reb Chaim Brisker gloss — all in a single paragraph. Splitting the sponsorship (one line) from the Reb Chaim yahrtzeit note (a second line) would make both breathe. If Section I is cut, the Reb Chaim yahrtzeit note in the lead becomes even more important as the only place the name lives.
  • The transliteration “sheigitzlach” / “shekotzim” in Section I is fine in a Yiddish anecdote context, but if Section I stays, a brief gloss (“little rascals”) would help non-Yiddish-fluent readers over what is otherwise a puzzling exchange.
  • Footnote 3 places Posei'ach es yadecha at Tehillim 145:16; standard chapter-and-verse numbering has it at 145:16 in most editions but 145:15 in a few. Consistent with the Koren/Artscroll standard is best; note that footnote 11 uses 145:14 for Somech Hashem, which is consistent, so 145:16 here is correct — but worth spot-checking as a matched pair.

6.Bottom Line

A strong, structurally alert piece whose central Maharil Diskin narrative is the best storytelling in the series so far; cut or bracket-back the floating Reb Chaim opening, move the “Eilav Tishma'un” aside out of the narrative's climactic moment, correct the Maharil Diskin age and the HaSaraf MiBrisk transliteration, and this becomes one of the standout pieces in the sefer.
