Mareh Mekamos · Independent Editorial Review

16 March 2025 — מָגֵן אַבְרָהָם — The Soul of the First Berachah

Independent review · read fresh, no exposure to the writing process · 30 August 2026

1.Overall Read

This is an ambitious, theologically substantial piece — more speculative in reach than the ketores document but also more architectonic. The core structure is a three-mahalach commentary on a single word ("ובְך חוֹתמִין") that widens in scope from a Kotzker vort, to a Radak-driven Tif'eres Banim chiddush on the future eschatology of prayer, to a Maharam Schick reading that folds the entire descendants of Ishmael and Esav into the chasimah. The arc genuinely intensifies from section to section; the third mahalach is not a mere third example but the theological payoff of the first two.

The strongest single stretch is the Tif'eres Banim section (IV) — the chain Yechezkel → Radak → tefilos ke-neged temidin → verbal tense of the Avos → Rambam / Ramban reconciliation is airtight and satisfying. The weakest stretches are Section V (a single-paragraph orphan), Section VII (a bulleted recap that adds nothing), and the closing paragraph (generic). One HTML bug in a callout, one internally contradictory sentence about the letters of ישו / עשו, and severely out-of-order footnote numbering in the body will need cleanup before print. The theological argument of the third mahalach is presented responsibly — anchored in Rambam, Radbaz, Yaavetz, and Etz HaDaas Tov — but it is provocative material and one paraphrased blockquote of the censored Rambam should be flagged as paraphrase rather than quotation.

2.What Works

  • Section IV (Tif'eres Banim / Radak) is the best piece of sustained argument in the document. The logical chain moves cleanly from a textual observation in Yechezkel, through Radak's willingness to read it at face value, through the standard tefilos ke-neged temidin mapping, to the verbal-tense confirmation from Rashi and Sforno on the three Avos, to a two-pronged reconciliation with "לא תשתנה התורה." Every step earns the next. This is sefer-quality writing.
  • The intensifying three-mahalach structure is the piece's architectural achievement. Kotzker (generational), Tif'eres Banim (eschatological-liturgical), Maharam Schick (universal-messianic) — each successively wider. The reader senses the widening rather than being told about it, which is the mark of good structural writing.
  • The Chizkiyahu callout in Section VI lands with the right weight: a specific biblical precedent for the theological anxiety the section has just introduced ("would Avraham have wanted to marry?"). It grounds a large claim in a small, verifiable moment.
  • The dual-layer footnote system continues to be an achievement of the series. The expanded passages on Radak, Ramak, Rav Chaim Vital, Radbaz, and Yaavetz are genuinely informative and give the sefer serious scholarly texture. The academic-flag treatment on fn24 (Rav Baruch Ber wagon story, no published source) and fn25 (Minkud Metav, uncertain attribution) is exactly the right way to handle sources the shiur could not pin.
  • The Radbaz on shituf is deployed at exactly the right moment — after the "painful math" and before the censored Rambam — to establish that the theological ground is halachically respectable, not idiosyncratic. Without this hinge the Maharam Schick reading would feel free-floating.
  • The Rav Baruch Ber wagon story is well-placed: it prevents the Radbaz's shituf paragraph from reading as a whitewash of Christianity by illustrating, in a very concrete way, why shituf still counts as belief in something.

3.Where It's Weak or Could Be Sharper

Section V (“Kavanah in the First Berachah”) is a one-paragraph orphan. It sits between Mahalach ב' and Mahalach ג' as a stand-alone section, breaks the momentum built by Section IV, and could easily be folded into the last paragraph of IV (as a practical kavanah takeaway) or moved to the end of VII as part of the synthesis. As currently placed, the reader has to re-accelerate to enter Mahalach ג'.

Section VII (“Synthesis”) does not synthesize; it recapitulates. A three-bullet restatement of the three mahalachim the reader has just finished. If a synthesis is warranted, it should do actual synthetic work — e.g., observe that all three converge on the reading of "ובְך" (with you) as pointing forward to a future state in which Avraham's role is not diminished but expanded, so that the single chasimah is at once historical (Kotzker), liturgical (Tif'eres Banim), and universal (Maharam Schick). If it cannot do that synthetic work, cut it entirely and let the closing paragraph of VI serve as the ending.

The closing paragraph (“When we close ברכת אבות tomorrow morning...”) is generic. Every third shiur ends with something like this. Given how forcefully Section VI concludes (the whole tree, not just the surviving 25%), the closing could return to the Kotzker's chesed and observe that chesed to one's own is the necessary condition for a chasimah that will eventually embrace even Ishmael and Esav — bracketing the piece back to Mahalach א'.

The Ramban / pig aside in Section IV is a large detour at a point where the argument is nearly complete. It runs to a full paragraph on species-development, complete with a Darwinian disclaimer. Compress to a single sentence (“Cf. Ramban's parallel pshat on the future kashrus of the pig: the animal develops, the Torah's simanei kashrus do not change”) and keep the expanded note in fn12 for readers who want it.

The “painful math” framing (Section VI) is rhetorically arresting but theologically over-clean. “Seventy-five percent went off the path within two generations” treats Ishmael, Esav, and Yaakov as three equal units, which is a shiur-room simplification. Consider softening to “the majority of Avraham's line, considered by branches,” or acknowledging the rhetorical use directly (“stated crudely, three of Avraham's four grandsons”).

4.Factual / Citation Concerns

The letters of ישו and עשו are not the same. The body text says: “the very letters: י-ש-ו contains the same letters as ע-ש-ו (with a single substitution).” This is self-contradicting — if a letter is substituted, they are not the same. Fix to: “the two names differ by only one letter (י for ע),” or drop the letter-play entirely and let the gilgul tradition (Etz HaDaas Tov) do the work.

The blockquoted “censored Rambam” passage is a paraphrase, not a quotation. It is presented in a <blockquote> in italicized English, which visually marks it as a translated citation. The actual language in Rambam Hilchos Melachim 11:4 is considerably more compact and does not include phrases like “we are a messianic people; we believe in Mashiach.” Either (a) replace with an accurate translation of the Frankel text, or (b) demote the blockquote to indirect discourse (“The Rambam writes, in substance...”) so the reader is not misled about what is verbatim Rambam and what is the Rebbe's expansion.

The claim that Muhammad's teacher was a Jew (Section VI, drawing on Etz HaDaas Tov) should be labeled explicitly as a Kabbalistic tradition transmitted by Rav Chaim Vital. The footnote (fn18) hedges toward this but then adds “independently attested in early Muslim biographical sources.” The best-known early Muslim biographical tradition (Ibn Ishaq / Ibn Hisham) attests to a Christian monk (Bahira) and to a Christian cousin (Waraqa ibn Nawfal), not Jewish teachers. There are separate traditions of Jewish informants in Medina at a later stage, but these are not the sources of the “dream of the tree” account, which appears to come from Etz HaDaas Tov specifically. The “independently attested” claim in fn18 should be removed or narrowed.

The Minkud Metav / Mateh Tov citation (Section V, fn25) is honestly flagged in the footnote as an uncertain attribution. The body text, however, presents it flatly (“The Minkud Metav writes...”) as if it were a pinned citation. Bring the hedge into the body: “A tradition transmitted in the name of the Minkud Metav” or “A kabbalistic siddur-kavanah in this tradition holds....”

Yechezkel's vision as “Bayis Shlishi” is the standard read but not universal — some Rishonim take the vision as describing Bayis Sheini. A one-line footnote acknowledging this range would strengthen the section rather than weaken it, and would insulate the Tif'eres Banim's chiddush from a facile objection (“but there was a temid shel bein ha'arbayim in Bayis Sheini”).

The Ramban / pig drasha. The famous drasha (“lámah nikra shemo chazir? she'asid HKB"H l&e-hachaziro l&e-Yisrael”) is widely cited in Ramban's name in chassidishe literature but the actual textual location in Ramban is not straightforward — the source is more commonly attributed to Rabbeinu Bachye or to midrashic collections gathered under Ramban's name. The footnote (fn12) says “Ramban on Parshas Re'eh and parallel drashos”, which is loose. Either pin the citation to a specific text or attribute more broadly (“in a well-known drasha cited in the name of the Ramban”).

5.Small Polish Suggestions

  • HTML bug — the Rav Baruch Ber wagon callout (Section VI) has doubled/unbalanced <em> tags around the Yiddish quotation. Rendered output shows an italic-inside-italic which likely displays as un-italicized text where it should be italic. Rebalance the tags — there should be one <em> around the internal Yiddish quotation, sitting inside the outer <em> that italicizes the story.
  • Footnote numbering in the body is severely out of order. The reader encounters footnotes in the sequence 17, 11, 1, 3, 21, 22, 9, 10, 11, 7, 8, 16, 14, 12, 25, 23, 13, 18, 15, 19, 24, 2, 20, 8. In a shiur transcript this is unremarkable; in a printed sefer it makes cross-referencing painful and signals to the reader that the notes were built as source-tags rather than as a numbered apparatus. Before sefer publication the notes should be renumbered in the order they appear in the body.
  • “מָגֵן אַבְרָהָם” as page heading and section heading both use the tag “The Soul of the First Berachah”. The subtitle in the header (“Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Sunday Morning Series on tefilah”) is good; the lead paragraph immediately below tries to summarize all three mahalachim in a single sentence and runs to nearly 100 words. Break into two sentences or into a short opening line plus a bulleted list of the three mahalachim.
  • The sponsorship / Purim Katan lead-block in the second lead paragraph is charming for a shiur but out of place in the sefer version. In the ketores piece this kind of material was folded into a light opening anecdote; here it sits as a raw dedication with the halachic aside about Tachanun. For the sefer edition, either move to a footnote (“delivered on Purim Katan; the shiur noted the halachic status of Tachanun...”) or drop.
  • “י"ב שבטי קַה” — the standard phrasing is שבטי י-ה (from Tehillim 122). Standardize.
  • “זאל גיין כפרות” is a Yiddishism (colloquial “let them go be forgotten”) — effective in the shiur; in the sefer, either preserve with a translation gloss or replace with the register-neutral “write them off.”
  • “גֵעַשְמַק” (“It may not be immediately ge'shmak”, Section VI opening) is warm and register-appropriate for the shiur; in a printed sefer it will read as jargon. Consider “may not be immediately palatable” or preserve the Yiddish with a transliteration note.
  • The lead and the section headings both use “Mahalach” interchangeably with “approach”/“chiddush”. A one-time gloss (“three mahalachim — three angles of approach”) would help the reader who is new to the term.

6.Bottom Line

A theologically ambitious piece with a genuinely intensifying three-mahalach arc; Section IV (Tif'eres Banim / Radak) is the strongest sustained argument the series has produced so far; Section V is an orphan and Section VII a redundant recap that should be folded or cut; the paraphrased “censored Rambam” blockquote must be either translated accurately or demoted from blockquote to indirect discourse; the ישו / עשו letter claim is self-contradicting and needs a one-line fix; one HTML bug in the wagon-driver callout; and the footnote apparatus must be renumbered in body-order before sefer publication — fix those and this is a strong entry.
