Mareh Mekamos · Independent Editorial Review

27 October 2025 — The Depth of כוונה in שמונה עשרה

Independent review · confined to the finished HTML document as it stood before the corrections of this pass; the transcript and the summary were not consulted for this part · 18 October 2026

1.Overall Read

A twelve-section write-up (roughly 4,700 words of body, plus a twenty-note Mareh Mekomos apparatus of about 1,900 words and a closing סיכום) on the end of the Amidah: Yihyu Leratzon, the history and meaning of Elokai Netzor, the closing lines on dust and humility, the four למען-phrases and the Chasam Sofer’s reading of the rainbow, and the three steps back. The spine is good. It moves from a verse (Section I), through a liturgical history (II–III), a spiritual theme (IV–VII), a prayer-text (VIII–IX) and a halachic practice (X–XII), and each unit is lifted by a story: the Volozhin land, the Novardok pharmacy, Rava on the bloodied threshold, Nebuchadnezzar’s letter. The piece is at its best where it states a difficulty and leaves it standing (the callout in Section XI).

The hard editorial issues are seven: (a) the page contradicts itself on Yoma: Section X first says the High Priest “took three steps back” on the authority of Yoma 53, then says the same Gemara “does not actually mention the number three”; (b) the opening verse is quoted short (seven words) but described as having exactly ten words; (c) the central story is told with two names for the same sage (רבה in the text, the mnemonic אביי ורבא in note 16) and, as far as I know, the wrong one; (d) the Nebuchadnezzar story is internally inconsistent (the body has him retreat three steps; note 17 has him run three steps; the passage as I remember it speaks of four paces); (e) two Hebrew “quotations” read as composed (Rav Yosef’s blessing; the Divine reply in note 17); (f) live-shiur residue that will date badly (“His first יארצייט is approaching: he would — I do not know whether you saw it”; “Last week we discussed”; “a few weeks ago we discussed”; the first-person Brisk anecdote); and (g) several notes carry unfinished editorial language (“Verify exact placement”, “Verify exact location”, “Verify the source as desired”).

2.What Works

  • The ten-יו״ד opening (I). A short, vivid hook. It gives the reader something to count on the page.
  • The two readings of Mar’s prayer (III). “Guard my tongue” read first as lashon hara, then as an apology for a heart that was elsewhere, is the most original passage in the piece, and the turn from the first to the second is well made.
  • The dust/stars balance (VI). The two-pockets image and the star-and-sand pairing explain each other; Section V sets the Slabodka and Novardok poles up well for it.
  • The Rava story (X). The image of the student who walks backward and bleeds, and the blind teacher who blesses him, carries the section; the line that the dried blood blackened the threshold is memorable.
  • The Nebuchadnezzar turn (X). The irony that the destroyer of the Temple gave honor to Heaven and that we imitate his steps for a different end is the cleverest point in the piece.
  • The honest difficulty (XI). “I do not understand for the life of me where three comes from” followed by the פרסאות conjecture is exactly how a sefer should leave an open problem.
  • Note discipline. Notes 1–20 are numbered in strict body order with back-links.

3.Where It’s Weak or Could Be Sharper

The Yoma contradiction. Section X opens: “the כהן גדול left the קדש הקדשים… He took three steps back.” Thirty lines later: “The גמרא in יומא that we cited earlier does not actually mention the number three… The number ‘three’ comes specifically from the נבוכדנצר story.” Both cannot stand. Decide which; cite the daf for the number (as far as I know, the page after the one cited contains the three steps at the end of prayer), and separate the High Priest, who walks backward, from the worshipper, who takes three steps.

The verse and its count. Section I quotes יהיו לרצון אמרי פי והגיון לבי לפניך and says it has “exactly ten תיבות” and ten יו״דין, beginning and ending with a יו״ד. The quotation as printed has seven words and ends with ך. The count works, if at all, for the full verse including ה׳ צורי וגואלי. Quote the whole verse and say how the Name is counted.

Rava or Rabbah. The central story is told of רבה, a student of רב יוסף, who “became the רבי of כלל ישראל”; but the same section says he disagreed with אביי in roughly three hundred disputes, and note 16 says “the standard mnemonic refers to אביי ורבא… not רבה”. The disputes of אביי ורבא are the famous ones, and as far as I know the מחוזא yeshivah was Rava’s. Either the section is about Rava or the note must say why not.

Composed Hebrew speech. Rav Yosef’s blessing (יהי רצון מלפני הקדוש ברוך הוא שתהיה ראש לכל ישראל, ראש לעיר הזאת — מחוזא) is in a Hebrew register that does not match the Aramaic of the Gemara it is attributed to, and note 17’s Divine reply (אתה רצת אחר כבודי שלוש פסיעות…) is likewise given as a quotation without a source. Either supply the Aramaic or recast as reported speech.

The gates of tears (IX). “And how do we know the gates of tears are still open? From a פסוק in איכה: ‘He has placed a cloud, and that cloud blocks תפילה’.” That verse proves the opposite of what the sentence asks. The sequence needs one more step (the proof that the gates of תפילה are locked, then the gates of tears, then the cloud).

Voice. The body alternates between a learned third person and the live first person (“I remember when I first went to my ישיבה”, “Vadai true”, “Are you going to let Yankel humiliate you?!”, “Whoa, the רב didn’t say…”). For a sefer edition choose one voice, and put the personal recollections in a clearly marked passage.

Block quotation of oral teachings. The Volozhin story, the pharmacy of Novardok, the Chasam Sofer’s vort and Rav Yosef’s blessing are given in the colloquial present with no source and no indication that they are reports. Mark them as such.

Section XII is thin and partly circular. “The הלכה is in the שלחן ערוך and is based on the מגן אברהם” reverses the chronology (a later commentary cannot be the basis of the code it comments on), and “one does not turn his back on the שלחן ערוך” reads like a mis-hearing. The paragraph on looking to the sides cites the שלחן ערוך without a סעיף.

The סיכום is an editorial prayer that restates the headings. It is not wrong, but it should be marked as a coda or dropped.

4.Factual / Citation Concerns

The header and dedication. The header gives כ״ו תשרי תשפ״ו with “27 October 2025” and a “Sunday” shiur. As far as I know, 27 October 2025 was a Monday, and כ״ו תשרי of that year fell nine days earlier; check the pair. The dedication reads “on her שניים שנים יארצייט”, which is not a phrase (“second יארצייט”?).

Note 1 (Seder HaYom). It places the author in עין זיתים, gives a first printing of Venice 1599, and connects the ten יו״דין to the ten ספירות, none of it sourced. The body says he “is, incidentally, the author of the famous יגדל אלוקים חי, which was composed only in the 1500s”; Yigdal is, as far as I know, conventionally attributed to a medieval Italian author of around 1300. The claim should be sourced or dropped. The note also calls it “widely (though not universally) attributed”, which the body does not.

Note 2 (Rav Amram). It ends “Verify exact placement against ed. Goldschmidt, Jerusalem 1971.” An unfinished instruction in a printed note. Also, the statement that the pesakim of the Geonim “reference closing personal תפילות… as רשות” is unsourced.

Note 3 (Avudraham). The body says the Avudraham reports that the prayer ended with ברוך אתה ה׳ עושה השלום; the standard closing of the blessing is, as far as I know, המברך את עמו ישראל בשלום (in most rites), with עושה השלום the closing line of the following supplication. The note’s “reserved for יחידים” and its dating of the printers (“late 15th and 16th centuries”) disagree with the body (“the 1500s”).

Note 4 (Berachos 16b–17a). “Fifteen or so אמוראים” is repeated in the body. The page lists, as far as I remember, about a dozen, and includes רבי (a tanna). Also, the heading of Section III quotes Mar as שפתי מדבר שקר; the text of the prayer reads ושפתי מדבר מרמה.

Note 5 (Vilna Gaon). Ends “Verify exact location in Biur HaGra on או״ח קכ״ג or in his Peirush al Tehillim.” The body states flatly that he brings it from the זוהר. Either locate it or hedge the body.

Note 6 (Maharsha). It cites Berachos 17a and says his reading is “widely cited in mussar and chassidic sources”; neither claim is sourced, and the reading itself should be checked in the comment.

Note 7 (Peshischa). “Founder of חסידות פשיסחא and רבי of קוצק.” As far as I know, R. Simcha Bunim led the Peshischa school after the Yid HaKadosh, and Kotzk was led by his disciple R. Menachem Mendel. The cited Sipurei Chasidim is given without a page. The note’s own phrase, “two slips of paper”, is the standard form, which the body (“the words” in each pocket) should match.

Note 8 (Kli Yakar). The note says the Kli Yakar “reads the dual analogies… as the dual identity of כלל ישראל: simultaneously a star… and a grain of sand”; the body says the Kli Yakar says it “on Vayetze”. Check that the comment on 28:14 in fact pairs dust with stars.

Note 9 (four phrases). The heading says the Beis Yosef cites a מדרש “associated in some sources with רבי עקיבא and רבי אליעזר”; the verse list (“ס׳:ז׳, ק״ח:ז׳…”) gives no book name before the first two and is not parallel to the four phrases (קדושתך and תורתך have none). It also says the phrases appear “in many סדורי אשכנז as a רשות”, again unsourced.

Notes 10–13 (Chasam Sofer; the rainbow; the gates). Note 10 gives no printed location for the קשת״י teaching (“Toras Moshe” is named but no page). Note 12 quotes the Gemara with שערי דמעות (plural); as far as I know the text reads שערי דמעה. Note 13 calls Eichah 3:44 “the רש״י-cited proof-text for the principle in Berachos 32b”; the Gemara’s own proof, as far as I remember, is a different verse in the same chapter.

Notes 14–17 (Yoma; Taanis; the mnemonic; Nebuchadnezzar). Note 14 says “the precise number ‘three’ derives from the נבוכדנצר source (see fn 17 below)”, which is the contradiction noted in Section 3 (and “fn 17” should read “note 17”). Note 15 cites “תענית ב׳ ע״א – ה׳ ע״ב and תענית כ״ז” for the מעמדות: a three-daf range that is not the passage (the Mishnah in the fourth chapter; the discussion near 27a). Note 16 lists six cases with dapim but puts הלכה כאביי in its heading while the body says the הלכה is like רבה, and ends with a “Note” contradicting its own body. Note 17 is the most worrying: the Hebrew “quotation” has no source, says “three steps” and “three kings”, and sits beside a body that says the steps were backward.

Notes 18–19 (Abuhav; Shabbos 88b). The body says the expulsion order “was lifted in 1493”, which, as far as I know, is not so; it also says Abarbanel was “treasurer of Spain”, which overstates a financier’s role. Note 18 says Abuhav “led a delegation” to Portugal; the body says he “fled” and “met with” the king. The body writes שבת ח״ח twice and note 19 שבת פ״ח ע״ב; the heading has “Hr Sinai”; the body has “a sופר”. Note 19’s Hebrew quotation (“חזרה נשמתן בטל”) reads as a corruption, and its reference to רש״י on שמות י״ט:י״ז should be checked against the verse where the retreat is mentioned. The reading of “three מילין” is attributed to the Beis Yosef “in the name of המהרי״א אבוהב” without the quoting words.

Note 20 (Magen Avraham). See Section 3. The note also asserts that the שולחן ערוך “details the order of the three steps, the בקשת שלום, and the prohibition of returning to one’s place before the חזרת הש״ץ”; the last item should be located or dropped.

Dates and spans that are consistent. Maharsha (1555–1631), Kli Yakar (1550–1619), Chasam Sofer (1762–1839), R. Simcha Bunim (1765–1827) and the Magen Avraham (1635–1682) are consistent with what I know.

5.Small Polish Suggestions

  • Hebrew density. Almost every noun is a Hebrew inline span, so the English spine is hard to read aloud; adopt the series convention (transliteration first, Hebrew in parentheses on first use).
  • Spelling drift. אלוקי נצור / אלהי נצור; “Rebbi” / “Rabbi” / רבי; “Nevuchadnezzar” and נבוכדנצר; the Novardok/Slabodka spellings in mixed script (נאוואַרדאָק).
  • Time cues. “Last week we discussed”, “a few weeks ago”, “this week”, “He took the farher”: replace with cross-references or drop.
  • The section headings are uneven (“The Two Pockets” versus “XII. כבוד שכינה and the ארון קודש”); and Section V and Section VI could be merged.
  • The rav callout (Section II) sits in the middle of the history; move it after Section III.
  • “The Story of R. Chaim” callout names the parties “Reb Yitzchak, Reb Moshe”; if the names are not in the source, delete them.
  • Footer and production note. “Hebrew terminology preserved in Hebrew script” is a production note, not colophon text.
  • The title block carries a Hebrew phrase in the kicker (“Sunday Morning שיעור on תפילה”); fine, but the day should be right.

6.Bottom Line

A strong, readable chapter with a clear spine and four unforgettable images (the ten יו״דין, the dust that finally treads on us, Rava on the bloodied threshold, Nebuchadnezzar’s steps). It needs six fixes before print: resolve the Yoma contradiction and the three-versus-four question in the Nebuchadnezzar story; quote the verse in full; settle Rava versus Rabbah; recast the two composed Hebrew “quotations” as reported speech or supply their sources; fix the logic of the gates of tears and the Magen Avraham chronology; and remove the live-shiur residue, the unfinished “Verify” instructions and the composed closing prayer. With these, it can stand as a short, sharp chapter in the tefillah series.
