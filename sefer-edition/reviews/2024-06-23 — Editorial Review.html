23 June 2024 — הַקְטֹרֶת — History, Mystery, and Impact

Independent review · read fresh, no exposure to the writing process · 10 August 2026

1.Overall Read

This is a substantial, well-organized piece of Torah writing that aspires toward sefer-style scholarship and, at its best, earns that aspiration. The structure is logical — opening anecdote, korbanos background, Zohar history and controversy, traditional resolution, the ketores teaching, the siddur history, and the closing three pesukim — and the dual-layer footnote system is a genuine structural achievement: it allows the body text to breathe while giving the learned reader full citation apparatus in parallel. The prose is generally dignified and registers correctly for a document of this kind. At roughly 3,500 words of body text plus fifteen footnotes, it is long, but the subject matter justifies the length.

The piece is strongest in its historical-narrative sections (Sections IV–V on the Zohar controversy) and weakest in its resolution (Section VI) and its closing (Section X). One significant factual error — the attribution to “Emperor Franz Joseph” of a prohibition that predates his reign by decades — needs correction. One digression (the “Hakadosh aside” in Section VIII) interrupts the narrative at its most critical moment. These are correctable problems in an otherwise accomplished document.

2.What Works

  • The Zohar controversy narrative (Sections IV–V) is the strongest sustained writing in the document. The progression from R' Moshe de León's discovery through R' Yitzchak of Akko's quest, the widow's testimony, the later reversal, the Yaavetz, and Fleckeles is a well-shaped arc that manages to be both historically precise and genuinely suspenseful. The reader is pulled forward without feeling rushed.
  • The dual-layer footnote system is excellent. Short citation first (for quick lookup), then expanded elaboration for the scholar who wants more. The academic-flag on footnote 5 (R' Yitzchak of Akko / Sefer Yuchasin) is exactly the right way to handle a source whose exact daf/siman cannot be pinned: it preserves transparency without cluttering the body text. This system should be preserved and applied consistently to future shiurim in the series.
  • The Shelah HaKadosh passage (Section VIII) is well-dramatized. The Parshas Vayetzei arrival story, the naming of Shaar HaShamayim, and the last-will-and-testament pivot are told with the right degree of biographical concision. The detail “don't forget to add the ketores” is memorable.
  • The battle-cry Radak lands cleanly. After several sections of historical argumentation, this note lands with the force of a punchline: the verse you say every morning after the ketores is the same verse your ancestors shouted running toward the enemy. It is the most effective moment of surprise in the document.
  • The opening Rav Wolfson anecdote is gracefully handled. It introduces the series context, dedicates the piece, and makes a theological argument (here is what genuine davening looks like) that no abstract framing could match.

3.Where It's Weak or Could Be Sharper

The “Hakadosh aside” (Section VIII, subsection “An Aside — The Title Hakadosh”) must be cut or repositioned. It interrupts the liturgical-history flow at its most critical moment — the Shelah has just been described completing his last will, and the document is building toward the institutional impact of Shaar HaShamayim — to deliver a somewhat curmudgeonly observation about modern overuse of the title heilig. The observation is charming in a shiur context but deadly in a written document. It breaks the reader's momentum and lands on a minor complaint. Either cut it entirely or move it to a sidebar box so it can be skipped without disrupting the flow.

Section VI (“Reconciling Authenticity with Tradition”) does not resolve the controversy; it stops pursuing it. The Vilna Gaon subsection ends on a note of irresolution (“we are left with the Zohar as a whole, and we accept it in its entirety”) that is theologically honest but structurally anticlimactic. There is no affirmative move — no positive argument for the Zohar beyond “the Arizal authenticated it.” A reader who arrived with genuine intellectual doubt leaves with the same doubt. The piece would be stronger if it acknowledged this gap directly and offered at least one substantive positive argument (e.g., the internal consistency and profundity of the Zohar's content, the unanimous reception by gedolei Yisrael since the sixteenth century) rather than simply declaring the mesorah side won.

The R' Yonason Eibeschitz ruling arrives at the wrong moment. The statement that one who disbelieves in the Zohar's authenticity is “not a koifer” appears in the middle of Section VI, just after the document has argued for traditional acceptance. As placed, it reads as a concession that undercuts the paragraph that just preceded it. It belongs either before the controversy section (as a throat-clearing note on the stakes) or in its own footnote.

Section X (“Closing”) is generic. The final two paragraphs restate the thesis without adding anything. A closing that returns to Rav Wolfson — completing the bracket opened in Section I, perhaps observing that the ketores's unique status illuminates something about the kind of prayer Rav Wolfson embodied — would be far more satisfying structurally and emotionally.

4.Factual / Citation Concerns

The “Emperor Franz Joseph” attribution is chronologically impossible and must be corrected. R' Elazar Fleckeles (1754–1826) is described as expressing gratitude to “Emperor Franz Joseph of the Austro-Hungarian Empire” for prohibiting the Zohar's printing. Franz Joseph I reigned 1848–1916 — more than two decades after Fleckeles' death. The Austro-Hungarian Empire itself was formally constituted only in 1867. Fleckeles' contemporary ruler was Emperor Francis I of Austria (also known as Francis II, Holy Roman Emperor; reigned as Emperor of Austria 1804–1835). The text should read “Emperor Francis I of Austria” or, in German-Jewish register, “Kaiser Franz I.”

Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi described as a “talmid of Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai” (Section IV). This is a simplification. Rabi was a generation later; he learned primarily from R' Yehudah bar Ilai, R' Shimon, R' Yose, and others of that generation, but calling him specifically the talmid of Rashbi is reductive. The correct formulation is that Rabi compiled the Mishnah from the traditions of Rashbi's generation, including Rashbi's own students. A brief qualifying phrase (“who learned from Rashbi's generation, including Rashbi's own students”) would suffice.

Footnote 1 (Mishnah Berurah 59:10) hedges in an unusual way — “the precise siman/se'if katan in the standard Vilna edition of MB on this halacha is best verified against a printed copy.” This kind of hedge belongs in a working note, not in a mareh mekomos. Either confirm the reference or cite more broadly (“Mishnah Berurah, siman 50–60, laws of korbanos before davening”).

The Chasam Sofer quip (fn12) is “widely cited in poskim and drashos rather than from a single fixed siman.” This is accurate but should be flagged in the body text with “in the tradition attributed to the Chasam Sofer” rather than “the Chasam Sofer famously commented,” which implies a pinned textual source.

5.Small Polish Suggestions

  • The phrase “by the way” appears three times in the body text (twice in the ketores section, once in the korbanos section). This is a colloquial tic that jars against the otherwise elevated prose register. Each instance should be cut or rephrased: “It is worth noting” or simply restructuring the sentence.
  • Section VIII has two headers that overlap: “The Ketores Enters the Siddur — The Shelah HaKadosh” and then immediately “Rabbeinu Yeshaya HaLevi Horowitz” as a sub-header. Since Rabbeinu Yeshaya is the Shelah HaKadosh, this doubling is redundant. Fold the inner header into the outer or drop it.
  • In Section VIII, “Czechoslovakia” is anachronistic for describing seventeenth-century Prague — the region was then Bohemia, part of the Habsburg Empire. “Bohemia” or simply “Prague” is more accurate.
  • The Zohar passage in Section VII is given in Aramaic with translation. The translation is accurate, but the blockquote ends without a source line (just the attributed speaker “Rabbi Shimon said”). Adding “Zohar, Parshas Vayakhel” as a tag under the blockquote would complete the citation cleanly.
  • The phrase “it became one of the most popular svarim even today” in Section V contains a Yiddish phonetic spelling (“svarim” for sefarim). This is the only such phonetic Yiddish in the document and should be standardized to the Hebrew transliteration used elsewhere: sefarim.

6.Bottom Line

An accomplished piece of Torah writing that is strongest in its historical-narrative sections and weakest in its theological resolution; the “Emperor Franz Joseph” error is a factual mistake that must be corrected before the document goes anywhere, the “Hakadosh aside” breaks narrative flow at a critical point and should be cut, and the generic closing needs to be replaced with a bracket back to the Rav Wolfson dedication — fix those three structural problems and this is a strong piece of sefer-quality Torah writing.
