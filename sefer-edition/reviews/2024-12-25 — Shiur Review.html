Content-based review · Erev Shabbos Mikeitz / Chanukah 5785 · reviewer read the reworked write-up in full

1.The Shiur in One Paragraph

A Chanukah shiur built around a single deep kushya: how did a family of Kohanim ascend a throne that Yaakov Avinu's blessing (lo yasur shevet mi'Yehudah) had reserved for the descendants of Yehudah? The Rav takes the long way in — roughly two-thirds of the shiur is the historical arc from Alexander Mokdon and Aristotle through the Misyavnim, the Seleucid reversal under Antiochus, the corruption of the Kehunah Gedolah, the Jason / Menelaus civil war, and the rebellion of Mattisyahu and his sons — before turning to the halachic-hashkafic core. There he lays out the foundational machloikes between the Ramban on Vayechi (the Chashmonaim's seizure of the throne was a cheit, punished by Hordus wiping out the line) and Rav Yaakov Kamenetsky in Emes L'Yaakov (it was a hora'as sha'ah, and the chachamim's silence is explained by their reading of Bayis Sheni as an interim tekufah). The shiur closes with the structural parallel of Rambam vs. Ramban on yishuv Eretz Yisrael (tzivui or havtachah), and a coda on why there is no Maseches Chanukah — running through the Chasam Sofer (which the Rav openly dislikes), the Vilna Gaon (a lost maseches), and the Sefas Emes (Chanukah as pure Torah she'b'al peh).

2.What Was Strongest

  • The Rambam-as-historian framing. The observation that the Rambam opens Hilchos Chanukah with straight divrei ha'yamim — something he does in essentially no other halachah — is the load-bearing move of the shiur. It licenses the whole historical arc that follows and makes the halachic kushya feel earned rather than parachuted in. The pairing with the Rambam's decision to close the Yad with Hilchos Melachim quietly establishes that malchus is a full parshah of halachos, not a rhetorical flourish.
  • The Rav Yaakov Kamenetsky yesod. The idea that Chazal deliberately withheld malchus in Bayis Sheni because they feared Klal Yisrael would mistake the moment for the geulah ha'sheleimah — and that the massive explosion of gezeiros, takanos, and siyagim in this tekufah was preparatory fortification for the coming 2,000-year galus — is a genuinely illuminating reading. It ties together three otherwise-loose facts (no king, tons of takanos, the Sanhedrin's silence) with a single explanatory arc. The Rav's characterization of Emes L'Yaakov as “one of the best and somewhat underappreciated sefarim of recent decades” is the kind of aside that sends people to a bookshelf.
  • The Ramban's midah k'neged midah. Anchoring the Ramban's claim to the Bava Basra 3b sugya of Hordus — and noting the daf yomi confluence — turns an abstract theological claim (“you take what is not yours, you lose what you have”) into a concrete, dateable historical judgment. The Chashmona'is on the rooftop declaring that any future claimant must be presumed an eved is the perfect closing image for the Ramban's reading.
  • The Yochanan Kohen Gadol irony. The observation that Mattisyahu's father was the very Yochanan Kohen Gadol of Berachos 29a — who served eighty years and turned Tzeduki at the end — is a superb piece of biographical framing. It transforms al ta'amin b'atzmecha ad yom mos'cha from a maxim into a family drama and makes the tzaddikim kedoshim u'tehorim of Al Ha'Nissim land with much more weight.
  • The Maccabi etymology. Standing firm on Mattisyahu Ben Yochanan Kohen as the pshat — and refusing to concede to the popular Mi Kamocha Ba'eilim Hashem reading even under pushback from the tzibbur — is a small but characteristic moment of scholarly integrity. It also models the difference between what is nice and what is pshat.
  • The tzivui vs. havtachah parallel to yishuv Eretz Yisrael. Recognizing that the same Rambam-Ramban dispute recurs across multiple parshiyos is exactly the kind of structural observation that lifts a shiur out of the local sugya. It gives the audience a portable framework.

3.What Was Weaker or Worth Pushing Back On

The historical prologue crowds out the halachic core. The shiur spends roughly two-thirds of its time on Alexander, Aristotle, the Misyavnim, the Jason / Menelaus civil war, and the battles — all vivid and well-told — before it even reaches the actual kushya. When we finally arrive at Ramban vs. Rav Yaakov, the treatment is compressed. The Ramban's lashon is paraphrased rather than quoted; Rav Yaakov's yesod is stated but not tested against obvious counterexamples (see below). Given that the shiur's title is a halachic-hashkafic question, one would want the ratio inverted, or at least evened out.

The Rambam's position is under-argued. The Rav asserts that the Rambam in Hilchos Chanukah praises the Chashmonaim for taking the throne (chazakim, gevurim, chazrah malchus le'Yisrael), and reads him as holding that lo yasur is a havtachah rather than a tzivui. But the Rambam nowhere explicitly says any of this — the reading is inferred from tone and from the parallel to yishuv Eretz Yisrael. A shiur that hangs this much on the Rambam owes at least a moment inside the lashon: does “v'chazrah malchus le'Yisrael yoseir al masayim shanah” actually praise, or merely narrate?

Shaul HaMelech and Zerubavel are handled too quickly. Both were raised from the floor and both are genuinely hard for the Ramban's reading. The Rav's response — Shaul preceded the selection of Beis David, Zerubavel was a hora'as sha'ah — effectively concedes the Rambam's havtachah reading in order to defend the Ramban's tzivui reading. If hora'as sha'ah from another shevet is halachically available for Zerubavel, it is not obvious why the same category cannot rescue the Chashmonaim, which is precisely the Rav Yaakov position. The two answers are in tension, and the tension is not resolved.

The “why no navi → no melech” piece is flagged and dropped. The Rav notes that a navi is normally required to appoint a king, and that this is a davar ha'shanu'i b'machlokes among Rishonim. Given that the whole shiur is about whether the Chashmonaim's malchus was halachically valid, the absence of a navi at the time is a first-order objection that deserves at least one line of engagement, not a promise to return.

The Rav's dislike of the Chasam Sofer tradition is stated but not argued. “I don't like it” and “are you serious?!” are honest reactions, but the substantive case — that a Chut Ha'Meshulash attribution should be discounted because the Chasam Sofer himself never wrote it — is a real methodological point that deserves to be stated as such. The reframing offered by others (l'sheim shamayim, b'cheshbon rather than personal grievance) also isn't given a fair hearing before the Rav moves to the Gaon.

4.Halachic / Lomdish Observations

The mechanics of hora'as sha'ah for malchus. Rav Yaakov's move — that the Chashmonaim should have declared their reign moradi / hora'as sha'ah — presupposes that there is a halachic category of temporary kingship distinct from full malchus. This is not a small claim. Does a hora'as sha'ah melech require meshichah? Does he acquire the halachos of Melech Yisrael for moredin, for bidud kavod, for arba'ah amos? Does he transmit to his son? The shiur uses the phrase but does not carve out its dinim, and the whole defense of the Chashmonaim depends on it.

The Rambam's placement of history at the head of a halachah. This is a real chakirah in the Yad: is the historical introduction to Hilchos Chanukah a halachah itself (i.e., knowing the nes is part of pirsumei nisa), or is it hakdamah external to the halachic content? The Rav uses it evidentially — the Rambam's decision to write it proves that the events are chashuv — but doesn't ask whether it functions as halachah.

The kehunah/malchus separation as an independent problem. Even granting the Rav Yaakov hora'as sha'ah, there is a distinct issue: a Kohen serving as melech combines two kesarim that the Torah keeps separate. The Ramban hints at this; the shiur does not draw it out. The question is not only from which shevet the king may come, but whether a serving Kohen can occupy the throne at all — independent of lo yasur.

The Shimon caveat deserves its own sugya. The Ramban's three-word aside that Shimon ben Mattisyahu had Tzeduki influence, and that this is why the Chashmonai line specifically deteriorated, opens a real question: is the Ramban saying the malchus itself was cheit, or that a specific line-level cheit in Shimon triggered the hordosian punishment? These are two very different readings with different implications for the other brothers' descendants.

The Sefas Emes's claim as a halachic proposition. If Chanukah is essentially Torah she'b'al peh, then the absence of a written maseches is not a historical accident but a formal siman of the yom tov's chelek in Torah. Does this cash out anywhere in halachah — e.g., in a preference for oral transmission of Chanukah chiddushim, or in the status of Chanukah drashos? The chiddush is beautiful; its halachic footprint is not explored.

5.Follow-up Questions Worth Asking the Rav

Q1.If Rav Yaakov's yesod is that Chazal feared Klal Yisrael would mistake Bayis Sheni for the geulah ha'sheleimah, why didn't they issue an explicit mecha'ah against the Chashmonaim's coronation for exactly that reason? Their silence is what Emes L'Yaakov is trying to explain, but if the concern was so pressing, silence seems like a strange strategy.
Q2.How does the Rav reconcile the two answers on Shaul and Zerubavel? If hora'as sha'ah from another shevet is admissible for Zerubavel after Beis David's selection, why does the same category not fully absolve the Chashmonaim in the Ramban's reading — and, correspondingly, what daylight remains between the Ramban and the Rambam once we grant hora'as sha'ah?
Q3.Is there a distinct halachic problem in a serving Kohen occupying the throne (combining keser kehunah and keser malchus), independent of the Yehudah question? If so, does the Ramban hold both objections, or is the Yehudah issue his sole complaint?
Q4.The Ramban's three-word aside about Shimon's Tzeduki influence: does the Rav read this as (a) a supplementary explanation for why the hordosian punishment fell so completely, or (b) the actual ikar of the cheit — with lo yasur as background rather than trigger? The two readings would change how we relate to the other sons' descendants.
Q5.The shiur flagged the machloikes between the Al Ha'Nissim version (battles and victory) and the Gemara's version (the pach shemen) for a future shiur. Independently of the resolution: is the Rambam's decision to lead Hilchos Chanukah with the military narrative rather than the menorah a positional statement in that machloikes?
Q6.Would the Rav place a hora'as sha'ah malchus from another shevet under any of the standard halachos of Melech Yisrael — moredin, bidud kavod, transmission to a son, the parshah of ha'melech? If yes, on what basis; if no, in what sense is it malchus at all rather than nesius renamed?

6.Bottom Line

A rich, well-paced Chanukah shiur that succeeds most as history and as the framing of a great kushya, and less as the resolution of one. The historical narrative — Alexander and Aristotle, the corruption of the Kehunah Gedolah, Jason and Menelaus, Antiochus and the pig, Mattisyahu at the altar — is delivered with the Rav's usual control and pays real dividends: by the time the Ramban is quoted, the Chashmonaim's malchus feels like a live event rather than a theoretical case. The two halachic pillars — the Ramban's cheit and Rav Yaakov's hora'as sha'ah — are cleanly stated, and the tzivui-vs.-havtachah framework travels beautifully to yishuv Eretz Yisrael. The weakness is that the halachic half of the shiur runs on rails once it starts — the Shaul/Zerubavel objections concede more than the Rav admits, the mechanics of a hora'as sha'ah malchus stay opaque, and the Rambam's position is inferred rather than argued from the lashon. A learned talmid chochom leaves knowing the story cold and holding a beautiful tzivui/havtachah framework; the halachic disposition of the Chashmonaim's throne remains, appropriately for a first pass, unfinished business.
