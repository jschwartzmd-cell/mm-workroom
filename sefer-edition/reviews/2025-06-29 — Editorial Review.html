Mareh Mekamos · Independent Editorial Review

29 June 2025 — בִּרכַת הַמִינִים and בִּרכַת עַל הַצַּדִּיקִים

Independent editorial review · writing-quality assessment, independent of transcript fidelity (handled in the companion Shiur Review) · 5 October 2026

1.Overall Read

A substantive, well-populated fourteen-section piece organized as a two-movement walk-through: Sections I–VIII treat בִּרכַת הַמִינִים (origin, minim, censorship history, lashon, שְׁמוּאֵל הַקָטָן), Sections IX–XIV treat עַל הַצַּדִּיקִים (its six categories, the Arizal's chiddush, bitachon, and the יַעֲקב-יוֹסֵף midrash). The document is paragraph-level readable throughout and does genuine work in several places: the Nicholas Donin narrative in IV, the Mussafia-and-Scholem callout in V, the Arizal-and-Chofetz Chaim twinning in XI, and the Rav Yisrael Salanter–Reb Naftali Amsterdam callout in X.6 are each memorable.

The document's structural weak point is footnote numbering: footnotes appear in body-order 1, 2, 17, 18, 15, 16, 3, 20, 4, 5, 7, 6, 8, 9, 25, 10, 19, 11, 21, 22, 12, 23, 24, 13, 14 — a severely scrambled sequence that will jar any sefer reader. Separately, two sections carry the identical title “The Original Moment” (III and XIII) without any disambiguation, and five of the Hebrew-callout ma'asim use nested <em> wrappers that will render inconsistently (often reverting to upright) across browsers and print engines.

2.What Works

  • Section IV (Nicholas Donin & the Paris Disputation) is the document's finest sustained narrative stretch. It moves cleanly from the fact of variant nusachaos, to רש"י's censored peirush, to Donin's biography (talmid of רבינוּ יחיאל מפָּרִיז, cherem, conversion, informing to the Pope), to the 1242 burning, to the מִינִים-to-עכ"ום substitution scheme. The “כּל עכ"ום קטניות” punchline earns its laugh and sticks the pedagogical point.
  • The Mussafia-&-Scholem callout in Section V is the document's cleverest piece of writing. The reader is handed the עֲרוּך's standard peirush, then the biographical colour, then the Sabbatean reveal via Scholem's letter — and only then handed Mussafia's striking aleph-removal chiddush. The sequence rewards patience and makes the chiddush memorable precisely because the reader has already been primed to read Mussafia skeptically.
  • Section X.6 (ve'aleinu) and the Rav Yisrael Salanter–Reb Naftali Amsterdam callout is the document's emotional peak. The sequence of three negations — not the מקוּבּל, not the Baal Shem Tov, not the חתם סוֹפֵר, but Naftali-the-gaon-Naftali-can-be — is cadenced and quotable. It also lands the peshat of “וְשִׂים חֶלְקֵנוּ עִמָּהֶם” naturally.
  • Section XI's twinning of the Arizal and the Chofetz Chaim is a graceful editorial move. The children-in-cheder parenthetical (“now I have all your מִצְוֹת!”) is exactly the kind of concrete, warm detail that keeps the kabbalistic chiddush from floating free. The two-types-of-tzaddikim framing in bullet form (claimants vs. trustees) is one of the clearest expositions of a שַׁעַר הַכַּוָּנוֹת teaching in the series.
  • The dual-tier Mareh Mekomos apparatus continues to carry serious weight. The academic-flag notes (fn16 Scholem, fn17 Donin, fn20 Frierdiker Rebbe, fn21 Rav Yisrael Salanter, fn25 Yesod HaAvodah) are the right register — dense, well-flagged, encyclopedic without crowding the body.
  • The oivim/zedim distinction at the close of Section VII is cleanly bulleted and the “anyone in עַזָּה actively seeking to kill a יְֹהוּדִי” application makes the chiluk concrete without sermonizing.

3.Where It's Weak or Could Be Sharper

Section III and Section XIII both carry the title “The Original Moment” — III for בִּרכַת הַמִינִים's origin at קְרִיעַת יַם סוּף, XIII for עַל הַצַּדִּיקִים's origin at יַעֲקב's deathbed. A reader scanning the mareh mekomos apparatus or returning to the piece will find the duplicate headers disorienting. Disambiguate — e.g., “The Origin of the Bracha — Kri'as Yam Suf” (III) and “The Origin of the Bracha — Yaakov and Yosef” (XIII).

The jump from Section III to Section IV is chronologically backwards. III closes with the mal'achim's original utterance and שְׁמוּאֵל הַקָטָן's formalization; IV then opens with centuries-later Christian censorship. The reader is yanked from yetzias Mitzrayim to thirteenth-century France without a transitional sentence. A one-line bridge (“Once the bracha was fixed, its history under Christian rule was anything but fixed —”) would smooth the gear-shift.

The Reb Faivy callout (Section II) breaks the fourth wall oddly. It opens with the editor's voice (“Looking into this — Reb Faivy is correct”), reports the research result, then adjudicates (“This does not affect the תַּקָּנָה”). The result is that the Rav's own voice disappears mid-section. For a sefer, either move the entire callout to a footnote on the trinity sentence, or reframe as “The Rav acknowledged Reb Faivy's point and noted…” so that the main-text voice remains the Rav's throughout.

The Rav Kalish tribute in Section VI eats its own sugya. The מַעֲשֶׂה from עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה כ"ז ע"ב is announced in one sentence and the entire section-callout then shifts to a biographical appreciation of Rav Kalish. The reader is left not knowing what the מַעֲשֶׂה actually says. Either expand the sugya (what the young man did, how רַבִּי אלְעָזָר or whichever Amora responded, what the halachah is on accepting healing from a min) or compress the Rav Kalish aside to a footnote and let Section VI do the Avodah Zarah work it promises in its header.

The lead paragraph runs to roughly 230 words and rehearses the entire arc before the reader has begun. Even by the series' standards this is long. For sefer edition, cut to a 50-word abstract or replace with a bulleted sectional table of contents.

The “Mamdani” and “Ayatollah” references in Section VII — “the כַּוָּנָה in our day … should reach toward the Ayatollah, toward Mamdani” — are vivid in shiur register and will date badly in sefer form. By the time a reader opens the printed edition five years on, “Mamdani” may need a footnote; by ten years, the name will be opaque. Consider generalizing (“toward the particular רָשָׁע whose name is in that morning's news”) and footnoting the two 2025-era examples the Rav offered.

Section XIV (“Closing”) is two short sentences and a שָׁבוּעַ טוֹב! After eleven substantive sections it reads as a cold hand-off rather than a closing. Given the extraordinary weight of Section XIII's יַעֲקב-יוֹסֵף midrash, the piece deserves a closing paragraph that returns to one of the document's threads (the Shmuel HaKatan paradox from VIII, the Arizal's trust-held מִצְוֹת from XI, or the bitachon of XII).

4.Structural / Internal-Consistency Issues

Footnote numbering is severely scrambled. The reader encounters footnotes in body-order 1, 2, 17, 18, 15, 16, 3, 20, 4, 5, 7, 6, 8, 9, 25, 10, 19, 11, 21, 22, 12, 23, 24, 13, 14 — i.e., fn17 and fn18 appear second and third, fn25 appears tenth, and fn14 is the last reference despite its low number. In a transcript this is unremarkable; in a printed sefer this will be the first thing a careful reader notices. Renumber all twenty-five footnotes in body-order before print.

Section IX cites fn7 before fn6, within a single paragraph: “a פָּסוּק in תְּהִלִּים[fn7] … mirrors that פָּסוּק[fn6].” In addition to the global renumbering, verify that the two notes do not duplicate content (fn5 and fn6 both cite the Megillah sugya; fn7 is the Tehillim pasuk). Consider consolidating fn5 and fn6 into a single note, and ensure fn7 is placed in body-sequence after it.

Two sections titled “The Original Moment” (III, XIII) with no disambiguation. See §3 above.

The “Rebbe” vs. “the Rav” vs. “Rabbi Ephrati”. The header reads “Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati”; the body consistently refers to the maggid shiur as “the Rebbe” (e.g., Section VI callout, Section VII, Section X.3, Section XI). The two registers do not cohabit well. Pick one (“the Rav” is series-standard) and apply throughout. The companion Shiur Review additionally flags that the attribution to Rabbi Ephrati itself may require verification.

Nested <em> tags inside callouts. The Frierdiker Rebbe callout (Section VII), the Rav Yitzchak Charif callout (Section X.3), the Rav Yisrael Salanter callout (Section X.6), and portions of the Mussafia callout (Section V) wrap the entire paragraph in <em> and then wrap internal quotations in a second <em>. The expected result — roman inside italic as a “flip” emphasis — is browser-dependent; some engines render the nested <em> as doubled-italic (no visual change), others flip. In print this will be unpredictable. Replace the inner <em> with a non-italic span, or (cleaner) remove the outer <em> wrapper and rely on the callout box's own styling to indicate the aside.

The Mussafia callout nests a parenthetical inside a parenthetical. “(To call him ‘רַב’ is technically an exaggeration.)” sits inside an already-italicized editorial-aside callout. The parenthetical reads as a stage-whisper inside a stage-whisper. Either hoist to main text with a signpost (“Note that calling him רַב…”) or move to a footnote on Mussafia.

Hebrew-vowelization inconsistency in Section VII. The six verbs — תֵעָקֵר, תִשָּׁבֵר, תְּכַלֶּה, תַשְׁפִּילֵם, תַכְנִיעֵם, תְּמַגֵּר — carry full nikud; nothing else in the document does. Either vowelize the לְשׁוֹנוֹת consistently throughout the document (recommended for pesukim and quoted tefillah phrases) or drop nikud on these six verbs. As it stands they stick out.

Title transliteration is inconsistent with body usage. The <title> tag and <h1> both render “Birkat HaMinim” and “Birkat Al HaTzaddikim” (Sephardic). The body uses Ashkenazic throughout (“בִּרכַת” where it transliterates would be Birkas). The lead paragraph also uses “Birkat Al HaTzaddikim.” Standardize to the series' Birkas register.

Section X.3 reference to Rav Aharon Schechter uses “שׁליט"א” while the preceding מָרָן clause carries full honorifics (“מָרָן הַגָּאוֹן רַבִּי משֶׁה פְַינְשׁטֵין זצ"ל”). Standardize the honorific pattern across the two names in a single sentence.

5.Small Polish Suggestions

  • “Rav Natan asks” (Section X.6) is unidentified. In context the referent is almost certainly Rav Natan of Breslov in Likutei Halachos; add a footnote giving the identification so the reader is not left guessing between Rav Natan of Breslov, Rav Natan Adler, or another possibility.
  • “Rav Kalish זצ"ל” in Section VI is first-named without any identification. Readers outside the Rav's immediate community will not know who this is. A one-sentence footnote (which generation, which kehillah) would make the tribute reach beyond the room it was first delivered in.
  • “Judeo-Christians” (Section II) is a modern academic term that sits oddly next to the Rishonim-register prose around it. Consider “Christian-adjacent Yidden” or simply “מִינִים within our own community” (already used two sentences later).
  • Section III: “The מדרש gives every בְּרָכָה … a moment when the מַלְאֲכֵי הַשָּׁרֵת first uttered it.” This claim is cited to no source and is a strong general statement (that every one of the eighteen/nineteen brachos has its own first-utterance midrash). Either source it in a footnote or soften (“a מַסוֹרֶת ties each בְּרָכָה to a מַלאֲכִים-first-utterance moment”).
  • Section IX: “The בְּרָכָה begins with a וְ — direct continuation.” The clause arrives without a lead-in. Suggest: “Confirming the connection textually: עַל הַצַּדִּיקִים opens with a וְ — the bracha is grammatically continuous with its predecessor.”
  • Section X.5 (גֵּרֵי הַצֶּדֶק) is only three paragraphs, yet opens with a Prague-drashah teaching whose author is not named in the body text (fn is missing). The companion Shiur Review notes the source is Rav Yonasan Eibeschutz's Yaaros Devash. Naming the source in body or footnote would significantly strengthen the section.
  • Section X.4 Hirsch citation. The body attributes “פְּלֵיטָה”-as-survivors to Rav Shamshon Raphael Hirsch; the companion Shiur Review flags that the Rav's actual citation in the transcript is Rav Shimon Schwab. Beyond the attribution-fidelity question (which the Shiur Review handles), the editorial point is that Hirsch's Siddur is a well-known reference that will be checked by scholarly readers; a wrong citation here is more exposed than most.
  • “אלְטֶר אִיד” (Section X.4 callout) is Yiddish without translation. Add “(an elderly Yid)” parenthetical or italicize with a one-time gloss.
  • Section X.4 callout about the Jerusalem cheder is powerful but delicate — it describes a Holocaust-survivor rebbi who hit children, and reports that only three of twenty-seven or twenty-eight boys remained פְּרוּם. The passing “Years later there was a full court case opened over the beatings” is a serious historical claim that will need either a source or a looser phrasing (“a case was reportedly opened”) in sefer form, since it is unsourced and identifiable.
  • “They should take their medication or grab another cup of coffee before they enter the classroom” (Section X.4 callout, final paragraph) is shiur-register humor that reads differently in print. Consider recasting (“they should be well-rested and composed”) or moving the sentence into a lighter aside.
  • The רַבִּי יִצְחָק חָריף callout in Section X.3 is a sixty-plus-second ma'aseh that interrupts an already-brief section (זִקְנֵי שְׁאֵרִית) and arrives by free association (זָקֵן → rebbi → the Yesod HaAvodah's rebbi moment). The ma'aseh is wonderful but it belongs in a section on chassidus, not inside the זִקְנֵי שְׁאֵרִית clause. Consider moving to its own appendix section or converting to an extended footnote on fn25.
  • Section VIII (שְׁמוּאֵל הַקָטָן) is one short paragraph. Beautiful but under-weighted. Consider absorbing the hespedim material noted in the companion Shiur Review (“a king dies; he leaves his crown for the next generation. But שְׁמוּאֵל הַקָטָן… חֲמוּדוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם וְהָלַך לוֹ”) to give the section the weight the tanna's legacy deserves.
  • “Reb Faivy” and “the Rebbe's younger brother” are both left unnamed; this is standard practice for the series and fine as a choice, but once in the piece worth a note (“Names of participants in the shiur retained in the shiur register; others anonymized”) in the colophon or a one-time footnote.
  • “פְְרוּם” / “פְְרום” appears several times in Section X.4 callout, sometimes with double sheva (a possible typo), sometimes without. Standardize spelling.
  • The six-verb list in Section VII is given as a comma-string. Consider a vertical list — six Hebrew verbs on six lines, each with a one-word English gloss — so the Frierdiker Rebbe's Q&A that follows reads more cleanly. Would also allow the gloss for תְּמַגֵּר (“to fragment, to crumble”) to land in the list rather than in a trailing “For the record” sentence after the dialogue.

6.Bottom Line

A substantive, materially rich fourteen-section piece with four genuine peaks — the Nicholas Donin narrative (IV), the Mussafia-Scholem callout (V), the Arizal-Chofetz Chaim twinning (XI), and the Rav Yisrael Salanter–Reb Naftali Amsterdam callout (X.6). The document's main editorial issues are structural rather than prose-level: footnote numbering is severely scrambled (body-order 1, 2, 17, 18, 15, 16, 3, 20… and fn7 appears before fn6 within a single Section IX paragraph), two sections share the identical title “The Original Moment,” and four callouts use nested <em> tags that will render inconsistently in print. Beyond that: Section VI's עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה sugya is overwhelmed by its own Rav Kalish aside; Sections III→IV need a transitional sentence; Section XIV is too thin a close for the material that precedes it; the lead paragraph is too long; and the “Mamdani / Ayatollah” and sixth-grade-rebbi-humor passages will date and read differently in sefer form than in shiur form. Fix the footnote numbering first, disambiguate the two “Original Moment” headers second, resolve the nested-em rendering third — and this is a top-tier entry in the series.
