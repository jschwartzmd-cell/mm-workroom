5 January 2025 — יְדִיד נֶפֶש — Origin and Journey of the Piyut

Independent review · read fresh, no exposure to the writing process · 23 August 2026

1.Overall Read

This is one of the more narratively ambitious pieces in the series and, on the strength of its central story, one of the most successful. The piece takes a small liturgical curiosity — why is Yedid Nefesh in our siddur, and why between Korbanos and Pesukei DeZimra? — and uses it as a doorway into a genuinely extraordinary chapter of post-Expulsion history: Diego Pires, Shlomo Molcho, David HaReuveni, the Beis Yosef's Maggid, and the encoded plea buried in a piyut most of us sing without understanding. The structural payoff in Section XII — the reveal that “בִנְך יְחִידְך” is a coded reference to the Beis Yosef — is the strongest single moment in any Mareh Mekamos piece I have read.

The piece is strongest in its biographical arc (Sections IV–XII) and in its two hidden-in-plain-sight reveals (the tzitzis in Section VII, the coded piyut in Section XII). It is weakest where the historical apparatus wobbles — one clearly wrong Portuguese king, one probably muddled midrash on the martyrdoms, one shaky Beis Yosef yahrtzeit — and where the prose register slips into casual shiur idiom that a written document cannot carry. These are correctable, and the underlying document deserves the correction.

2.What Works

  • The Diego Pires → Shlomo Molcho biographical arc (Sections IV–X) is the strongest sustained narrative in the shiur. The escalation is well-shaped: royal court, mysterious emissary, self-bris, the Sforno, the Maharit'tz circle, the tzitzis, the Pope, the gates of Rome, Yud-Gimmel Teves. Each section earns the next; the reader is genuinely pulled forward.
  • The tzitzis reveal (Section VII) is a superb structural payoff. Establishing that Molcho is not merely a colorful historical figure but is quietly present in a mitzvah every reader performs daily transforms the stakes of everything that follows. The chain of witnesses (Beis Yosef → Magen Avraham → Tosfos Yom Tov → Mishnah Berurah) is exactly the right kind of Torah-scholarly detail.
  • The coded-piyut reveal (Section XII) is the emotional and intellectual center of the piece. The move from the Maggid's threefold unfulfilled promise, to the group of mekubalim who understood they could not permit its fulfillment, to the piyut we sing without knowing what we are asking — this is exactly the kind of illumination the series exists to deliver. The JTS manuscript detail with the shift from בִנְך to עַמְך and back is the perfect kind of textual evidence.
  • The 100-years-before-Shabsai-Tzvi flag (Section VIII) is a valuable orienting note. It gives the reader a scaffold to hold the messianic atmosphere of the 1520s without collapsing it into what came a century later, and it silently addresses the question that every reader is starting to ask.
  • The dual-layer footnote system with academic-flag notes (fn18 Lehmann, fn19 JTS manuscript, fn20 Prague artifacts) continues to work well. Flagging historical-fiction, manuscript, and artifact sources as distinct source-types is exactly the right kind of transparency for sefer-quality writing.
  • The Rav Yaakov Chaim Sofer teretz (Section XIII) lands cleanly — accepting moser nefesh equals dying al kiddush Hashem — and the Sefer Chasidim proof gives it real weight.

3.Where It's Weak or Could Be Sharper

Section XIII's Rabbeinu Bachya paragraph appears to conflate two distinct martyrdom traditions. The text describes “last week's parshah, Parshas Mikeitz, the parshah that includes the early midrashim on the Asarah Harugei Malchus,” then says “the Gemara in Avodah Zarah describes how Chananyah, Mishael, and Azaryah were killed — and we recite this in our kinos on Tisha B'Av and our avodah on Yom Kippur in Eileh Ezkerah.” This tangles two different things. Eileh Ezkerah and the martyrology of Avodah Zarah 18a are about the Asarah Harugei Malchus (R' Akiva, Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, R' Chanina ben Teradyon, etc.), not Chananyah, Mishael, and Azaryah — who in the biblical narrative were saved from the furnace and not killed at all. The Rabbeinu Bachya on Mikeitz discussing Pirkei Heichalos is most naturally read as the tradition that the Asarah Harugei Malchus were substituted by an angel. Either the names need to be corrected to the Asarah Harugei Malchus throughout the paragraph, or, if the Rabbi really did speak of Chananya-Mishael-Azarya, the Eileh Ezkerah and Avodah Zarah 18a references need to be removed. As it stands the paragraph is not internally coherent.

Section XVI (“Closing”) delivers the payoff but underplays it. The final paragraph — “Next time you say Yedid Nefesh, you will have a deeper appreciation… because you are saying a tefillah for the survival of the man who gave us the Shulchan Aruch” — is the correct closing beat, but it's given only three lines. The whole piece has been building to this. It deserves a fuller landing: name the paradox one more time (a piyut composed to defeat a heavenly promise, that succeeded so well that generations of Jews now recite it without knowing what they are asking for), and let the reader sit with it.

Section XV (“The Paradox — Reuveni and Molcho”) belongs earlier or should be folded in. As a stand-alone section between the Rabbeinu Bachya paragraph and the closing, it functions as a digression that pulls the reader back to Molcho at the exact moment the piece has pivoted to the piyut's journey into the siddur (XIV) and needs to build toward the close. The Reuveni/Molcho contrast is a real insight but would land better as a paragraph inside Section X (right after the Yud-Gimmel Teves death) or as a footnote — not as its own late-stage section that interrupts the trajectory.

The blockquote structure for Molcho's last words (Section X) is broken. The English translation appears inside a <div class="trans"> with no corresponding Hebrew original above it — the “trans” class exists specifically to be paired with a <div class="heb">. Either supply the Hebrew original of Molcho's reported last words (before the Shema), or restructure this as plain quoted speech without the translation-pair markup.

4.Factual / Citation Concerns

“King Manuel III” of Portugal (Section IV) does not exist in this period. Diego Pires, born in Lisbon around 1500 and rising to court secretary in his early twenties (c. 1522–1523), would have served under King João III of Portugal (reigned 1521–1557). Manuel I (“the Fortunate,” reigned 1495–1521) was João III's father and the king who forcibly converted Portuguese Jewry in 1497. There was no Manuel II until 1908 and no Manuel III at all in the Portuguese royal line. This should read “King João III of Portugal.”

The Beis Yosef's yahrtzeit and dates (Section XI) need tightening. R' Yosef Karo was niftar on Yud-Gimmel Nisan 5335 = 1575, at age 87. The text gives “around 1573–1574,” which is off by one to two years and hedges an unhedgeable date. Fix to 1575.

“The Maharit Tzitzit” (Section VII) is an unusual formulation. R' Yosef Taitazak (Salonika, d. c. 1545) is conventionally known by the acronym Maharitatz (מהרי"ט צהלון is a different figure — R' Yom Tov Tzahalon). If the intended name is Taitazak, “Maharitatz” is the standard form. If the “Tzitzit” element is meant to reflect Molcho's tzitzis story, it needs to be flagged as an in-shiur play rather than the standard title. Worth verifying against the recording.

“The Tosfos Yom Tov… traveled to Prague specifically to see them” (Section VII). R' Yom Tov Lipmann Heller (1579–1654) was av beis din of Prague for a substantial portion of his career — he lived in Prague; he did not need to travel there. If the tzitzis-viewing episode occurred during a period when he was serving elsewhere (Vienna, Nikolsburg, Krakow) and he made a return trip, the sentence should specify that. Otherwise it should read simply “examined them in Prague and confirmed the Beis Yosef's description.”

“The Alshich HaKadosh” listed as a talmid of Taitazak (Section VII). The Alshich's primary rebbi was R' Yosef Karo (the Beis Yosef himself); his connection to Taitazak, if any, was secondary. This bears verification — as stated it flattens a more complex teacher-student picture.

“David HaReuveni… killed by the Inquisition in 1538” (Section X). The commonly-cited date for HaReuveni's death at the Inquisition's auto-da-fé in Llerena is c. 1538, but sources also give 1535–1541 depending on the archive. The text's confident “1538” is defensible but a hedge (“around 1538”) would be more honest.

5.Small Polish Suggestions

  • Section IV: “in Lisbon, Portugal (Lisbon)” — the parenthetical repeats the same English word it just used. Either cut the parenthetical, or, if the intent was to give the Portuguese form, use “Lisboa.”
  • Section VI: “geo'politische” — a Yiddishized form of “geopolitical” that jars against the surrounding prose. Either use plain “geopolitical” or, if a Yiddish register is wanted, rework the whole phrase. Same section: “malchus Catolikus” is an unusual pseudo-Latin construction — “the Catholic monarchy” or “malchus ha'Catolit” would be cleaner.
  • Section X: “faced a Inquisitorial court martial” — article agreement (“an Inquisitorial”), and “court martial” is a military term. “Inquisitorial tribunal” is the correct register.
  • Section XV: “Molcho is b'kohol kadosh” — the intended phrase is presumably “b'kahal kedoshim” or “b'kehal kadosh.” The current spelling reads as a transcription artifact.
  • Lead paragraph: “post-Expulsion Sephardic historiah” — mixing an English adjective (“Sephardic”) with a Hebraized noun (“historiah”) is inconsistent. Either “Sephardic history” or “historiah Sefaradit.”
  • Section XIV heading redundancy: The h2 heading “Rav Yaakov Emden — 1730s” is immediately followed by a paragraph that opens with the same identification (“Siddur Beis Yaakov… of Rav Yaakov Emden, the Yaavetz, in the 1730s”). One or the other should be trimmed.
  • The em-tag saturation is a real problem in this piece. Sentences like “Rav Elazar and his chevra reached a terrible realization” contain three separate italic styles in eleven words. The document would read more smoothly if the em.term markup were reserved for genuinely foreign Hebrew/Yiddish terms and dropped from words that have naturalized into English liturgical vocabulary (siddur, parshah, Torah). Currently the italic weight is doing so much work that it stops signaling anything.
  • Section II: “the Maharit Tzitzit” — see factual note above; also, if retained, italicize consistently (currently sometimes bold, sometimes italic, sometimes both).

6.Bottom Line

A narratively powerful piece anchored by two exceptional structural payoffs (the tzitzis of Section VII and the coded piyut of Section XII); fix the “King Manuel III” error, untangle the Chananya-Mishael-Azarya / Asarah Harugei Malchus confusion in Section XIII, correct the Beis Yosef date to 1575, and give the closing the fuller landing this story has earned — and this will be one of the strongest pieces in the series.
