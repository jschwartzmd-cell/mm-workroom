1.The Shiur in One Paragraph

This shiur, delivered Chol HaMoed Chanukah of the Asarah b'Teves week, opens with the Shelah and Bach on the misuse of Chanukah as bein ha'zmanim, then turns to a single line of the siddur — Baruch Shem K'vod Malchuso L'Olam Va'ed — and unfolds it across nearly the entire arc of post-Talmudic hilchos Krias Shema. The Rav traces the makor through the Mishnah in Pesachim (the men of Yericho who "wrapped" the Shema) and the Gemara's story of Yaakov Avinu on his deathbed and the twelve shevatim; presents the classic mashal of the bas melech and the tzikei k'deirah; then reframes the entire question through the Tzelach's chiddush that the issue is hefsek b'Krias Shema, not the words themselves — with the Bayinaer Kloyz incident as living illustration. Against the Tzelach stands the Midrash's kinas ha'malachim, sharpened by Rav Sholom Shvadron's question about Kol Nidrei versus Motzaei Yom Kippur. The Shiltei Giborim — with a lengthy tribute to Rabi Yehoshua Boaz as the unnamed architect of the printed Shas and the original matir of sheitlach — introduces the startling psak that Baruch Shem is minhag b'almah. This is then set against the mekubalim (Nefesh HaChaim, Tanya), the Vilna Gaon's vort on the six versus four wings of the malachim, the Targum Yerushalmi's alternative version of the story, and Rav Asher Weiss's vort on Amen Yehei Shemei Rabbah as the licensed outlet for the Baruch Shem impulse. The whole is knotted back to Chanukah through k'vod malchuso.

2.What Was Strongest

  • The Tzelach's reframing. The single most clarifying move in the shiur: the problem is not the words but the hefsek. This one chiddush reorganizes the entire topic and immediately generates a real halachic map — when b'lachash, when b'kol ram, and why. The Bayinaer Kloyz story ("what are you doing?!") is precisely the sort of vivid teaching case that lets a chiddush land not just intellectually but practically.
  • The Vilna Gaon's vort on the wings. The reading of the Chagigah stirah through the six words of Baruch Shem — six wings pre-churban, four post-churban with the middle two ("k'vod malchuso") folded under — is the emotional and conceptual peak of the shiur. It gives the daily whisper an actual kavanah the listener can hold onto, and it turns the routine Galei k'vod malchuscha of Yom Tov into a specific, imageable petition. This is the payoff the shiur is built toward.
  • The extended tribute to Rabi Yehoshua Boaz. A model of restoring an anonymous giant to view. The observation that every daf of the printed Shas carries his triple contribution (Mesoras HaShas, Torah Or, Ein Mishpat) — and that his heter underwrites every sheitel in the room — is both historically substantial and homiletically rich, and the audience will not walk past the side of a daf the same way again.
  • Rav Shvadron's kavanah-not-guf vort. The Kol Nidrei-versus-Motzaei-Yom-Kippur reversal is genuinely surprising, and the resolution — the malach-state lives in the machshavah, not the body — is a piece of practical mussar the listener can carry into every Ne'ilah going forward.
  • The Yaakov-and-the-shevatim pshat. The reading of "Shema Yisrael" as literal address to Yaakov (with the honest caveat that one does not normally call one's father by name) makes the opening of Shema into a family scene with real dramatic weight, and cleanly explains why Baruch Shem is functionally Yaakov's amen to his children.

3.What Was Weaker or Worth Pushing Back On

The two competing yesodos are set up but never resolved. The shiur draws a genuinely sharp machlokes between the Tzelach (issur is hefsek-based, local to Krias Shema) and the Midrash/kinas ha'malachim yesod (issur is global, permitted only Yom Kippur). It even names the nafka mina — how one recites Baruch Shem outside the Shema. But the shiur then drifts to the next topic without telling us how the Rav himself, or the Mishnah Berurah, paskens the nafka mina in practice. Given how carefully the two shitos are laid out, letting the practical result float is a real loss.

The mekubalim-Shiltei Giborim tension is announced and then abandoned. If Nefesh HaChaim and the Baal HaTanya both treat Baruch Shem as inseparable from Krias Shema, and the Shiltei Giborim says one may skip it entirely, this is not a soft difference — it is a first-order disagreement about what kabbalas ol malchus shamayim even consists of. The shiur asks the question ("how then can the Shiltei Giborim say one may skip?") and moves on. Even a provisional resolution (nigleh vs. nistar, b'di'eved vs. l'chatchilah, the possibility that Baruch Shem is essential kabbalistically but only minhag halachically) would have closed the loop.

The "brachah l'vatalah-type dynamic" answer is offered as the Rav's own sevarah and left un-tested. The kushya on the Mishnah Berurah is a strong one — if you may skip l'chatchilah, why must you go back for lack of kavanah? — but the proposed teretz (once you have opened your mouth, the words carry their own weight) is a mechanism that the Biur Halacha explicitly does not invoke. Presenting a novel sevarah as the resolution without flagging that it is the reviewer's own is a small transparency issue in an otherwise very careful shiur.

The gematria to Chanukah is thin. The Rav flags his own reluctance ("I am not a gematria guy"), and then leans on the gematria of 1,358 without explaining how the number connects. The real Chanukah link — k'vod malchuso revealed in the victory of the Chashmonaim without rechev or susim — is thematically strong and would have carried the closing on its own. The gematria aside actually dilutes what is otherwise a very tight landing.

4.Halachic / Lomdish Observations

The minhag-vs.-takanah question deserves its own moment. The Rav notes correctly that there is a real chiluk in halachah between the two categories even where the Gemara uses the lashon "tiknu." That distinction is doing enormous work in this shiur — it is what allows the Bach to pasken that no chazarah is required. But the criteria for what elevates a widespread practice from minhag to takanah (universal acceptance? formal beis din enactment? ma'aseh rav?) are not spelled out, and the whole edifice hangs on a category-distinction the audience must take on trust.

Where the hefsek belongs is presented as a debate but not decided. The two shitos — pause between Shema and Baruch Shem versus pause between Baruch Shem and V'ahavta — carry a real nafka mina about whether Baruch Shem is part of kabbalas ol malchus shamayim. The Rav gestures at the mekubalim's side but does not say which side our siddurim or the standard minhag reflects, or what an Ashkenazi person davening from a standard Nusach Ashkenaz should actually do at that hefsek.

The Yom Kippur Katan practice needs a machmach. The observation that Coney Island Avenue and the Aguda say Baruch Shem aloud on erev Shabbos while much of Flatbush does not is exactly the kind of live minhag-data point where a follow-up sentence identifying which side traces to which posek would make the difference between local color and practical halachah.

The kavanah requirement across the two frameworks is not aligned. If the Tzelach is right and the issur b'kol ram is a Krias Shema-local hefsek issue, the kavanah requirement should be a Krias Shema requirement. If the Midrash/kinas malachim yesod is right, the kavanah requirement should be tied to the k'vod malchuso being "smuggled" past the malachim. The two frameworks predict subtly different dinim about what counts as adequate kavanah, and neither is worked through.

5.Follow-up Questions Worth Asking the Rav

Q1.How does the Rav (or the Mishnah Berurah) actually resolve the Tzelach-versus-Midrash nafka mina when saying Baruch Shem outside the Shema — e.g., after a brachah l'vatalah, or in the Ashkenazi practice after the second brachah of tefillin? Should it be b'kol ram, b'lachash, or does it depend on which yesod one has accepted?
Q2.If the Nefesh HaChaim and the Baal HaTanya treat Baruch Shem as inseparable from Krias Shema, and the Shiltei Giborim holds one may skip it, is there a way to hold both — e.g., that the kabbalistic essentiality operates on one plane and the halachic minhag-status on another? Do the mekubalim themselves acknowledge the Shiltei Giborim's psak and respond to it?
Q3.The Rav offered a brachah l'vatalah-style teretz for why one must return for skipped kavanah even though the underlying obligation is only minhag. Is this teretz found in any acharon, or is it the Rav's own construction? And does it hold up against cases like Baruch Shem after tefillin, where the same "words once spoken" logic should apply but the kavanah-chazarah requirement seems weaker?
Q4.On the Vilna Gaon's vort — are there any rishonim or earlier mekubalim before the Arizal who read the Chagigah stirah this way? And is there a source that specifies whether the two middle wings "disappeared" or were "folded under" — does the difference in girsah carry any halachic or hashkafic weight?
Q5.Given the Ben Ish Chai's psak to keep the eyes covered through Baruch Shem, and the Rav's observation that even most Sephardim he knows do not follow this — is that a case of minhag drift, or is there a defensible pesak against the Ben Ish Chai here? And does that same question apply to the placement of the hefsek before versus after Baruch Shem?
Q6.Rav Asher Weiss's vort on Amen Yehei Shemei Rabbah as the outlet for the Baruch Shem impulse is beautiful, but it treats the two as psychologically parallel. Is there a source for the equation of their tochen — some earlier mefaresh who linked the loud response of Kaddish to the whispered Baruch Shem as complementary expressions of the same malchus theme?

6.Bottom Line

A tightly built shiur that takes a single whispered line and unpacks it across Chumash, Mishnah, Gemara, Midrash, Rambam, Bach, Tzelach, Shiltei Giborim, Nefesh HaChaim, Tanya, Vilna Gaon, Arizal, Ben Ish Chai, Aruch HaShulchan, and Minchas Asher without ever feeling like a survey. The Tzelach's reframing and the Vilna Gaon's vort on the wings are the two anchors, and both land with real force. The shiur's weakness is a recurring pattern of raising a sharp tension — Tzelach vs. Midrash, mekubalim vs. Shiltei Giborim, minhag vs. takanah — and then moving to the next topic before paskening. A learned talmid chochom leaves knowing far more about the history and structure of Baruch Shem than he did an hour earlier, but with a fistful of halachic loose ends that a second sitting would tie down. The Chanukah landing, the Rav's own reservations about the closing gematria notwithstanding, is genuinely earned.
