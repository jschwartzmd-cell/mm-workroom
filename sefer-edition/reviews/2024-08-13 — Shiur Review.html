Mareh Mekamos · Shiur Review

זוֹכֶה וְרוֹאֶה בְּשִׂמְחָתָהּ — Mourning That Already Tastes of Joy

Tisha B'Av shiur of 13 August 2024  ·  Content-based review · reviewer read the transcript, summary, and finished write-up · 10 August 2026

I. The Shiur in One Paragraph

Delivered on the afternoon of Tisha B'Av — when only divrei emunah u'bitachon are permitted — this shiur takes a single grammatical anomaly as its engine: why does the Gemara in Ta'anis (30b) phrase the reward for mourning Yerushalayim in the present tense (zoche v'roeh) rather than the expected future (yizkeh v'yireh)? The answer, built on a yesod from the Chasam Sofer, turns on the midrashic principle that the divine decree of forgetting (gzeiras shikchah) operates only on the truly dead — never on the living. Since Klal Yisrael continues to mourn Yerushalayim after nearly two thousand years, that very mourning is testimony that Yerushalayim is alive to them; and because she is alive, the mechanism of shikchah cannot engage, the wound never heals into indifference, and the future simchah already flows backward into the present moment of aveilus. The shiur runs this lomdish yesod through three narrative anchors: Yaakov Avinu's refusal to be consoled (Vayema'en l'hinachem), Rabbi Akiva's laughter at Har HaBayis (with a re-reading of the talmudic dialogue that is, on its own, worth the entire hour), and a long biographical digression on Rav Yisrael Meir Lau's childhood in Buchenwald and his Neilah drashah on the Torah's witness-testimony for Klal Yisrael. The Daniel Goldhagen episode stitches Holocaust memory into the same structure of covenantal fidelity.

II. What Was Strongest

The re-reading of the Makkos dialogue is the most original and most forceful moment in the shiur. On the conventional reading, Rabbi Akiva's counter-question — "Why are you crying?" — is a rhetorical deflection, a Jew answering a question with a question. The Chasam Sofer's yesod, applied here, dissolves the deflection entirely: Rabbi Akiva was not avoiding the question; he was answering it. The reason I am laughing, he tells his colleagues, is precisely because you are weeping. Your tears prove that Yerushalayim is alive to you. If she were dead, the mechanism of shikchah would have engaged centuries ago and there would be no tears left. Your aveilus is the evidence of her vitality, and her vitality is the guarantee of the simchah that must follow. Therefore: "Why are you crying?" is not a challenge — it is the answer. This reframing makes the closing "Akiva, nichamtanu" land with entirely new weight: the Tannaim understood that their own weeping was already a form of consolation.

What makes this strong is not merely its elegance but its exegetical completeness. It resolves the kashya on Chazal's present tense, explains why Rabbi Akiva asked what he asked, and accounts for why the other Tannaim understood and were comforted — all from one move. The shiur earns this moment by building the vayema'en l'hinachem scaffolding carefully beforehand.

The Rav Yisrael Meir Lau biographical section is also excellent — the selection scene, the "I am older than you" exchange, and the letter-testimony inversion in the Midrash Eichah are each independently powerful, and they accumulate well. The historical grounding (Buchenwald, Rabbi Herschel Schacter, the specific geography of Piotrkow) gives the drashah a vividness that abstract lomdus alone cannot supply.

III. What Was Weaker — Points Worth Pushing Back On

The Shmelke of Zalachov identification is historically suspect. The HTML names this figure "Rav Shmuel 'Shmelke' Rozovsky of Zalachov, mashgiach of Yeshivas Chachmei Lublin under Rav Meir Shapiro." However, Reb Shmuel Rozovsky is a well-known historical figure — the great Rosh Yeshiva of Ponevezh (1913–1979), not a mashgiach of Chachmei Lublin and certainly not of Zalachov. The transcript leaves the surname unclear ("Reb Shemel Lezalachover"), and the surname "Rozovsky" appears to have been interpolated during the rework without sufficient verification. The mashgiach of Chachmei Lublin under Rav Meir Shapiro was Rav Shmuel Weinberg of Slonim (the "Slonimer Rebbe") according to some accounts, though even this is not unanimously attested. Before this goes into a sefer, the figure's actual identity and any printed source for the Yeshayahu counsel need to be established — or the attribution needs to be generalized ("a mashgiach of the pre-war generation").
The Goldhagen anecdote has the flavor of a parable. The shiur presents, as a documented exchange at a Berlin academic conference, a German professor challenging Goldhagen and receiving a sharp reply about Jewish memory of the Churban. This is rhetorically devastating and theologically apt — but its provenance is unclear. Goldhagen did participate in conferences in Germany, and he did write extensively on German guilt, but this specific exchange is not documented in any readily traceable source. The concern is not that it is wrong but that it could be an embellished mashal circulating in the shiur-circuit that got retold as fact. The write-up hedges slightly ("Goldhagen's answer was quiet and devastating") but presents it without a citation note. For a sefer, it should either be sourced or introduced as "said to have responded."
The Napoleon legend is correctly flagged as dubious, but the Rema attribution is equally loose. The HTML notes (fn. 6) that the insight predates Napoleon and appears in the Toras HaOlah / drashos of the Rema. But "Toras Soferim of the Rema" (as the transcript calls it) is not the same as "Toras HaOlah" — these are two different works. The Toras HaOlah is the Rema's philosophical work on the Beis HaMikdash; "Toras Soferim" is not a standard title for a Rema work. The footnote should either identify precisely where in the Rema this appears or remove the attribution.
The theological claim that post-chatzos relaxations "are the aveilus revealing its inner nechamah" is evocative but imprecise. The minhagim of sweeping the house, sitting on chairs, and some communities painting walls after midday are practical accommodations or symbolic anticipations of the coming geulah, but they are not themselves halachically classified as expressions of inner simchah. Presenting them as organically arising from the Chasam Sofer's yesod imports a philosophical reading onto a set of practical customs. A learned audience might push back: these minhagim predate this particular framing and have their own independent rationales in the poskim.

IV. Halachic and Lomdish Observations

The Chasam Sofer's mechanism deserves a precise locus. The shiur attributes the core yesod — gzeiras shikchah on the dead, but not the living, applied to Yerushalayim — to the Chasam Sofer's drashos. The footnote in the HTML gives "Drashos Chasam Sofer, drush on Vayema'en l'hinachem," which is a reasonable pointer but not a specific volume, siman, or page. The Drashos Chasam Sofer runs to multiple volumes; pinning the citation would significantly strengthen the sefer edition.
The Midrash citation in Bereishis Rabbah is solid. Bereishis Rabbah 84:21 (brought by Rashi on Bereishis 37:35) does state: אֵין גּוֹזְרִין גְּזֵרָה שֶׁל שִׁכְחָה אֶלָּא עַל הַמֵּת — "the decree of forgetting is issued only for the dead." This is a firm textual anchor. The application to Klal Yisrael's mourning of Yerushalayim is the Chasam Sofer's extension, not the Midrash's own statement, which is worth clarifying.
The moed designation of Tisha B'Av (Eichah 1:15) is a legitimate source, but the halachic literature is more nuanced. The Gemara in Yevamos and Mo'ed Katan discuss the special status of Tisha B'Av; some Rishonim do connect the moed designation to the day's underlying redemptive quality. The shiur's reading is in good company, but the source-line could usefully include the Ran or Ritva on Mo'ed Katan.
Rav Lau's "inversion" of the Midrash Eichah letters is theologically arresting but should note whether it is his own chiddush or a received reading. The claim that the Torah-as-Shirah testifies for Klal Yisrael (not against) rests on Devarim 31:19, 21 and is a plausible read of the peshat, but it inverts the simple reading of both the Midrash and most commentators on the parshah. If Rav Lau presents it as his own creative inversion at Neilah, that should be noted. If he cites a source, that source should appear.

V. Follow-Up Questions Worth Asking the Rav

1 Can the precise location in the Drashos Chasam Sofer (volume, page, or siman) for the vayema'en l'hinachem / gzeiras shikchah / zoche v'roeh connection be identified? Is this the Chasam Sofer's original synthesis, or is it a reading that later acharonim attributed to him?
2 Who precisely is "Reb Shmelke of Zalachov," the mashgiach of Chachmei Lublin? Is there a printed source for the counsel to read the Sheva D'nechemta as a remedy for depression, or is this purely oral tradition?
3 Is the Daniel Goldhagen–German professor exchange documented anywhere in print? And was it in Berlin specifically, or is that a detail from a secondary retelling?
4 Rav Lau's "let the letters testify for Klal Yisrael" — does he cite any earlier source for this inversion, or does he present it as his own reading? And does it appear in his published writings (Out of the Depths or collected drashos)?
5 The shiur applies the Napoleon-legend yesod to the Holocaust (Jews who mourned a two-thousand-year-old destruction cannot move on from a seventy-year-old one). Is there a responsum or post-war hashkafah work — perhaps R' Hutner, R' Lichtenstein, or R' Weinberg — that formally develops this parallel between Churban memory and Shoah memory?

VI. Bottom Line

A shiur that wears extremely well: the lomdish core is intellectually tight, the re-reading of the Makkos dialogue is an original and satisfying contribution to the pshat, and the Rav Lau biographical section combines historical vividness with theological reach. The main concerns are factual — the Shmelke of Zalachov identification is probably wrong as written, the Goldhagen exchange lacks a verifiable source, and the Rema attribution in the Napoleon footnote is imprecise — and all three should be resolved before this appears in a sefer. Fix those, and this is among the strongest pieces in the Mareh Mekamos corpus.
