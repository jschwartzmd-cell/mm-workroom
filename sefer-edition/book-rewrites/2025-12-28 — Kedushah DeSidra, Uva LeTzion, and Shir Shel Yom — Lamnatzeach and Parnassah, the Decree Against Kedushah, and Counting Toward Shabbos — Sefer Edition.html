קְדֻשָּׁה דְסִדְרָא, וּבָא לְצִיּוֹן, וְשִׁיר שֶׁל יוֹם

Kedushah DeSidra, Uva LeTzion, and Shir Shel Yom — Lamnatzeach and Parnassah, the Decree Against Kedushah, and Counting Toward Shabbos
Studies in Tefillah (תפילה) — From the Close of the Eighteen to the Song of the Day
8 Tevet 5786 · 28 December 2025 · Sefer Edition

This discourse resumes the study of Tefillah (תפילה) after several weeks, at the point where it left off: Kedushah DeSidra (קדושה דסידרא), the portion of the morning service after Lamnatzeach (למנצח). It first adds to what was said of Lamnatzeach Mizmor LeDavid (למנצח מזמור לדוד): its place as the nineteenth chapter after the eighteen blessings, the midrash (מדרש) that speaks of a woman in labor, and the sustenance (parnassah (פרנסה)) for which Dovid HaMelech (דוד המלך) is said to have recited it.

It then turns to Uva LeTzion (ובא לציון): the Gemara (גמרא) at the end of Maseches Sotah (מסכת סוטה) on the merit by which the world endures; the answer of Rashi (רש״י) and the original structure of the daily communal learning; and three questions of practice (the Aramaic targum (תרגום), the individual, Shabbos (שבת) and Yom Tov (יום טוב)). Two further reasons are given: the decree against saying Kedushah (קדושה) in the synagogue, as recorded in the Sefer HaPardes (ספר הפרדס), and the consideration for those who come late.

The discourse closes with the opening of Shir Shel Yom (שיר של יום): the source in Maseches Tamid (מסכת תמיד), why it is said only in the morning, and why it begins with the count of the day toward Shabbos (שבת). It stops, as the lecturer says, in the middle of the subject, with the promise to continue the following week.

I. Lamnatzeach as the Completion of the Eighteen

The Nineteenth Chapter

The lecturer recalls that Lamnatzeach Mizmor LeDavid (למנצח מזמור לדוד), Yaancha Hashem BeYom Tzarah (יענך ה׳ ביום צרה), is the twentieth chapter of Tehillim (תהלים). The Gemara (גמרא) in Maseches Berachos (מסכת ברכות) teaches that one of the reasons for the eighteen blessings of Shemoneh Esrei (שמונה עשרה) is that they correspond to the first eighteen chapters of Tehillim (תהלים).[1] In ancient times the first and second chapters were a single unit; our twentieth chapter was therefore originally the nineteenth. Just as Dovid HaMelech (דוד המלך) said eighteen chapters and we say eighteen blessings, Lamnatzeach (למנצח) is the nineteenth: the completion of the prayer. The Holy One answers the requests asked in the eighteen blessings, and this is the culmination. Everything said afterwards (Shir Shel Yom (שיר של יום), Aleinu (עלינו), Pitum HaKetores (פיטום הקטורת), Kaveh El Hashem (קוה אל ה׳)) is, the lecturer says, an addition that came later.

The Yahrtzeit of Ezra

The lecturer reports that Rabbi Yehuda Honig (so in the clearest transcript) sent him that morning the date of the yahrtzeit (יארצייט) of Ezra HaSofer (עזרא הסופר); he first said that it was today and then corrected himself: it falls tomorrow.[2] Ezra (עזרא) and the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah (אנשי כנסת הגדולה) instituted these prayers, including the Kedushah DeSidra (קדושה דסידרא) that the discourse takes up, and the reason for it rests on what he established. The Gemara (גמרא) explains that the Avos (אבות) may have said the same blessings, but the exact wording was forgotten, and the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah (אנשי כנסת הגדולה) recorded it for posterity. We do not know for certain that the Avos (אבות) said these very words; in Pirkei DeRabbi Eliezer (פרקי דרבי אליעזר) the wording seems somewhat different.[3] Today our wording is accepted by nearly all of Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל).

II. The Midrash on Chapter Twenty: Comforting a Woman in Labor

The Midrash

The lecturer introduces a midrash (מדרש) that shows how Chazal (חז״ל) regarded this chapter and how it came into the prayer.[4] In ancient times it was said to a woman in the pains of labor (chavlei leidah (חבלי לידה)). What does one say to a woman who is screaming from pain, whether for half an hour, an hour, or twenty-four hours? The doctor says to breathe in and breathe out. But a husband, a relative or a friend who wishes to appease her?

The lecturer recalls the statement of Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai (רבי שמעון בר יוחאי) on why the new mother brings an offering in Parashas Tazria (פרשת תזריע): in the throes of labor she swears never to be with her husband again, and the offering is atonement.[5] We will never understand what a woman goes through. The midrash (מדרש), as the lecturer relates it, offers a psychological answer: one says to her, “How did you end up here? Do you think it was easy for your mother when she gave birth to you? And yet you are here, you are fine, life went on.” Someone suffered to bring you into the world; continue for the next generation. It is the curse that Chavah (חוה) received. And one says, “Yaancha Hashem biyom tzarah”: as the Holy One answered your mother when she prayed, and all her pain gave way to a new life, a new breath, a new soul, so too with you. When she sees that she herself is the proof that the pain ends in light, it resonates.

The Application to the End of Davening

This is what is done at the end of every prayer. The pain and frustration are present in the words of Shemoneh Esrei (שמונה עשרה) (the sicknesses, the salvations, the end of exile), and we acknowledge: יענך ה׳ ביום צרה ישגבך שם אלקי יעקב. As He answered Yaakov Avinu (יעקב אבינו), He will answer us. According to this midrash (מדרש), that is the simple meaning of Lamnatzeach (למנצח) at the close of the prayer.

A Question on the Oath

A listener asked when a woman last actually took such an oath. The lecturer replied that this is a recognized question on Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai (רבי שמעון בר יוחאי) and that there are other opinions on why she brings an offering (some say it is a type of korban todah (קרבן תודה); some link it to the impurity of childbirth), but this was not the time to take it up.

When It Is Not Said, and the Bris

Yaancha (יענך) is not said on Yom Tov (יום טוב), because the verse says ביום צרה and the day is not a day of distress. The lecturer says the practice differs, and that he is unsure about Sephardim. On a day on which Tachanun (תחנון) is omitted, such as a day of yoma depagra (יומא דפגרא), Yaancha (יענך) is likewise omitted. But at a bris (ברית), according to some (the Ashkenazim say it), the great authorities of Ashkenaz explained that there is distress: the pain of the infant. All of us are happy, smelling the bagels and lox of the meal, but the child is in pain.

III. Dovid HaMelech and Parnassah

The Daily Visit of the Sages

The Gemara (גמרא) in Berachos (ברכות) relates that Dovid HaMelech (דוד המלך) rose at midnight, the lyre playing over his bed, and learned, prayed and recited Tehillim (תהלים): חצות לילה אקום להודות לך על משפטי צדקך.[6] At sunrise the sages of Israel came to him and said, “Your people Israel need sustenance.” The task of a king is to see that the economy provides for all. The Gemara (גמרא) records his replies: that they support one another, and that they go out against the troop (lechu ufshtu bagdud (לכו ופשטו בגדוד)). The lecturer glosses this as the advice of conquering the Philistines and taking their fields and vineyards, which he calls not politically correct but understandable once the passage is learned.

The lecturer mentions a rebbe in Eretz Yisrael, known as the “Pishtu Rebbe,” who on the departure of Shabbos (שבת) gives out garlic, calling out פשטו, the word of the Gemara (גמרא) (spread out your hands and take what you want), as a segulah (סגולה) for livelihood.[7] That, he says, is the sense of the word.

The Chapter He Recited

Dovid HaMelech (דוד המלך), the lecturer says, did not return to what he was doing after giving this counsel. As a true leader he took out Tehillim (תהלים) again and prayed that Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל) should have livelihood that day and that week. The chapter he said was Lamnatzeach Mizmor LeDavid (למנצח מזמור לדוד); that is why it is a segulah (סגולה) for livelihood. The lecturer states this as the reason the prayer ends with this chapter; the Gemara (גמרא) itself, as retrieved, does not say what he recited.

Two Roles of Poseiach Es Yadecha

The prayer has Poseiach Es Yadecha (פותח את ידך) in Pesukei DeZimra (פסוקי דזמרה), where it is praise: You provide for all. Here it is a request, and so the lecturer says one should have great intention at Lamnatzeach (למנצח), since it is the chapter that lifts a person to the same place from which Dovid HaMelech (דוד המלך) prayed for livelihood.

Why Not on Tisha B’Av, Shabbos, and Yom Tov

It is not said on Tisha B’Av (תשעה באב), a member of the congregation answered, because one may not work on that day. The lecturer accepted this, though the actual reason, he said, is that the day is called a moed (מועד).[8] On Shabbos (שבת) and Yom Tov (יום טוב) it is not said because there is no labor and no livelihood.

IV. Becoming a Vessel

Opening One’s Own Hands

A question was raised: whose hands does Poseiach Es Yadecha (פותח את ידך) speak of? The Holy One, figuratively, opens His hands. But we said earlier that we, too, must open our hands and make ourselves a vessel (keli (כלי)). A person may say the verse all day; if he does no effort (hishtadlus (השתדלות)) he may sit in the study hall and learn all day. Some do that, but it requires a high level of trust (emunah ubitachon (אמונה ובטחון)). This, the lecturer says, the Ramban (רמב״ן) holds to be for the exalted alone.[9]

The Story of the Alshich

The lecturer recalls the well-known story of the Alshich HaKadosh (אלשיך הקדוש): he taught that everything comes from the Holy One, that one may sit and learn and pray and livelihood will arrive. After the lesson his students went to work; they had heard, but in practice they needed to earn that day. One simple Jew stayed, prayed, and learned all day, and miraculously received not ordinary livelihood but abundance (parnassah berevach (פרנסה ברווח)). Asked why, the Alshich (אלשיך) said, “He really believed.” To sit with folded hands, one must really believe. “We are not on that level at all today; perhaps some are.”[10]

The Empty Carriage

A member of the congregation told a story, which the lecturer received with interest, about a Lubavitcher chassid (חסיד) who had no children for many years. Another couple, from France or elsewhere, came with a baby carriage; there was nothing in the carriage, and that year they had a child. The chassid (חסיד) who had waited all those years went to the Rebbe. The lecturer drew the lesson: when one comes with the vessel (keli (כלי)), with the readiness to receive.[11]

A Segulah From This Week’s Parashah

The lecturer says he told someone the previous day, in the break between the aliyos, of a wonderful segulah (סגולה) from the end of the week’s portion, to be shared with anyone waiting for children. The distress of not having children is very great; he is now dealing with a couple who have waited years. The verse is spoken by Yosef HaTzaddik (יוסף הצדיק): הא לכם זרע.[12] The holy books, he says, teach that Hei (הא) (an uncommon word in the Torah) is an acronym for hachnasas orchim (הכנסת אורחים): even when it is uncomfortable for a couple who have no children, they should bring guests into the home, and they will have offspring. The reason, he says, is beyond the scope of the lesson.

V. Kedushah DeSidra: The Source in Sotah

The Gemara

Why is the prayer called Kedushah DeSidra (קדושה דסידרא), “the sanctification of the order”? The Gemara (גמרא) at the very end of Maseches Sotah (מסכת סוטה) (49a) says that from the day the Temple was destroyed, no day lacks a curse greater than that of the day before.[13] After the destruction came trouble upon trouble and exile upon exile, so that the verse in the rebuke says בבקר תאמר מי יתן ערב ובערב תאמר מי יתן בקר. In the morning a person wishes it were evening; at night, that it were morning. A person even wishes that yesterday’s troubles, which he knew, would return rather than the unknown strength of tomorrow’s. The Gemara (גמרא) asks how the world endures.

The Answer

The world endures, the Gemara (גמרא) answers, on account of kedushah desidra (קדושה דסידרא) and Yehei Shmei Rabba (יהא שמיה רבא) of the aggadah (אגדתא): the Kedushah (קדושה) of Uva LeTzion (ובא לציון) with its קדוש קדוש קדוש, and the Amen Yehei Shmei Rabba (אמן יהא שמיה רבא) answered after the Kaddish DeRabbanan (קדיש דרבנן) that follows a lesson. If one is sensitive to the tragedy of exile and to the Divine Presence no longer resting among Israel as in the days of the Temple, if we are so disconnected, as much as we think we are connected, how does the world stand? On these two.

Rashi

Rashi (רש״י) there explains why it is said every day.[14] Every person must learn Torah daily. We do not have the time; we must work, and what we learn at night is not enough. The sages therefore wished Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל) to learn Torah together as a community in the morning, in the synagogue, after Shacharis (שחרית), instead of going straight home or out to work: a little Torah, caught up in passing. In earlier times, Rashi (רש״י) says, more verses were said.

What Was Originally Learned

The lecturer recalls the letter of Rav Natronai Gaon (רב נטרונאי גאון) to the community of Lucena, discussed some weeks earlier. Originally, after the prayer everyone sat and learned together, for an hour or two. (The Mishnah (משנה) in Berachos (ברכות) speaks of the early pious, who waited an hour before praying, preparing; after prayer they learned.)[15] There was a lesson every day, as there is in many synagogues today, and the lecturer expects that the time will come when there will be lessons after prayer every day, a group learning together; that is what sustains the world. As exile continued many could not stay. The sources describe the course of study as Scripture (verses from the Prophets), Mishnah (משנה), and an explanation of the Mishnayos (משניות), but people were poor and had to give those minutes or hour to their work. What was left were the verses of Kedushah (קדושה) from Yeshayahu (ישעיה), קדוש קדוש קדוש; with a few verses before and a few after, they are the core of kedushah desidra (קדושה דסידרא). It was part of the seder hayom (סדר היום): prayer, a little learning, and then Kaddish (קדיש) and Yehei Shmei Rabba (יהא שמיה רבא).

VI. Questions on Kedushah DeSidra

Why the Targum?

The Kedushah (קדושה) was already said in Yotzer Or (יוצר אור) and in the Chazaras HaShatz (חזרת הש״ץ), without targum (תרגום). Why here with it? (The lecturer notes that many say the targum (תרגום) quietly (bilachash (בלחש)), Sephardim in particular; that some Rishonim (ראשונים) say it quietly because targum (תרגום) is not said aloud, though not by right; and that the Arizal (אריז״ל) was not strict.) The reason, according to Rashi (רש״י), is that this is a piece of learning, and even the unlettered (amei haaretz (עמי הארצות)), who did not know the holy tongue and spoke Aramaic in Babylonia, were to take part. Ezra HaSofer (עזרא הסופר) and the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah (אנשי כנסת הגדולה) therefore instituted that the verses be said with their translation, so that all would understand that we are learning verses of Yeshayahu HaNavi (ישעיה הנביא) and sanctifying the Name at once. Rashi (רש״י) says that the two together are the merit by which the world stands. It is Torah study, not prayer proper; it is a miniature lesson in verses of Scripture. This is the simple meaning according to Rashi (רש״י); and the Rambam (רמב״ם), it seems, also holds that it is a matter of Torah study.[16]

May It Be Said by an Individual?

A listener asked why one sits and whether it may be said with fewer than ten. The Tur (טור) brings an opinion that it may not be said by an individual, as with Kedushah (קדושה). The ruling, however, follows the other Rishonim (ראשונים): since it is not truly Kedushah (קדושה) but in place of Torah study, it does not carry the same law as Kedushah (קדושה) in the presence of ten, and an individual may say it.[17] Rav Chaim Vital (רב חיים ויטאל) reports that his master the Arizal (אריז״ל) was not strict, though it seems they used to be careful to say it with the community.

Why Not on Shabbos and Yom Tov After Shacharis?

On Shabbos (שבת) and Yom Tov (יום טוב) Uva LeTzion (ובא לציון) is not said after Shacharis (שחרית), only at Minchah (מנחה). The Rishonim (ראשונים) say the reason is that on those days people had time to learn and did not need the extra learning. Why at Minchah (מנחה)? The lecturer said he did not have a good answer; the point, he suggested, is that the enactment not be forgotten, so it was moved to Minchah (מנחה), showing that Shabbos (שבת) differs from a weekday morning, when people must rush out. (A listener raised the saying of Uva LeTzion (ובא לציון) on the departure of Shabbos (שבת) at Maariv (מעריב); the lecturer said that is a different reason, to be taken up another time.)

VII. The Decree Against Kedushah

Sefer HaPardes

The lecturer says he now comes to a reason that he finds fascinating, which concerns the disciples of Yeshu (ישו). It is found in the Sefer HaPardes (ספר הפרדס).[18] The name already sounds mystical: in the language of Chazal (חז״ל) the Pardes (פרדס) is the mystical orchard, as in the Gemara (גמרא) in Chagigah (חגיגה), four entered the orchard, and only Rabbi Akiva (רבי עקיבא) came out unharmed.[19] The book was written in the period of the Rishonim (ראשונים), seemingly by a student of Rashi (רש״י), either Rabbeinu Nosson (רבינו נתן) or Rabbeinu Simcha (רבינו שמחה), and consists chiefly of rulings of Rashi (רש״י), with much on the order of prayer. The lecturer says it records this explanation in the name of Rashi (רש״י).

The Disciples

The lecturer mentions that he once gave a whole lesson on how Chazal (חז״ל) and the Rishonim (ראשונים) regarded Yeshu (ישו), and refers to the Rambam (רמב״ם) at the end of Hilchos Melachim (הלכות מלכים) and to the Yaavetz (יעב״ץ), who writes that Yeshu (ישו) is the acronym of ימח שמו וזכרו (he mentioned it the previous Friday night; the Gemara (גמרא) in Gittin (גיטין) mentions him).[20] But the point here is not he; it is his disciples. Whatever one holds of him, the lecturer says, his disciples were dangerous to Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל); see Rashi (רש״י) on the Shas (ש״ס) wherever they are mentioned, with the phrase הוא הסית והדיח וכישף. (The lecturer declined to take up, or to name, a rabbi in Eretz Yisrael who had spoken about Yeshu (ישו) that week.)

The historical setting, he stresses, is not today’s. Today Christians are Christians and Jews are Jews. In those centuries the origins of Christianity lay in our own synagogues and community; they were Jews who chose to follow him, with his heresy and denial, and it was hard to separate them from those who believed in the Torah and the Oral Torah.

The Decree

These disciples were so hostile to Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל) that they went to the authorities in Eretz Yisrael and told them that what the Jews say in their synagogues mocks you and is bad; guard them and see that nothing is said against the government. Among the decrees of the government of that time, a piece of Jewish history that most do not know, was that Jews might not sanctify the Name in the synagogue: they could not say Kedushah (קדושה), קדוש קדוש קדוש, nor ברוך כבוד ה׳ ממקומו, which for some reason were perceived as against him and against the local government. The lecturer says he does not understand the reason, but that there was such a decree for a long time. Imagine going to synagogue and omitting the Kedushah (קדושה) of Yotzer Or (יוצר אור), and that of the Chazaras HaShatz (חזרת הש״ץ). How can a Jew begin the day without Kedushah (קדושה)?

The Enactment

The sages of that time made an enactment. The guards sent by the government left at a certain point: after Sim Shalom (שים שלום), at the end of the Chazaras HaShatz (חזרת הש״ץ), when the Jews were known not to say Kedushah (קדושה). Once they left, the Jews rose and said Kedushah (קדושה): that is, in Uva LeTzion (ובא לציון). This, he says, is also why the targum (תרגום) is said: we had missed two Kedushos (קדושות), that of Yotzer Or (יוצר אור) and that of the Chazaras HaShatz (חזרת הש״ץ). The Sefer HaPardes (ספר הפרדס) writes this in the name of Rashi (רש״י). Since we must make up what we missed, with the guards standing over us like hawks, at the end of the prayer, when they had left, we say Uva LeTzion (ובא לציון). The essence of it is the Kedushah (קדושה); we repeat it in its targum (תרגום) to make up the two omitted. This is a completely different reason from the one given before.

Why the Enactment Remained

Today we say Kedushah (קדושה) in the morning and in the Chazaras HaShatz (חזרת הש״ץ) and again in Uva LeTzion (ובא לציון), because the enactment remained. The Rishonim (ראשונים) say something that, the lecturer says, boggles his mind: who knows, perhaps they will return one day and decree again, so we keep it in place. This reminds him of the second day of Yom Tov (יום טוב) in the Diaspora, which bothers many people: the Gemara (גמרא) in Beitzah (ביצה) (4) speaks of keeping the custom of one’s fathers lest the government issue a decree. He did not remember the exact wording and wondered aloud whether there is reason to be concerned today.[21] Some enactments of the sages remained even though the reason is gone. When one says Kedushah DeSidra (קדושה דסידרא), the lecturer says, one is reenacting what was done when it could not be said in the synagogue.

Shema in the Kedushah of Musaf

A listener asked whether the same applies elsewhere, and the lecturer asked for a source before he would accept it; perhaps, he said, the listener meant the reason the Rishonim (ראשונים) give for the שמע ישראל ה׳ אלקינו ה׳ אחד in the Kedushah (קדושה) of Musaf (מוסף) on Shabbos (שבת). Why say Shema (שמע) again? Because there was once a decree that Shema (שמע) might not be said in the synagogue, and by the time Musaf (מוסף) came, whoever was watching had left, so it was inserted into the Kedushah (קדושה); today there is no problem, but it remained part of the enactment.[22] He finds it striking that Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל) still clings to these because of that decree, which, he says, specifically the disciples of Yeshu (ישו) caused.

A Digression on a Date

The conversation touched on the legend that Nishmas (נשמת) was composed by one of those disciples (spoken of in an earlier lesson), and the lecturer said the yahrtzeit of that figure, “whatever his name,” falls tomorrow, citing a source in the Shulchan Aruch (שלחן ערוך) on the fasts of Tevet and correcting himself on whether the date is the eighth or the ninth.[23] The recording is not clear enough to state this further; it is recorded in the Open Questions.

VIII. The Latecomers

The Avudraham

There is one more reason, the lecturer says, which he loves. We frown on those who come late to synagogue, himself included. There are excuses, such as children at home or trudging through snow, as on the Shabbos before this lecture, and we cannot judge; that is the Holy One’s task. Still it bothers a Jew. Chazal (חז״ל) were much more sensitive. This is at least the sixth or seventh time on this journey through Tefillah (תפילה) that he has stressed that certain prayers were instituted because of latecomers.

The Avudraham (אבודרהם), whom the lecturer quotes often and whom the Beis Yosef (בית יוסף) cites here, writes that one reason we say Uva LeTzion (ובא לציון) at the end of the prayer is for those who come so late that the prayer is already over. The sages made a miniature prayer for them, containing Ashrei (אשרי), the core of Pesukei DeZimra (פסוקי דזמרה) (we say פותח את ידך ומשביע לכל חי רצון), and Kedushah (קדושה), because these are important. Shema (שמע) they will have to manage on their own. So all of us stay an extra five minutes because of the latecomers of that time.[24] Instead of dismissing them, the sages were sensitive to those who do not recognize the importance of coming on time, and made a whole new prayer for them. (The lecturer says that he was thinking aloud and was not sure of the connection of this to Lamnatzeach (למנצח).)

Why Not on Shabbos

This is also why it is not said on Shabbos (שבת) and Yom Tov (יום טוב): everyone comes on time, not everywhere, but generally, because on those days the service is later. In the weekday morning one comes at seven or eight; on Shabbos (שבת) one begins much later, though the Shulchan Aruch (שלחן ערוך) speaks of praying early, and there is a dispute there; our custom is to pray later. The lecturer relates that his grandfather, Rabbi Yisrael Belsky (as in the clearest transcript), told him when he first married, on visiting Miami, that Shabbos (שבת) morning in his son-in-law’s kollel (כולל) was at 8:15, and that in ordinary synagogues it was 8:00 to 8:30. When the lecturer prayed at a Chassidic shtiebel at nine and sat sipping his coffee for forty-five minutes, his grandfather was angry. He said that the nine o’clock hour was a new thing, from the Chassidic shtieblach that opened in America after the war, to show that they were not working on Shabbos (שבת). A listener added that he had read in a biography of Rabbi Dovid Feinstein that MTJ prays at nine because originally it was made late deliberately, as not for those who go to work.[25] Since on Shabbos (שבת) we pray later, nobody is late; it is said at Minchah (מנחה) only to remember the enactment.

IX. Baruch Hu Elokeinu

Gratitude

After Uva LeTzion (ובא לציון) we say verses beginning ברוך הוא אלקינו שבראנו לכבודו והבדילנו מן התועים. If Rashi (רש״י)’s theory is correct, that the Kedushah (קדושה) in Uva LeTzion (ובא לציון) is there because of those who did not want us to say it, and so we waited until they left and said it here, then the verse is understood: thank God, we are not like them; we managed to snatch a Kedushah (קדושה), and now we can leave the synagogue and go home. This too, he says, is explained by some of the Acharonim (אחרונים) on the basis of this Rashi (רש״י).

X. Shir Shel Yom

The Source in Tamid

Every day we say the song of the day. The Mishnah (משנה) in Maseches Tamid (מסכת תמיד) teaches that every day the Levi’im (לוים), when they brought the wine libation (nisuch hayayin (ניסוך היין)) after the morning tamid (תמיד) and the afternoon tamid (תמיד), said a psalm: on Sunday לדוד מזמור לה׳ הארץ ומלואה, then six more for the days following, and on Shabbos (שבת) מזמור שיר ליום השבת.[26]

Why Only in the Morning?

There were two offerings every day. We say the song to remember the Levi’im (לוים) standing on the platform; why not at Minchah (מנחה), when the same offering and libation were brought? The answer, the lecturer says (he notes that the congregation is now learning Zevachim (זבחים)), is that in the Temple service there was sometimes such crowding, with so many offerings, that the priests delayed the wine libation, and it came only at night, after the afternoon tamid (תמיד). And song is not said at night.[27] So although we say it daily to remember the song of the Levi’im (לוים), we do not say it at Minchah (מנחה).

XI. “Today Is the First Day Toward Shabbos”

The Ramban on Zachor

Why does the song begin היום יום ראשון בשבת שבו היו הלוים אומרים בבית המקדש? A listener supplied the answer: זכור את יום השבת לקדשו, and the lecturer said that is exactly what he wished to say. There is a Ramban (רמב״ן) on the verse, and it is important for every Jew to know: do not remember Shabbos (שבת) only on Shabbos (שבת), in the prayers, Kiddush (קידוש) and Havdalah (הבדלה).[28] The commandment applies every day. Some say the six remembrances (sheish zechiros (שש זכירות)) daily at the end of the prayers, of which one is זכור את יום השבת לקדשו; for those who do not, this is where the commandment is fulfilled. (The lecturer says that it is not counted among the commandments, which he calls a whole question among the Acharonim (אחרונים).) What better place than the song of the day to remember Shabbos (שבת)?

The Names of the Days

The Ramban (רמב״ן) adds that we must be careful of the names. Among the nations the days are called Sunday, Monday, Tuesday, and so on, and the names are those of the seven planets known in antiquity, which were deities: the sun, the moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, Saturn. (The lecturer worked out the order aloud with the help of the congregation, and mentioned Constantine as the one who fixed the week.) How can a Jew say “Sunday”? He is saying, in effect, “the day of the sun.” What sun? Stars and constellations: idolatry. The Ramban (רמב״ן) says Jews count differently, as the lecturer’s three-year-old does, with the innocence of children: asked what day it is, he answers היום ראשון, מחר שני. A listener asked about the months, and the lecturer replied that the Ramban (רמב״ן) has a long discussion of that on החודש הזה לכם.

We live in the world and say Sunday and Monday, but the Ramban (רמב״ן) says: at least once a day call the days as they should be called, Rishon LeShabbos (ראשון לשבת), because every day is connected with the Shabbos (שבת) that follows.

Wednesday and Lechu Neranenah

A listener asked why on Wednesday לכו נרננה is added at the end. The lecturer replied that the chapter of the day does not end there: it ends with ויצמיתם ה׳ אלקינו, and a few verses are added so as not to end on a bad word.[29]

XII. Closing

The lecturer ended by saying that the following week would continue with Shir Shel Yom (שיר של יום), and wished all a good day and a good week. The discourse closed with the Mishnah (משנה): רבי חנניה בן עקשיא אומר רצה הקדוש ברוך הוא לזכות את ישראל לפיכך הרבה להם תורה ומצוות שנאמר ה׳ חפץ למען צדקו יגדיל תורה ויאדיר.[30]
