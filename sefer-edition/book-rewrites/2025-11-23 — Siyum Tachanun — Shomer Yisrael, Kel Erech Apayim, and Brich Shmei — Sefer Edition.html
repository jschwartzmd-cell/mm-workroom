סִיּוּם הַתַּחֲנוּן וּפְתִיחַת הָאָרוֹן — שׁוֹמֵר יִשְׂרָאֵל, קֵל אֶרֶךְ אַפַּיִם, וּבְרִיךְ שְׁמֵהּ

Closing Tachanun and Opening the Aron: Shomer Yisrael, Kel Erech Apayim, and Brich Shmei
Studies in Tefillah (תפילה) — From Tachanun to the Torah Reading
3 Kislev 5786  ·  23 November 2025  ·  Sefer Edition

This discourse completes Tachanun (תחנון) and crosses into the Torah reading. It begins with Shomer Yisrael (שומר ישראל) and the stanza of it that almost no siddur prints, and with the closing verses of Tachanun; it takes up VaAnachnu Lo Neda (ואנחנו לא נדע) and the three postures of prayer, and the verse Al Tizkar Lanu Avonos Rishonim (אל תזכר לנו עונות ראשונים) as read by the Ach Pri Tevuah (אך פרי תבואה); and it explains the Chatzi Kaddish (חצי קדיש) that follows.

It then turns to the opening of the Aron (ארון) as a time of favor (עת רצון); to Kel Erech Apayim (קל ארך אפים), the one prayer of the weekday siddur that is printed in two versions, with the three reasons given for it and the count of fifty words in the Avudraham (אבודרהם); and to VaYehi BiNsoa HaAron (ויהי בנסוע הארן), the verse of the ark that went before Israel.

It closes with Brich Shmei (בריך שמיה): its source in the Zohar (זוהר), the time for saying it, the words in parentheses that ask for children, and the saying Ana Avda (אנא עבדא דקודשא בריך הוא), together with the manner in which the moment is to be kept.

I. Shomer Yisrael

An Ancient Piyyut

Shomer Yisrael (שומר ישראל) is said every day: not only on the days when Tachanun is said, but also on Monday and Thursday, when it follows Vehu Rachum (והוא רחום). It opens:

שׁוֹמֵר יִשְׂרָאֵל, שְׁמוֹר שְׁאֵרִית יִשְׂרָאֵל, וְאַל יֹאבַד יִשְׂרָאֵל הָאוֹמְרִים שְׁמַע יִשְׂרָאֵל.
Guardian of Israel, guard the remnant of Israel, and let not Israel perish, those who say “Hear, O Israel.”

The author of this piyyut (פיוט) is unknown. Researchers have tried to trace its first appearance, and it is found already in the siddurim of the Ashkenazic Rishonim (ראשונים), among them the Siddur Rashi (סדור רש״י), of which the lecturer has spoken on many occasions.[1] The piyyut is a petition, at the end of Tachanun and after Shemoneh Esrei (שמונה עשרה), that the Guardian of Israel guard the people that has just said Shema Yisrael (שמע ישראל).

The Missing Stanza

A stanza of the piyyut is missing from the siddurim of most Ashkenazic communities. Those who pray Nusach Sefard (נוסח ספרד) and the Sephardic and Eastern rites add it; as the lecturer recited it, it reads:

שׁוֹמֵר גּוֹי קָדוֹשׁ, שְׁמוֹר שְׁאֵרִית עַם קָדוֹשׁ, וְאַל יֹאבַד גּוֹי קָדוֹשׁ, הַמְשַׁלְּשִׁים בְּשָׁלשׁ קְדֻשּׁוֹת לְקָדוֹשׁ.
Guardian of the holy nation, guard the remnant of the holy people, and let not the holy nation perish, who three times proclaim sanctity to the Holy One.
As recited in the recording; the text is that of the Sephardic rites.

The Shelah HaKadosh (של״ה הקדוש) issued his own siddur, Shaar HaShamayim (שער השמים),[2] and charged his children in his ethical will (צוואה) that when they printed it they not omit a further verse that he had found to belong to the piyyut. The lecturer gave it as Shomer Goy Rabba (שומר גוי רבא), “guardian of the great nation,” continuing in the same pattern and ending HaOmrim Amen Yehei Shemei Rabba (האומרים אמן יהא שמיה רבא); he said that he did not remember the rest of it.[3] Rabbi Nosson Adler (ר׳ נתן אדלר), the teacher of the Chasam Sofer (חתם סופר), is reported to have added it as well.[4] The Ashkenazic community does not say it, and the lecturer has seen no Ashkenazic siddur that prints it; yet to the Shelah it was essential.

Differing Customs

The Gra (הגר״א) did not say Shomer Yisrael on an ordinary weekday, but only on a public fast (תענית ציבור); the lecturer cites the Maaseh Rav (מעשה רב), the record of the Gra’s customs and practices.[5] The rest of Israel is not like that: Shomer Yisrael is printed in every Ashkenazic siddur and is said every day.

II. Closing Tachanun

After Shomer Yisrael the text continues Mispayyes BeTachanunim (מתפייס בתחנונים), “He is appeased by supplications,” the culmination of all the supplications that have been said: the worshipper asks that God be appeased by all that has just been poured out before Him.

Then one last Avinu Malkeinu (אבינו מלכנו) is said, the Avinu Malkeinu of Rabbi Akiva (רבי עקיבא).[6] In a year of drought no one could bring rain; Rabbi Akiva rose as shaliach tzibbur (שליח ציבור) and said Avinu Malkeinu, and the rain came. Why this one Avinu Malkeinu was set into the daily Tachanun the lecturer does not know; it is the custom.

III. VaAnachnu Lo Neda

The Three Postures

A short passage follows: VaAnachnu Lo Neda Mah Na’aseh, Ki Alecha Eineinu (ואנחנו לא נדע מה נעשה כי עליך עינינו), “we do not know what to do, for our eyes are upon You.” The Rishonim, as the lecturer reports, explain that it comes at the end of the three physical postures of prayer.

The opening of the service, Pesukei DeZimra (פסוקי דזמרה) and Kri’as Shema (קריאת שמע), is said miyushav (מיושב), seated; the lecturer says that the Shulchan Aruch directs one to sit for Kri’as Shema, although some people stand.[7] Shemoneh Esrei is said me’umad (מעומד), standing. Tachanun is nefilas apayim (נפילת אפים), falling on the face: in earlier times lying flat on the floor, today on the forearm. The worshipper has stood, has sat, and has fallen; he has prayed in whatever manner he can. What is left is to say: please accept.

Sitting and Standing

On this basis the halachah (הלכה) is that VaAnachnu Lo Neda is said seated and Mah Na’aseh standing. The Mechaber (מחבר) is not strict about it, but the Mishnah Berurah (משנה ברורה) brings it in the name of the Shelah.[8] People are not always careful about it; many rise at VaAnachnu Lo Neda, but the proper practice is the reverse. The lecturer adds that the siddurim (he names the ArtScroll) print it so, and that the Rambam in Hilchos Tefillah (הלכות תפילה) writes that after nefilas apayim one sits.[9] He says that he does not know the reason for the division, calling such matters “secrets” (סודות), and offers the three postures as a possible one.

IV. Al Tizkar Lanu Avonos Rishonim

The Ach Pri Tevuah

The verse is Al Tizkar Lanu Avonos Rishonim, Maher Yekademunu Rachamecha Ki Dalonu Me’od (אל תזכר לנו עונות ראשונים, מהר יקדמונו רחמיך כי דלונו מאד):[10] do not remember our earlier sins; let Your mercy come quickly to meet us, for we are very poor. The lecturer brings a reading from the Ach Pri Tevuah,[11] the book of Rabbi Hershele of Liska (ר׳ הירשעלע ליסקא), whom he describes as a great talmid chacham (תלמיד חכם) and tzaddik, a disciple of the Yismach Moshe (ישמח משה) and one of the major Hungarian Chassidic Rebbes. The book is difficult to study, but rich.

Rivkah’s Veil

The Ach Pri Tevuah writes on a verse in Chayei Sarah (חיי שרה). Yitzchak is seen by Rivkah as she approaches on the camels; she falls from her camel at the sight of him (ותפל מעל הגמל), and then “she took the veil and covered herself” (ותקח הצעיף ותתכס).[12] Many commentators find this difficult. The Ach Pri Tevuah reads it as an allusion (בדרך הרמז) to the sentence Amcha Yisrael Tzerichin Parnasah (עמך ישראל צריכין פרנסה), “Your people Israel need sustenance,” which the lecturer identifies with the Gemara at the very beginning of the Talmud.

Dovid HaMelech’s Day

Berachos 3b (ברכות ג׳ ע״ב) gives the daily schedule of Dovid HaMelech (דוד המלך).[13] A harp hung above his bed; at midnight the north wind blew on it and it played of itself, and he rose and learned Torah until dawn. At first light he took questions: releasing wives to their husbands, the laws of niddah (נדה), questions of every kind. And every day, the Gemara continues, the sages of Israel came to him and said: “Our master the king, Your people Israel need sustenance.” The first task of a king, of any leader of Israel, is the livelihood of his people. First he told them to do business with one another.

The lecturer observes that sometimes it takes a king to tell Jews to do business with Jews. There is a mitzvah (מצוה) to buy from Jews and to sell to Jews; the practical question is price. If the other store is much cheaper, one may buy there, but lechatchilah (לכתחילה), even at some added cost, one should give a fellow Jew a little livelihood. The greatest mitzvah is to raise the livelihood of other people. When that was not enough, the Gemara has Dovid say Lechu UFishtu BaGedud (לכו ופשטו בגדוד), “go and take up arms with the troops.” The lecturer glosses this as taking from the Philistines, who were in constant battle with Israel, and calls it controversial.[14]

The Two First Questions

The Ach Pri Tevuah continues: Rivkah is chesed (חסד), Yitzchak is din (דין) and gevurah (גבורה); to sweeten the judgments (להמתיק הדינים), a term common in kabbalistic and Chassidic books, she drew the veil over herself. The whole matter is folded into our verse. What are the first two questions a Jew is asked in heaven?[15] The first is Nasasa VeNasata BeEmunah (נשאת ונתת באמונה): did you deal faithfully in business? The second is Kavata Itim LaTorah (קבעת עתים לתורה): did you set fixed times for Torah, however busy and however hard you worked? (The lecturer notes that another Gemara seems to say otherwise, but these are the first two.)

The only excuse a Jew has for either, a poor excuse but an excuse, is: I had to earn a living. I could not learn enough, for I had to feed my family. I cut a corner, for I needed the money. That, the lecturer says, is what the verse tells God: Al Tizkar Lanu Avonos Rishonim, do not hold against us the first two sins we know we shall be asked about; Ki Dalonu Me’od, we are so poor. We ask for sustenance in ease (ברווח), so that we are not tempted to steal and not tempted to neglect Torah. Rivkah covered the gevuros of Yitzchak with the veil for the same purpose, to soften the judgments that flow from those two questions. The lecturer adds that this is not a good excuse, and that one should not rely on it.

V. Chatzi Kaddish — Why Half?

Then a Chatzi Kaddish (חצי קדיש) is said. Why only half? The Rambam (רמב״ם) in Hilchos Tefillah writes that the shaliach tzibbur says Kaddish before and after Kri’as Shema.[16] Ordinarily a Kaddish that closes a section of prayer contains Titkabel Tzelosehon UVa’usehon (תתקבל צלותהון ובעותהון), that the prayers be accepted, and that is not said until after Ashrei and UVa LeTziyon (אשרי ובא לציון). What is this short Kaddish doing here?

Rabbeinu Manoach

The lecturer says that he will not speak of kabbalah (going up and coming down through the worlds, according to the Arizal); he has no license for that, and is speaking peshat halachah. He brings a commentary on the Rambam’s Hilchos Tefillah by Rabbeinu Manoach (רבינו מנוח),[17] not often cited because, he says, little of it survives. (The name is rare today; Manoach was the father of Shimshon. The lecturer reports that he found a Rabbeinu Manoach cited once in Tosafos and thinks it the same Rishon.)

Rabbeinu Manoach explains that Chazal (חז״ל) knew that not every Jew can stay to the end of the prayer. People run to catch the train and must be in the office on time; and if the shaliach tzibbur is slow, or it is Monday or Thursday with the additional supplications, some must leave before Ashrei and UVa LeTziyon. So they instituted a Kaddish immediately after Tachanun, but only half of one, to make clear that the prayer is not yet finished. The Jew who must leave receives a partial send-off; only one who stays through Ashrei and UVa LeTziyon merits the Titkabel of the full Kaddish. The lecturer adds that there are further reasons, which he does not know.

VI. The Open Aron — A Time of Favor

Silence at the Opening

When the Aron is opened to take out the Sefer Torah, it is an eis ratzon (עת רצון), a time of favor. The Zohar teaches, as the lecturer reports, that when the Aron opens below the gates of heaven open above.[18] The Chida (חיד״א) says that one must be silent at the opening of the Aron.[19] The lecturer applies this to a familiar scene: the Aron opens, the gabbai is settling who will receive the aliyah, there are a few loose seconds, and conversation begins, even while Kel Erech Apayim and Brich Shmei are being said. He says that he does not know the exact limits of speech once the Sefer Torah is open, but that the Chida does not permit speech at the opening of the Aron: Ki MiTziyon Teitzei Torah (כי מציון תצא תורה);[20] as the scroll leaves the Aron, the gates of heaven are opening.

The Baal Shem Tov on Brich Shmei

The Baal Shem Tov (בעל שם טוב), in Kesser Shem Tov (כתר שם טוב),[21] is reported to teach that a simple Jew who says Brich Shmei from the depth of his heart and with simple faith, using the very words of the prayer, can accomplish much in heaven, for all the gates are open at that moment. If he believes, and says the words with kavanah (כוונה), God will answer his prayer: perhaps not all of it, but at least something will be granted.

VII. Kel Erech Apayim — Two Versions Side by Side

A Unique Phenomenon

Kel Erech Apayim (קל ארך אפים) is, as far as the lecturer knows, the only prayer in the weekday siddur that is printed in two versions, Nusach Ashkenaz (נוסח אשכנז) and Nusach Polin (נוסח פולין), each labeled. The differences are minute: two short sentences, one reading Hoshi’enu (הושיענו) where the other has Hatzilenu (הצילנו). In Selichos (סליחות) there is a similar division, but in the ordinary daily prayer this is unique. Every siddur, whether Nusach Ashkenaz, Sefard or Ari, prints both, and has for centuries. Why here?

The lecturer’s own practice had been to say only the first. In preparing the discourse he concluded that those who say both are doing the right thing. It takes about fourteen seconds. The reasons follow.

First Reason — The Latecomers

Some Rishonim who wrote commentaries on the prayers explain that Kel Erech Apayim was instituted for those who arrive late on Monday and Thursday. By the time they come in, the Sefer Torah is already being taken out, and they have not said Vehu Rachum, which has become a beloved prayer throughout Israel (the lecturer recalls the discussion of two weeks earlier, with the three great men and the miracle of the fire). They want to say it. But during the Torah reading everyone must listen and follow every word, and Vehu Rachum cannot be said. So Chazal gave the latecomer a small abbreviated form of “do not punish us”: these two short stanzas, enough to discharge the intent of the missed prayer without disturbing the reading.

According to this view the prayer is said only on days when Tachanun is said. The Gra (הגר״א) attributes the institution to the chachmei Italia (חכמי איטליא), the scholars of Italy.[22]

An Aside on Nusach Bnei Romi

Beyond the principal rites — Nusach Ashkenaz, Sefard, Ari (the rite of Chabad) and the Eastern communities — there is a distinct Nusach Bnei Romi (נוסח בני רומי), the rite of the Italian communities, which the lecturer knows to be kept in only two places in the world. Its siddur is based, he says, on the Shibbolei HaLeket (שבלי הלקט) of Rabbi Tzidkiah ben Avraham HaRofe (ר׳ צדקיה בן אברהם הרופא), one of the Rishonim.[23] Its Friday-night Maariv differs; its Emes VeEmunah (אמת ואמונה) reads differently; on Shabbos it returns the Sefer Torah between Ashrei and UVa LeTziyon (as does one other community); and it omits HaNoten LaYaef Koach (הנותן ליעף כח), with a different blessing at that place in Birchos HaShachar (ברכות השחר). The lecturer has not prayed in Rome; he watched videos of the service.

There is also a former Italian synagogue in Jerusalem, on Rechov Hillel in Nachalat Shiva, near Nachla’os, which preserves the tradition. The lecturer reports that after the founding of the State it opened, in 1951, as a shul for Italian immigrants; the community dwindled and the building became a museum, though minyanim are still held there on Shabbos and Yom Tov. The Aron of an Italian town was reconstructed in it, with objects from the Italian communities of the 1700s and 1800s. For many years the Nusach Bnei Romi was prayed there.[24]

When one opens the Machzor Bnei Romi, however, Kel Erech Apayim does not appear. The lecturer therefore says that he is not sure which Italian community the Gra meant: perhaps a regional variant, perhaps a tradition in manuscript, perhaps a mistake. The result, on this view, is the same: the prayer was instituted for the latecomer, and everyone says it now so as not to single out the latecomers.

Second Reason — Days of Din

Most authorities follow a different line, which has nothing to do with Tachanun. The Machzor Vitry (מחזור ויטרי) explains:[25] Monday and Thursday are the days on which the Torah is read. They were also, in earlier centuries, the days on which the beis din (בית דין) of every city sat, not daily but on those days, because they were market days when the villagers came to the city (compare the opening mishnah of Kesubos and Megillah).[26] When there is judgment below there is judgment above. It is not wished that the judges err, and so the whole community prays on Monday and Thursday, even when Tachanun is not said: Hatzilenu MiKol Ra (הצילנו מכל רע), save us from every evil, perhaps including a mistake in judgment. The lecturer notes that he sees no direct reference to judges in the prayer itself; the link is a reading. On this view the prayer belongs to the day and not to Tachanun.

Third Reason — An Apology Before the Reading

Other Rishonim give a reason that is still more moving. The Sefer Torah about to be taken out contains mitzvos that, in honesty, we do not always keep. So we apologize first: Kel Erech Apayim, be patient with us, have mercy. We know that we are about to read from Your Torah of things that we do not always do properly. The Siddur HaRokeach (סידור הרוקח) is cited for this explicitly.[27] On this view the prayer belongs to every taking-out of the Sefer Torah: whenever the Aron opens one apologizes first.

VIII. The Avudraham — Fifty Words

Why Two Versions?

The two versions may have begun as something close to an error. In ancient siddurim both prayers appear together, without any labeling of “Ashkenaz” and “Polin,” before the distinction existed.

Who the Avudraham Was

The Avudraham (אבודרהם),[28] the work of Rabbeinu David Avudraham (רבינו דוד אבודרהם), is the foundational book for the explanation of the prayers, quoted hundreds of times. The correct pronunciation of the name, the lecturer says, is Arabic: Abu Dirham. The dirham is the ancient Middle Eastern coin still used in Morocco and the Emirates. On the simple explanation, his family were money-changers, and he was “father of the coin.” On a more poetic one, since he gave his life to explaining the prayers, and prayer is called matbea shetavu chachamim (מטבע שטבעו חכמים), the coinage that the Sages minted, which may not be altered, he was “father of that coinage.” The lecturer does not know which is historically accurate but finds the second beautiful.

The Count

The Avudraham counts every word of the prayers, and in his commentaries he regularly finds hints in the numbers of words and letters. He counted the two versions of Kel Erech Apayim together and found fifty words: forty plus ten, corresponding to the forty days and forty nights that Moshe Rabbeinu spent on Har Sinai and to the Aseres HaDibros (עשרת הדברות). Therefore, the lecturer reports, one should say both versions; he states that the Avudraham calls it an error to say only one. The lecturer adds that a later printing of the ArtScroll siddur notes that some are accustomed to say both, citing this very matter of fifty words as an arousal of mercy before the taking-out of the Sefer Torah. He resolved, bli neder (בלי נדר), to begin saying both the next day: fourteen seconds, and the Rishonim are clear.

IX. VaYehi BiNsoa HaAron

Rashi on the words of this verse explains why it is said as the Aron is opened.[29] He brings from the Midrash Tanchuma that when Israel traveled in the wilderness the ark went before them, a three days’ journey ahead. (One tends to picture the ark traveling with the camp; in fact it traveled in front, while the whole camp was surrounded by the clouds of glory.) Moshe Rabbeinu would pray, each time the ark moved: Kumah Hashem (קומה ה׳),[30] “rise up, Hashem,” and the Rashi has him add: stand and wait for us, and do not go farther from us. We want to stay near the Shechinah (שכינה).

The same is true when the Aron is opened to remove the Sefer Torah. We feel how far we are from the Shechinah and from the ark. We say these verses to relive the plea of Moshe Rabbeinu: stay close, do not distance Yourself. A Jew who says VaYehi BiNsoa HaAron should think: Master of the world, I want to cling (דבק) to the Torah. The Sefer Torah is leaving the Aron, traveling, as it were, from the ark to the bimah (בימה). The Torah is always ahead of us; we are always falling behind. Let us not fall so far behind; let us cling to the holy Torah. A beautiful kavanah for that moment.

Then Ki MiTziyon Teitzei Torah (כי מציון תצא תורה ודבר ה׳ מירושלים) is said because the Torah is going forth, out of the Aron toward the people. At the end of days, when all enemies are gone, Torah will go forth from Yerushalayim. We are not yet worthy of that fully, though those who live in Eretz Yisrael are closer to it; but we yearn for the full revelation.

X. Brich Shmei

From the Zohar

Brich Shmei (בריך שמיה) is a passage of the Zohar, and every word of it is in the Zohar.[31] No one in Israel said it until the Zohar was revealed. Before it was printed a few select individuals knew it from manuscript and said it; after the printing it spread rapidly, in the lecturer’s account, into almost every siddur. Sephardim say it. The Yekkes (German Jews) do not, which the lecturer says he had not known. In an aside he adds that some say Shabbsai Tzvi had a hand in adding it, and that this is not the time to discuss that.[32]

Where to Stand

The lecturer is asked whether it matters if one says Brich Shmei at one’s seat or near the Aron. Many in his shul go to the Aron, a beautiful custom, which he saw brought in a book of halachah (he thinks the Seder HaYom): there is a matter of going to the Aron in order to honor the Torah and show love for it. It is not halachah; one need not do it, but it is a fine thing. In a large shul it is impractical; if everyone went, there would be a traffic jam. The Mishnah Berurah discusses how far one accompanies the Sefer Torah, depending on where one stands.[33]

When to Say It

There is a real dispute in the Zohar itself, the lecturer says, whether it is to be said only on Shabbos and Yom Tov or also on Monday and Thursday, as we do. The Magen Avraham (מגן אברהם) writes that it is not said on Monday and Thursday, only on Shabbos, Yom Tov and Rosh Chodesh.[34] The reason, he says, is the words Brich Kitrach VeAtrach (בריך כתרך ועתרך), “blessed be Your crown”: the crown of God is mentioned only on the days when it is revealed, Shabbos and Yom Tov (compare Yisgadal Shemei Rabba in Kaddish). The Magen Avraham is an Ashkenazic authority, but apart from the Yekkes no one follows him here; all say it during the week, because the plain sense of the Zohar is that it is said whenever the Aron is opened.

As to the time within the opening, the Mishnah Berurah rules that one may say Brich Shmei until the Sefer Torah is placed on the bimah and opened for reading.[35] For one who is running behind, that is the latest opportunity.

The Parenthesis for Children

At the end of Brich Shmei, in many siddurim, there are words in parentheses: a prayer for male children who do the will of God (בנין דכרין). It is beautiful; why the parentheses? The Acharonim (אחרונים) explain, as the lecturer reports, that whatever stands in parentheses in the siddur is not “equal to every soul” (שווה לכל נפש). An unmarried young man: should he pray for children? The lecturer says that he himself began to pray for good children as a bachur, and that one should; it is a mistake to think that one begins to pray for a match at twenty-one or twenty-two. Pray now.

And what of an older man who can have no more children? Perhaps the right reading is that he asks not for new children but that the children and grandchildren he already has be doers of the will of God. On that reading the prayer fits everyone. The lecturer finds it hard to drop the words; they are in the Zohar; whoever can say them with kavanah should say them.

Ana Avda DeKudsha Brich Hu

The lecturer once attended a shalosh seudos (שלש סעודות) at which Rabbi Ram Shor, a rav in Flatbush, spoke of how special Brich Shmei is, and then put a question to the room, sharp mussar (מוסר). One says Ana Avda DeKudsha Brich Hu (אנא עבדא דקודשא בריך הוא), “I am a servant of the Holy One, blessed be He.” Truly? Are you a servant of the Holy One? One is about to declare it before the open Aron, with the Sefer Torah before him. What was he doing before the prayer? What will he do after?[36]

The speaker said that he does not say Ana Avda, though the Zohar has this text, but Ana Mevai LeMehevei Avda DeKudsha Brich Hu (אנא מבעי למהוי עבדא דקודשא בריך הוא), “I wish to become a servant of the Holy One”; he does not have the audacity to say that he is one. After that shalosh seudos the lecturer did not have the audacity to change the text. He still says Ana Avda, but cringes each time, and the words still echo in his ear. Who is he to say that he is a servant? At the least, when one says it, one should have in mind: help me earn the right to say these words; I want to be a true servant of the Holy One.

XI. The Proper Manner

Because all this is so significant, so great a moment of mercy, the Yesod VeShoresh HaAvodah (יסוד ושורש העבודה)[37] writes, as does the Chida, that everyone should be silent and have kavanah while these words are said. One should look at the Sefer Torah in the open Aron and try to arouse in oneself love for the holy Torah and love for those who learn Torah. Then one says LeTav LeChayim LeShalom Amen (לטב לחיים ולשלום אמן).

The discourse closed with the mishnah of Rabbi Chananya ben Akashya:[38]

רַבִּי חֲנַנְיָא בֶן עֲקַשְׁיָא אוֹמֵר: רָצָה הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא לְזַכּוֹת אֶת יִשְׂרָאֵל, לְפִיכָךְ הִרְבָּה לָהֶם תּוֹרָה וּמִצְוֺת, שֶׁנֶּאֱמַר: ה׳ חָפֵץ לְמַעַן צִדְקוֹ יַגְדִּיל תּוֹרָה וְיַאְדִּיר.
Rabbi Chananya ben Akashya says: The Holy One, blessed be He, wished to make Israel worthy; therefore He gave them much Torah and many mitzvos, as it is said: “Hashem desired, for His righteousness’ sake, to make the Torah great and glorious.”
Makkos 3:16; Yeshayahu 42:21

and with Kaddish.

Editorial Notes  ·  Open Questions

Items unresolved at the time of writing, to be settled with the lecturer or from the sources before printing. Recollections of reviewers are recorded as such and are not asserted in the text.

  1. The stanzas of Shomer Yisrael. The wording of the Sephardic stanza and of the Shelah’s verse (goy rabba) is to be checked in a printed siddur and in the Shaar HaShamayim and the Shelah’s will; the place of the piyyut in the Siddur Rashi; the printing details of the Shaar HaShamayim; and the source of the testimony about R’ Nosson Adler.
  2. The Maaseh Rav. The locus of the Gra’s practice of saying Shomer Yisrael only on a public fast is to be cited.
  3. Sitting for Shema. The siman of the Shulchan Aruch and the ground (kabbalah; kavod in the summary) are to be established.
  4. The Rambam on sitting after nefilas apayim. The halachah in Hilchos Tefillah is to be located.
  5. The Ach Pri Tevuah. The passage, the edition, and whether the words כי אם ראש חודש ושבת are his or the lecturer’s are to be confirmed; the author’s identification (Liska) follows the clearest recording.
  6. “The Philistines.” To be confirmed whether the lecturer’s gloss on bagedud has a source.
  7. The Tosafos citing Rabbeinu Manoach. The recording says Shabbos, the summary Chullin; the locus, and whether the Rishon is the commentator on the Rambam, are to be established; and the claim that little of his commentary survives.
  8. The Zohar. The locus (II, 206a) and the Aramaic are to be checked; the dating of the Zohar’s revelation (the lecturer said the 1400s) and the first printing (Mantua, not Turkey) are to be confirmed.
  9. The Chida and the Yesod VeShoresh HaAvodah. The works and simanim on silence at the opening of the Aron are to be cited.
  10. The Kesser Shem Tov. The passage on Brich Shmei is to be located.
  11. The Gra and Italy. The source of the attribution of Kel Erech Apayim to the chachmei Italia is to be located; it is not in the Biur HaGra to Orach Chaim 131 or 134 as available.
  12. The Roman rite and the Jerusalem museum. The particulars of the rite (HaNoten LaYaef Koach; the different Maariv and Emes VeEmunah), the date of 1951, and the town of the reconstructed Aron are to be confirmed.
  13. The Machzor Vitry. Whether the Machzor Vitry links the Monday and Thursday prayer to judicial error, or only to the likeness of the two courts, is to be established; the lecturer sees no direct reference.
  14. The Siddur HaRokeach. The passage on Kel Erech Apayim as an apology is to be located.
  15. The Avudraham. Where he says that saying only one version is an error, and which printing of the ArtScroll siddur notes that some say both, are to be located.
  16. The remark on Shabbsai Tzvi. The source and meaning of the remark are not given; it is recorded as an aside.
  17. Going to the Aron. The Seder HaYom and the passage of the Mishnah Berurah on accompanying the Sefer Torah are to be located.
  18. The Magen Avraham. The locus of the exclusion of weekday Brich Shmei, and of the reasoning from כתרך, is to be located; Orach Chaim 282:1 does not contain it.
  19. The saying of Rabbi Ram Shor. To be confirmed with the lecturer, and whether he wishes the rav to be named.
  20. The calendar. The lecturer calls the parshah of the veil “last week’s”; Chayei Sarah was read two Shabbosos before this discourse. The edition says only Chayei Sarah.
