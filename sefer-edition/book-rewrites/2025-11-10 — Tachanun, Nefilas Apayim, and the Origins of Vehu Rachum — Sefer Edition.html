תחנון, נפילת אפים, ומקורו של והוא רחום

Tachanun, Nefilas Apayim, and the Origins of Vehu Rachum
Studies in Tefillah (תפילה) — The Structure and History of Tachanun
Dated 10 November 2025 (19 Cheshvan 5786)  ·  Sefer Edition

This discourse was delivered לעילוי נשמת הרב חיים ז״ל on the occasion of his yahrzeit (יארצייט), and לעילוי נשמת all the souls of Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל). It was sponsored by Reb Azriel Dovid. [The full name of Rav Chaim, and the surname (given as Langer) and spelling of the sponsor’s name, to be confirmed before printing.]

This discourse examines the structure of Tachanun (תחנון) and the origins of its parts: the confession (vidui, וידוי) and the Thirteen Attributes of Mercy (שלש עשרה מידות) said after Shemoneh Esrei (שמונה עשרה); Nefilas Apayim (נפילת אפים) itself, originally optional and later accepted as an obligation; and the history of its words, including the verse Vayomer Dovid el Gad (ויאמר דוד אל גד), the controversy over the Kitzur Shelah (קיצור של״ה), and the work Shaarei Tzion (שערי ציון).

It then turns to Vehu Rachum (והוא רחום), the long supplication added on Mondays and Thursdays: the two opposed reasons for those days; the account, preserved by the Rishonim (ראשונים), of three sages adrift on the Mediterranean who composed it before a trial by fire; the name Azriel (עזריאל) discerned in its text; and a second tradition that traces the prayer to Chizkiyahu (חזקיהו) and the army of Sancheriv (סנחריב).

Where the lecturer reports a source he did not name, the report is marked as his; matters left unresolved are gathered at the end.

I. The Parts of Tachanun (תחנון)

Confession and the Thirteen Attributes After the Amidah

Tachanun is not a single unit. The confession (vidui, וידוי) and the Thirteen Attributes of Mercy (שלש עשרה מידות) said after Shemoneh Esrei (שמונה עשרה) form a section distinct from Nefilas Apayim (נפילת אפים) proper. In Nusach Sefard (נוסח ספרד) the Thirteen Attributes are said first, although the confession still stands at the head of the sequence.

The Zohar (זוהר) teaches that immediately after Shemoneh Esrei one recites the confession, so that the accusing angels (mekatrigim, מקטרגים) not block the prayer from ascending to the Kisei HaKavod (כסא הכבוד).[1] After the confession comes Kel Erech Apayim (קל ארך אפים), an ancient liturgical poem (piyyut, פיוט) that has always served as the introduction to the Thirteen Attributes in Selichos (סליחות) and on Yom Kippur (יום כיפור). Then the Thirteen Attributes of Mercy are recited.

It is important to grasp that this entire institution has no source in the Gemara (גמרא) or in basic halachah (הלכה). It exists because the Zohar instructs it, and the Arizal (אריז״ל) was exacting about it: he held that the confession and the Thirteen Attributes are to be said immediately after Shemoneh Esrei, and that the immediacy is essential.

II. The Source of the Thirteen Attributes

The Gemara in Rosh Hashanah

The Thirteen Attributes are familiar from Selichos. Their source is the well-known passage in Rosh Hashanah (ראש השנה) 17b.[2] After the sin of the golden calf (מעשה העגל), the Holy One told Moshe (משה), in effect, that in order to forgive Klal Yisrael He would teach him what to say. The Gemara prefaces its account with the statement of Rabbi Yochanan (רבי יוחנן) that, were it not explicitly written, it would be impossible to say it: the Holy One, as it were, wrapped Himself in a tallis (טלית) like a prayer leader and showed Moshe the order of prayer, saying that whenever Israel sins they are to recite it before Him and He will forgive them.

The Maharsha: A White Garment of Light

The Maharsha (מהרש״א) asks the obvious question: who wore a tallis? What does it mean that the Holy One wraps Himself in one? He answers from the verse recited before donning one’s own tallis each morning, עוטה אור כשלמה, “wrapped in light as in a garment” (תהלים ק״ד:ב׳). The Holy One did not wear a physical tallis; rather the Divine Presence (שכינה) was surrounded by a garment of light, and within it He taught the Thirteen Attributes of Mercy.[3] The Maharsha adds that this garment is white: he calls it טלית לבנה, the very garment in which He was wrapped at the creation of the world.

The lecturer professes not to know what a “white spiritual light” may be, but suggests, tentatively, that this may be the source of the care taken that a tallis be white and unsoiled. A soiled tallis is unseemly; the tallis of the Holy One, as it were, is white.

A Story Told in This Connection

A mother-in-law once watched from the women’s section as her son-in-law, who had come with her daughter from Eretz Yisrael (ארץ ישראל) for the festival, prayed beside her husband, and she saw that his tallis was stained and dirty. At the festive meal she was visibly upset. Pressed by her daughter, she said that she did not like the match: a man who wears a filthy tallis is neither neat nor clean. The daughter, who had never seen her husband from the women’s section, said that she would speak to him and have it cleaned. He replied that he had forgotten his own tallis in Eretz Yisrael and had borrowed his father-in-law’s. It was her own husband’s tallis that she had seen. The moral is to judge no one, even over a soiled tallis, and always to give the benefit of the doubt (לכף זכות). The lecturer adds that he has no licence to speak of the tallis of the Holy One; on that he can only cite the Gemara.[4]

III. Nefilas Apayim: Originally Optional

A Practice, Not an Obligation

Turning to Nefilas Apayim itself, the discourse recalls that many Chassidim (חסידים) are not strict about it and do not say Tachanun on a yahrzeit, a practice discussed at length in the previous discourse. One must understand that the entire institution of Nefilas Apayim is not, in origin, an obligation. It was originally only reshus (רשות). A search of the whole Talmud finds no source that obligates it.[5] The one Talmudic narrative in which a sage is described as falling on his face, that of Rabbi Eliezer (רבי אליעזר) in Bava Metzia (בבא מציעא) 59b, whose brother-in-law Rabban Gamliel (רבן גמליאל) died at the time, was treated in the earlier discourse; the lecturer cites it as showing the practice but not as showing an obligation.[6]

The lecturer cites a responsum of the Rivash (רי״בש) that states outright that it is only optional. This is the source of the practice, among many who are not Chassidim, of omitting Tachanun when there is a sufficient reason (how sufficient the reason must be is itself a wide-ranging dispute).

A member of the congregation noted a useful parallel: the evening prayer (Maariv, מעריב) was likewise originally optional, yet Klal Yisrael took it upon themselves as an obligation.[7] So, too, the Aruch HaShulchan (ערוך השלחן) writes that in the end Klal Yisrael accepted Nefilas Apayim as an obligation, and one is therefore bound to say it on the appropriate days.[8] It is clearly not said on Rosh Chodesh (ראש חודש) or on the other days specified in the Shulchan Aruch (שלחן ערוך), and, for a Chassid (חסיד) by his custom, not at all.

IV. A Hint in the Torah: V’es Ma’acha (ואת מעכה)

A matter omitted from the previous discourse bears on this theme and on what is read at the end of the Akeidah (עקדה). How does Parshas Vayeira (פרשת וירא) end? When the Akeidah is read on Rosh Hashanah, the portion that follows it is added. What is its connection to Rosh Hashanah? Why is it read on Rosh Hashanah at all?

One answer, which the lecturer calls “cute” and attributes to an authority whose name is not clear, is that the last two names in that passage are acronyms: מעכה stands for מלוך על כל העולם (“rule over all the world”), and תחש for the sounding of the shofar (שופר), whose words are not clear.[9] The Baal Shem Tov (בעל שם טוב) taught something else. Every name in the Torah (תורה) contains hints (remazim, רמזים). The words ואת מעכה, the Baal Shem Tov would say, are an acronym:

וידוי אחר תפילה מגיע עד כסא הכבוד.
A confession after prayer reaches the Throne of Glory.

This is worth remembering. There are hints in the Torah, as the Zohar already taught, that when one recites the confession after Shemoneh Esrei, one first pleads before the Holy One, and then nullifies himself: he falls and says that he is unworthy of anything, and cries חטאתי, עויתי, פשעתי, and the Holy One accepts the prayer. In earlier times the fall was literally to the ground; today one falls only on the head.

V. The History of the Words of Nefilas Apayim

The Old Text

The original Nefilas Apayim had no uniform text. Today one says רחום וחנון חטאתי לפניך, and then ה׳ אל באפך תוכיחני, which is chapter 6 of Tehillim (תהלים).[10] Formerly a different chapter was said; the lecturer reports that the Levush (לבוש) mentions a different chapter altogether.[11] The Zohar instructs that during Nefilas Apayim one say chapter 25 of Tehillim, in which David gives himself over to the Holy One: לדוד אליך ה׳ נפשי אשא, “I am going to surrender my soul to You.”[12]

The Danger

The Zohar warns that this may be said only with the proper intention (kavanah, כוונה). Without it there is danger that the Holy One will take a person before his time, without length of days (אריכות ימים). Many, seeing this in the Zohar, said: “I will not even open this chapter,” although it is the original source of Nefilas Apayim, because the danger is too great. To say אליך ה׳ נפשי אשא without intention is to tell the Holy One, “Take my soul.” It is meant to be self-sacrifice (mesiras nefesh, מסירות נפש); but self-sacrifice without the proper intention may result in the Holy One taking the person before his time. For this reason Klal Yisrael stopped saying it.

What the Sephardim and the Ashkenazim Did

The Sephardim (ספרדים) do say chapter 25, but without performing Nefilas Apayim; they say Tachanun and then Ledavid (לדוד), and they do not fall. In the lecturer’s words, the combination of the two chapters, 6 and 25, with the falling is too dangerous, and Nefilas Apayim itself is very dangerous. Among the Ashkenazim (אשכנזים), over the years, the printed siddur (סידור) substituted chapter 6, ה׳ אל באפך תוכיחני, a kind of confession by which one beseeches the Holy One. The shift from chapter 25 to chapter 6 was driven by this concern.

VI. The Custom at Minchah (מנחה)

The discourse raises a question about the afternoon prayer: why, in many congregations, is Tachanun not said at Minchah? The lecturer asked those present whether anyone said it; one said that he did, in a rite the lecturer could not identify, with whose members he had prayed many times without noticing it; the matter was left to be taken up later. The explanation he offered is tentative.

Tachanun is not said at night, and to say it then is dangerous: such recitations rouse the forces of impurity. (The Rishonim use the expression kitzetz banetiyos (קיצץ בנטיעות) for one who does what he should not do at the wrong time.)[13] Many pray Minchah late, and by the time they finish Shemoneh Esrei it is already after sunset (shkiah, שקיעה), when Tachanun may not be said. The congregations therefore cancelled it at Minchah in general, and even those who pray in the middle of the afternoon, far from sunset, refrain. The danger of Nefilas Apayim without the proper intention is real; performed with the proper intention it is of overwhelming power, and without it, dangerous.[14]

VII. ויאמר דוד אל גד — A Strange Verse

Has Anyone Wondered?

Has anyone, while saying Tachanun in the morning, been startled by the verse Vayomer Dovid el Gad (ויאמר דוד אל גד)? It is said; but what is it doing here? One can understand נפלה נא ביד ה׳, which speaks of falling. Yet the phrase says something different from what is done in Nefilas Apayim: נפלה נא ביד ה׳ means “let us fall into the hand of Hashem (ה׳),” not “before” Him. In Nefilas Apayim one falls before Him, not into His hand. What, then, is this verse doing in the prayer?

The Source in Shmuel II

The verse comes from the very last chapters of Shmuel II (שמואל ב׳), in chapter 24.[15] King David sent Yoav (יואב), his general, to count Klal Yisrael from north to south, a census. Jews are not counted (the reason is a sugya (סוגיא) unto itself, and a serious one, which the discourse does not enter); David erred in counting them. Gad (גד החוזה), the prophet of David’s days, came to him and said: you have done a terrible thing; the Holy One offers you three options, choose your punishment.

  1. Seven years of famine in Eretz Yisrael.
  2. Three months of an enemy in pursuit, with terrible troubles, pogroms or whatever they might be.
  3. Three days of plague (דבר).

David answered, and this is the verse: each of the punishments is very hard, but he preferred to fall into the hand of the Holy One and not into the hand of man. Were he to choose famine, he would be in the hand of man, for the wealthy would corner the market and sell at a much higher price; were he to choose an enemy in pursuit for three months, that too is in the hand of man. He preferred to be punished by the Holy One; let there be a plague among Klal Yisrael. And indeed seventy thousand died in those three days, a mortality beyond that of any epidemic of recent memory.

What Is It Doing in Tachanun?

Who placed this verse in the prayer books? Here it becomes important to research the source of every prayer. There is a serious possibility that this passage is a Sabbatean addition. The lecturer adds that this is not meant to alarm anyone, and that one who says it need not stop; but the trail leads, perhaps, somewhere that deserves to be known.

VIII. The Kitzur Shelah (קיצור של״ה) and a Hidden Gematria

Who Yechiel Michel Epstein Was

There was a Jew named Yechiel Michel Epstein (יחיאל מיכל עפשטיין). He is not the well-known Michel Epstein of the Aruch HaShulchan; there are many Michel Epsteins in Jewish history. The lecturer places this one in the early seventeenth century.[16] He wrote the Kitzur Shelah, an abridgment of the Shelah (של״ה), Shnei Luchos HaBris (שני לוחות הברית) of Rabbi Yeshayah Horowitz (רבי ישעיה הלוי הורוויץ), one of the most important works on the Jewish bookshelf; the lecturer describes the abridger as coming some fifty to seventy years after the Shulchan Aruch. The Shelah is beautiful to study but very long, and an abridged version was a useful thing to have. Yet one who opens the Kitzur Shelah finds many things that the original does not mention. That anomaly did not escape one of the most relentless defenders of orthodoxy in Jewish history.

Rabbi Yaakov Emden and Toras HaKenaos

Rabbi Yaakov Emden (רבי יעקב עמדן), the great opponent of the surviving followers of Shabsai Tzvi (שבתי צבי) in the generations after his apostasy, wrote an entire work, Toras HaKenaos (תורת הקנאות) (the lecturer gives the title from memory), documenting Shabsai Tzvi and his disciples and showing how their influence persisted in Klal Yisrael.[17] Rabbi Yaakov Emden had the eyes of a hawk. Whenever a new book appeared he examined at once its introduction and its approbations (haskamos, הסכמות); if he detected even a faint scent of Shabsai Tzvi he responded with a sharp and fearless zeal (kanaus, קנאות).

The Discovery

When the Kitzur Shelah appeared, the lecturer reports, Rabbi Yaakov Emden obtained a copy, opened it, and at once issued a public proclamation (kol koreh, קול קורא): no one may bring this book into his home. The author, he held, was a Sabbatean, a heretic who believed himself bound to the false messiah. His proof: in the introduction (hakdamah, הקדמה) the author refers to Shabsai Tzvi. Yet one who opens the introduction finds no explicit mention of Shabsai Tzvi anywhere. What did Rabbi Yaakov Emden see?

The Hidden Gematria

The answer requires extreme attention; one must be a Yaakov Emden to catch it. In the brief introduction the author concludes, in the lecturer’s paraphrase, with a prayer: that by learning this book Israel may merit to see the true Messiah (משיח האמיתי) and may merit also the days of the Messiah (ימות המשיח). On the surface this is entirely proper. But two small marks, apostrophe-like signs, stood over those words. Wherever such a mark is seen, it is a sign to the careful reader to analyze the word, by calculating its numerical value (gematria, גימטריא) or by reading its initials as an acronym. The lecturer reports that the numerical value of the phrase is exactly 814, which is the value of שבתי צבי. Rabbi Yaakov Emden, in the lecturer’s telling, was at once convinced: “This is Sabbatean. Remove this book from my home; no one should buy it.”[18]

The Impact on Our Prayer

The verse Vayomer Dovid el Gad that is said in Tachanun, specifically because it contains the words נפלה נא ביד ה׳, originates, so the lecturer reports, from that very work. Therefore the Gaon of Vilna (הגאון מווילנא) and, as heard, the Maaseh Rav (מעשה רב) hold that one should not say it, and many others concur.[19] This is why in some Ashkenazic siddurim (סידורים) the passage appears in small print or in parentheses: not everyone says it.

The lecturer relates that he said it himself as a child, because he said everything printed in the siddur; later he decided otherwise. The community at large still says it, and many congregations maintain the custom. That its source may be Sabbatean does not necessarily invalidate the words. The Gaon gave a further reason for omitting it: it speaks of a plague, of calamity, and one prefers not to invoke such things before Tachanun. Another famous work by an Italian kabbalist also omits it.[20] The practice varies. In most printed siddurim it appears, and one who wishes to say it has a source, though the source is questionable.

IX. Nasan Nata Hanover and Shaarei Tzion

There was a Jew named Nasan Nata Hanover (נתן נטע הנובר), best known for one major work, Yeven Metzulah (יוון מצולה). He lived through Tach VeTat (תח״ת), the persecutions of Bogdan Chmielnicki in 1648–1649, when the Cossacks swept through Poland and killed, in the lecturer’s figure, between twenty and fifty thousand Jews. Many fled, and he was among them. He was an eminent rabbi from western Ukraine; as an Ashkenazic Jew he made his way to Italy, to Venice, where he met a kabbalist who was a disciple of Rabbi Chaim Vital (רבי חיים ויטאל), the chief disciple of the Arizal. The kabbalist took such a liking to him that he became his student and himself a kabbalist.[21]

Yeven Metzulah is the most important historical source on the events of Tach VeTat. (The Shach (ש״ך), who was also there, wrote his own, Megillas Eifah (מגילת איפה).)[22] But Hanover wrote another work as well, Shaarei Tzion (שערי ציון).[23] In it he records that many of the prayers he transmits were received from his teacher, Rabbi Chaim HaKohen (רבי חיים הכהן) of Aleppo (חלב) in Syria, who also ended up in Italy, and he writes that he sets them down so that Klal Yisrael should say them.

Remarkably, almost every prayer book today contains these prayers, though it is not known how they spread so universally. Which prayers? Those said when the ark is opened on Shabbos (שבת), on festivals, and on Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur, which all come from Shaarei Tzion. All say them with reverence, and people pay substantial sums for the privilege of opening the ark. Yet, the lecturer says, none of these prayers existed in Klal Yisrael before the disciple of the disciple of the Arizal decided to teach them; he recorded them in his book and somehow they spread like wildfire. Every prayer book includes them, as well as the prayer said before entering the sukkah (סוכה), the prayer before taking the lulav (לולב), and many others. The lecturer adds that the verse Vayomer Dovid el Gad is also found there, though he suspects that it comes from the Kitzur Shelah, which preceded Shaarei Tzion by a little, and that this is why many do not say it.

X. Vehu Rachum: The Prayer of Monday and Thursday

Why Monday and Thursday at All?

On Monday and Thursday, in the synagogue, Vehu Rachum is said, והוא רחום יכפר עון, followed by a long supplication running all the way to the Shema (שמע ישראל) said every day. Why on these two days? What is special about them? When fasts are kept, they are kept on Monday and Thursday. The Torah is read on Monday and Thursday; the Gemara in Bava Kama (בבא קמא) 82 teaches that this was an ordinance (takanas Ezra, תקנת עזרא).[24] The Rishonim give two reasons, and they truly oppose each other.

The First View — Days of Mercy

Some Rishonim hold that Monday and Thursday are days of mercy (rachamim, רחמים). Moshe ascended Har Sinai (הר סיני) to receive the second tablets on a Thursday and descended forty days later on a Monday, and that day was Yom Kippur. Monday and Thursday therefore became days of mercy forever. If they are days of mercy, one asks for more: one adds prayers to the morning service, and stays an extra few minutes to say Vehu Rachum.

The Second View — Days of Judgment

Other Rishonim hold exactly the opposite: Monday and Thursday are days of judgment (din, דין), because one of Ezra (עזרא)’s ordinances was that every city have a court sitting on Monday and Thursday. One who studies Kesubos (כתובות) knows this from its first Mishnah (משנה).[25] When there is judgment below, there is judgment above; the lecturer has this from the sources (the Gemara in Shabbos 129b gives it as the reason that bloodletting is not done on these days). And precisely because there is judgment above, one must ask for more mercy, and so the additional supplications are added. It is a curious dispute: either days of mercy or days of judgment. But where did this prayer come from?

Standing and Sitting

The lecturer notes in passing that Vehu Rachum is to be said slowly and, according to the Shulchan Aruch, standing; the Mishnah Berurah (משנה ברורה) is cited as permitting one to sit, though it is better to remain standing, which is the plain sense of the Shulchan Aruch. One may not interrupt it with other matters.[26]

XI. The Story Behind Vehu Rachum

An Ancient Prayer From the Times of the Tanaim

The story has several versions and is brought by many Rishonim, among them the Kol Bo (כל בו) and the Rokeach (רוקח).[27] This is one of the most ancient prayers in the prayer book, and most people say it twice a week without knowing where it came from. It comes from the times of the Tanaim (תנאים).

The Three Ships

After Titus (טיטוס) destroyed the Second Temple many Jews were exiled; the accounts of how differ. According to one, Titus sent ships out into the Mediterranean with no sailors and no means of navigation, only sails, לכל רוח, wherever the wind would take them. Three full ships of men and women drifted on the sea. One ended up in Portugal, another in Italy, another in Africa; the versions disagree on the exact places.

The King’s Test

The ship that came ashore (let us say in Portugal) was met by the king. “Where do you come from?” “We are Jews.” “What are Jews?” “We are the descendants of Avraham Avinu (אברהם אבינו).” Hundreds of men and women, strangers from a foreign land: “What are you doing here?” “We were exiled when Titus destroyed the Temple.” These were Tanaim, Jews of two thousand years ago.

The king said: “You are the children of Avraham Avinu? I know a little of the Bible. We have just read of Avraham Avinu thrown into the furnace of Ur Kasdim (אור כשדים) and emerging unscathed. Let us see whether you can do the same. If I throw you into the fire and you come out like your forefather, then you are indeed his descendants and you are welcome in my land; I will give you homes, real estate, vineyards, fields and farms, whatever you want. If not, I will send you back on the ship.”

Three Days of Prayer and Fasting

The three greatest of the sages on that ship gathered. “What are we to do? We are not Avraham Avinu. This does not happen every day.” They sat and prayed, and fasted for three days, and during those three days the three sages composed Vehu Rachum:

  1. One wrote from והוא רחום until אין כמוך לחנון ורחום.
  2. The second wrote from אין כמוך until ה׳ אלוקי ישראל שומע תרועת עמך.
  3. The third wrote the remaining part to the end.

They recited it over and over.

The Dream of the Yerushalmi

On the third day the king demanded that one of them be thrown into the fire. They asked one another what each had dreamed in the past three nights. The first two had nothing of significance to report. The third, the Kol Bo writes, was a great man from Jerusalem (Yerushalmi, ירושלמי) but not a scholar in the strict sense, for he did not know the Bible well. (One who does not know Tanach (תנ״ך) is not called a talmid chacham (תלמיד חכם).) “I dreamed a verse.” “Which verse?” “I cannot remember the verse. All I remember is that the word כי occurred twice and the word לא three times.”

Today a search would produce it in seconds. But the scholars among them said at once: “I have it,” right on the spot. The verse is in Yeshayahu (ישעיהו) 43:[28]

כי תעבור במים אתך אני ובנהרות לא ישטפוך, כי תלך במו אש לא תכוה ולהבה לא תבער בך.
When you pass through the waters I am with you, and through the rivers they shall not overwhelm you; when you walk through fire you shall not be burned, and the flame shall not consume you.

Two times כי, three times לא. (These are also the words of a well-known melody.) They said to him: “You are the one we are choosing to throw into the fire, because you had the dream. As the prophet promises, you will be cast into fire and will not burn.” He answered: “All right, no problem.”

The Miracle

They brought him before the king and threw him into the fire. The fire split; he walked in, and he walked out, unharmed. The king declared: “You are indeed the children of Avraham Avinu.” All the Rishonim tell the story as a nes (נס) that took place soon after the destruction of the Temple.

The Letter to the Communities

The king accepted them, and gave them a place to live, money and real estate. They settled there and Judaism flourished, whether in Portugal or in Africa, it is not certain. After the event they sent letters to all the communities of Klal Yisrael: “We did this. We composed this prayer, Vehu Rachum, and said it over and over, and it helped us; the king accepted us and the man was saved. Therefore we ask all of Klal Yisrael to say it.” All the communities accepted it upon themselves. That is why Vehu Rachum is said twice every week, asking the Holy One to save us from exile and to have mercy. It is a beautiful, deeply moving, crystal-clear prayer, so important that one may not interrupt it; and it comes from before the Rishonim, from the Tanaim themselves. The names of the three Tanaim are even known, though in different versions, and the lecturer does not enter into them.

XII. The Insertion of Rabbi Azriel

In Vehu Rachum there is one piece that does not come from the three Tanaim: the line ה׳ אלוקי ישראל שומע תרועת עמך ורחם על עדת עמך, the beautiful refrain at the end of each stanza. Who wrote it? Rabbi Azriel, it is said, and his name can be seen in it. The words עוזרנו אלוקי ישענו על דבר כבוד שמך (taken from Tehillim 79:9) contain the letters of עזריאל: the letters עזר of עוזרנו, the letters אל of אלוקי, and the yud (י) of ישענו.[29] There was a Jew by that name, one of the Rishonim, and one tradition holds that he composed this addition and that it thus became part of Vehu Rachum.

XIII. The Prayer of Chizkiyahu

There is, however, another view of where the body of the prayer comes from. The Rishonim say that in the days of Sancheriv, king of Assyria, when he laid siege to Yerushalayim (ירושלים), King Chizkiyahu woke in the middle of the night, went to the Temple and prayed for two hours without stopping; and the entire army fell, one hundred and eighty-five thousand soldiers.[30] What did Chizkiyahu say that could fell so great an army? The answer, the lecturer reports, is in the initials: one stanza of Vehu Rachum spells out חזקיהו. Chizkiyahu said this prayer; and when it is said on Monday and Thursday, the prayer of Chizkiyahu is recalled, and the Holy One is asked that all the enemies of Klal Yisrael fall as the army of Sancheriv fell.

Summary of the Discourse

Three strands run through the discourse. The confession and the Thirteen Attributes before Nefilas Apayim are kabbalistic in origin, instituted by the Arizal on the authority of the Zohar as a defense against the accusing angels. Nefilas Apayim itself was originally optional, taken on by Klal Yisrael as an obligation, accompanied by real danger when said without intention and by great power when said with it. And Vehu Rachum, the long supplication of Monday and Thursday, is traced to three sages adrift on the Mediterranean, completed by the name of Rabbi Azriel, and linked by a second tradition to the prayer of Chizkiyahu. The discourse breaks off with more to be said.
