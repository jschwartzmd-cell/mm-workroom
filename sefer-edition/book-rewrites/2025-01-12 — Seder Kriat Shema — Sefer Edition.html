סֵדֶר קְרִיאַת שְׁמַע וְהִלְכוֹתֶיהָ

Seder Kriat Shema and Its Halachos
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Sunday Morning Series on Tefillah
Recorded 12 January 2025  ·  Sefer Edition · 23 August 2026

Presented in honor of the birth of Yehuda Meir, grandson of Reb Tzvi’el — that he grow to be a light for Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל) and bring his family much nachas (נחת).

The subject before us is the architecture of Kriat Shema (קריאת שמע): the reasoned order of its three parshiyos (פרשיות), the inclusion of Parshas Tzitzis (פרשת ציצית) though it be a separate mitzvah, the principle of someich geulah litefillah (סומך גאולה לתפילה), and the doctrine of the ramach teivos (רמ״ח תיבות) — the 248 words. Around this last principle turns one of the longest and most consequential machalokos in the standard siddur: the closing call of the shaliach tzibbur (שליח ציבור) at the conclusion of the parshiyos. What follows traces that architecture from the arrangement of the three parshiyos through the sustained machlokes over the words Hashem Elokeichem Emes (ה׳ אלקיכם אמת).

I. The Order of the Three Parshiyos

The Formal Structure

The halachos of Kriat Shema stand upon a settled premise: the recitation twice each day is a mitzvah d’oraysa (מצוה דאורייתא) according to most of the rishonim (ראשונים).[1] Its liturgical form was fixed by Chazal (חז״ל), who arranged the three parshiyos in a specific sequence:

  1. Shema V’ahavta (שמע ואהבת) — Devarim 6.
  2. V’haya Im Shamoa (והיה אם שמע) — Devarim 11.
  3. Parshas Tzitzis — Bamidbar 15.

The third of these is, in its own right, a distinct mitzvah: its essence is zeicher yetzias Mitzrayim (זכר יציאת מצרים) — the daily mitzvah, morning and night, to remember the Exodus — and it is on this ground, as will be seen, that it attaches to the two preceding parshiyos.

The Gemara’s Question

Were the order of Scripture itself governing, Parshas Tzitzis would come first, for it stands in Parshas Shelach, well before Va’eschanan and Eikev. Why then is Shema recited first, followed by V’haya Im Shamoa, and only afterward Tzitzis?

The Gemara in Berachos raises this precise question,[2] and Rabbi Yehoshua ben Korchah (רבי יהושע בן קרחה) — one of the great tannaim (תנאים) — supplies the foundational answer:

תְּחִילָּה יְקַבֵּל עָלָיו עוֹל מַלְכוּת שָּמַיִם, וְאַחַר כָּך יְקַבֵּל עָלָיו עוֹל מִצְוֹת.
First one accepts upon himself the yoke of the Kingdom of Heaven; only afterward does he accept upon himself the yoke of the mitzvos.
Berachos 13a

The Reason: Kingship Precedes Command

The first parshah, Shema V’ahavta, is kabbalas ol malchus shamayim (קבלת עול מלכות שמים): the declaration that there is one Elokim (אלוקים), and the acceptance of the Kadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא) as King. V’haya Im Shamoa is kabbalas ol mitzvos (קבלת עול מצוות): the promise of berachos for one who listens, of kelalos for one who does not. The King is accepted first; only then are His gezeiros received.

The Ramban (רמ״בן) in Parshas Yisro, on the verse Anochi Hashem Elokecha, adduces the well-known Mechilta (מכילתא) that formulates the identical logic:[3] when the servants approached the king saying, “We will fulfill all your decrees,” the king answered them, “First proclaim that you accept my kingship; only afterward will I issue my decrees.” This is the sequence of the Aseres HaDibros (עשרת הדברות) — Anochi precedes all — and it is the sequence of Kriat Shema.

The Origin of “Shema Yisrael”

The very words Shema Yisrael (שמע ישראל) reach back to the deathbed of Yaakov Avinu (יעקב אבינו). The Gemara in Pesachim[4] records that Yaakov, apprehensive that perhaps one of his sons might resemble Eisav (עשו) or Yishmael (ישמעאל), sought to reveal the end of days — the keitz (קץ). The shevatim (שבטים), sensing his concern, called out with one voice:

שְׁמַע יִשְׂרָאֵל ה׳ אֱלֹקֵינוּ ה׳ אֶחָד.
Pesachim 56a

They were addressing their father, whose name was Yisrael: “Listen, our father Yaakov — Hashem Elokeinu Hashem Echad. You have nothing to fear; the Kadosh Baruch Hu is One for us as He is for you.” These were the words of Shema Yisrael spoken before Moshe Rabbeinu (משה רבינו) inscribed them in the Torah.

To Whom the Declaration Is Addressed

When we say Shema Yisrael, the rishonim understand that we are addressing the people gathered around us in shul. The recitation is a daily eidus (עדות) — a formal act of testimony — and an eidus requires those who hear. Twice each day, the yid (יהודי) turns to the community of Klal Yisrael around him and declares: “You who are part of Yisrael — know that Hashem Elokeinu Hashem Echad.”

II. Why Tzitzis Belongs Within Kriat Shema

The Five Yesodos of Rabbi Yehudah bar Chaviva

If Tzitzis is an independent mitzvah, upon what basis is it folded into Kriat Shema?

The Gemara in the opening chapter of Berachos[5] brings the teaching of Rabbi Yehudah bar Chaviva (רבי יהודה בר חביבא) — the only halachah in all of Shas (ש״ס) transmitted in his name, so that even his classification as tanna or amora is uncertain — that Parshas Tzitzis contains five foundational yesodos (יסודות):

  1. Mitzvas tzitzis itself.
  2. Zechiras kol ha’mitzvos (זכירת כל המצוות) — looking at the tzitzis recalls all the mitzvos. Rashi (רש״י) frames the arithmetic: eight chutin (חוטין) and five kesharim (קשרים), together with the numerical value tzitzis = 600, yielding the taryag mitzvos (תרי״ג). The Ramban, citing the Gemara in Menachos,[6] adduces the classical chain of visual associations: techeiles resembles the sea, the sea resembles the sky, the sky resembles the kisei ha’kavod (כסא הכבוד).
  3. V’lo saturu acharei levavchem (ולא תתורו אחרי לבבכם) — guarding against minus (מינות).
  4. V’acharei eineichem — shemiras einayim (שמירת עיניים).
  5. Zechiras yetzias Mitzrayim.

The Rambam and the Kesef Mishneh

The Rambam, at the opening of Hilchos Kriat Shema,[7] gives precisely this as the reason for the order: Parshas Tzitzis houses both kabbalas ha’mitzvos and zechiras kol ha’mitzvos, so that its recitation completes the sequence begun by malchus and mitzvos. The Kesef Mishneh (כסף משנה) adds a deeper consideration: the same parshah houses zechiras yetzias Mitzrayim, which flows directly into the berachah of V’hotzianu me’eretz Mitzrayim (והוציאנו מארץ מצרים) that follows. From Kriat Shema through Tzitzis and on into Emes VeYatziv, the liturgy is one continuous hemshech (המשך).

III. Someich Geulah LiTefillah

The Simple Ground

That Kriat Shema stands immediately before Shemoneh Esrei (שמונה עשרה) is not merely a matter of chronological precedence. Kriat Shema is mid’oraysa; tefillah, at least in its fixed times, is mid’rabbanan (מדרבנן) according to all rishonim. On that ground alone, d’oraysa precedes d’rabbanan. But the deeper connection is set forth by the Gemara in Berachos:[8]

אֵיזֶהוּ בֶּן עוֹלָם הַבָּא — כָּל הַסּוֹמֵך גְּאוּלָּה לִתְפִּלָּה.
Who is one who acquires the World to Come? He who joins geulah to tefillah.
Berachos 4b

The Covenantal Flow

The simple reading traces a continuous covenantal arc. One has accepted the Kadosh Baruch Hu as King, accepted His mitzvos, declared zechiras yetzias Mitzrayim in Parshas Tzitzis and again in the berachah of V’hotzianu me’eretz Mitzrayim — and by these declarations one has stood as makir tovah (מכיר טובה), His servant. How does the eved (עבד) express his avdus? Through avodah (עבודה): korbanos in their day, tefillah in ours. Someich geulah litefillah is thus the daily rededication of the yid as eved Hashem; from that rededication flows bitachon (בטחון) — the emunah that the Kadosh Baruch Hu answers tefillah.

The Ritva’s Caveat

The Ritva (ריטב״א) on Berachos[9] tempers the formula. Wherever Chazal employ the phrase eizehu ben olam ha’bah, the intent is never that one mechanical action alone acquires the World to Come. Were that so, a person of thoroughly corrupt character who happened to be someich geulah litefillah would inherit olam ha’bah. Rather, the meaning is mi’tzad ha’da’as (מצד הדעת): one careful with this detail is the sort of yid who is medakdek in the words of the chachamim across the board, and it is that quality of character that opens the gates of the World to Come.

The Older Minhag of Kriat Shema at Minchah

The very first Tosefta in all of Shas, in Maseches Berachos,[10] preserves the trace of a minhag no longer observed: the recitation of Kriat Shema at Minchah (מנחה) as well. Kabbalas ol malchus shamayim would then have been performed three times daily — at Shacharis, Minchah, and Maariv. In our day, the recitation of Ashrei (אשרי) before Minchah is all that survives; but the older minhag attests to the gravity that the earlier generations assigned to semichas geulah litefillah.

IV. The Doctrine of Ramach Teivos

The Teaching of the Zohar HaKadosh

The Zohar HaKadosh (זוהר הקדוש)[11] establishes that Kriat Shema must contain exactly ramach — 248 — teivos, corresponding to the ramach evarim (רמ״ח אברים) of the human body. The correspondence is not merely symbolic. In the metaphysics of the Zohar, each evar of the body has its counterpart in the spiritual architecture of the neshamah (נשמה); when the parshiyos are recited with kavvanah, the 248 words draw down healing and vitality to the 248 limbs, and Kriat Shema becomes a refuah (רפואה) for the whole person. The classical gematria associates the word romach (רומח) — the spear taken up by Pinchas — with the ramach of Kriat Shema:[12] the daily recital of the parshiyos is itself a weapon of kedushah raised against the forces that pursue the yid.

Everything that follows — the addition of words for the yachid, the chazan’s repetition for the tzibbur, and the centuries-long machlokes over the closing call — rests upon this premise. The 248-word count is not a stringency of pious arithmetic; it is me’akev in the eyes of the mekubalim, a condition upon whose satisfaction the spiritual efficacy of the recitation depends.

The Counting Problem

When one counts the words of the three parshiyos as they appear in the Torah, one arrives at only ramah — 245. Three words are missing. From this shortfall, the entire structure of what follows unfolds.

For the Yachid: Kel Melech Ne’eman

The Ramban, followed by others,[13] institutes that the yachid (יחיד) davening alone prepends the three words Kel Melech Ne’eman (אל מלך נאמן) to Shema — the Meiri (מאירי) recording a machlokes concerning the precise mechanism — and thereby reaches the required 248. In a tzibbur (ציבור), the three words are omitted, because the missing count is supplied through the chazan.

For the Tzibbur: The Chazan’s Repetition

In the tzibbur, the missing three words are furnished by the shaliach tzibbur’s aloud repetition of Hashem Elokeichem Emes at the conclusion of Kriat Shema. By virtue of shomea ke’oneh (שומע כעונה), one who listens carefully to those three words is credited as though he himself had said them — and the count is brought to exactly 248.

The Shaarei Teshuvah (שערי תשובה)[14] gives a related directive of practical weight: if one is in the midst of his own recitation of Kriat Shema when the shaliach tzibbur reaches Hashem Elokeichem Emes, he should pause his own recitation, listen carefully to the three words, and then continue. This pause is not accounted a hefsek (הפסק), for the listening itself is the very means by which the count is completed.

A Story of Rav Chaim Kanievsky, Zt’l

Rav Chaim Kanievsky, zt’l (רבי חיים קנייבסקי), was makpid each morning to recite Kel Melech Ne’eman in order to complete the ramach. After some time he came to realize that the shaliach tzibbur at his minyan (מנין), out of respect, was waiting for him each day — and only after the Rav had finished was he calling out Hashem Elokeichem Emes aloud. In consequence, Rav Chaim was adding not three extra words but six. So great was his humility that the realization was slow in coming; once it did, the arrangement was quietly corrected. The story illustrates both the arithmetic mechanics of the ramach and the exacting self-awareness that the doctrine demands.

Rav Ben Tzion Abba Shaul on Repeating Words

Rav Ben Tzion Abba Shaul, zt’l (רבי בן ציון אבא שאול) — rosh yeshivah of Porat Yosef and one of the leading poskim (פוסקים) of the Sephardic world in the twentieth century — rules in his Shu”t Or LeTziyon (שו״ת אור לציון)[15] that one who realizes mid-Kriat Shema that he lacked kavvanah upon a particular word does not repeat that word — with one exception, the first pasuk (Shema Yisrael Hashem Elokeinu Hashem Echad), which is me’akev. To repeat any other word would push the count past 248; the arithmetic must be exact. This psak is a striking illustration of how far-reaching the Zohar’s revelation became in the fabric of practical halachah: the count is treated not as an ornament but as a hard constraint upon the form of the tefillah.

V. Connecting Emes to Elokeichem

The Ruling of the Shulchan Aruch

The Shulchan Aruch,[16] in Siman 61, rules that the yachid, at the conclusion of Kriat Shema, appends the word Emes, saying Ani Hashem Elokeichem Emes (אני ה׳ אלקיכם אמת) — even though the word Emes does not appear at the close of Parshas Tzitzis in the Torah. What is the source?

The Gemara in Berachos 13a derives the addition from a verse in Yirmiyahu:[17]

וַה׳ אֱלֹקִים אֱמֶת.
And Hashem, God, is Truth.
Yirmiyahu 10:10

Because Scripture itself binds the divine Name to Emes, when we recite Ani Hashem Elokeichem the word Emes is immediately attached.

The Accounting for the Yachid and for the Tzibbur

The arithmetic is the mechanical heart of the entire subsequent machlokes, and is worth setting out with clarity. Consider the running count in the tzibbur-side case:

StageWords addedRunning total
The three parshiyos as written in the Torah245245
The yachid’s addition of Ani Hashem Elokeichem…+3248
…continuing to Emes (derived from Yirmiyahu)+1249

At first glance the count now stands one too many. The classical resolution of the Beis Yosef (בית יוסף) is that Ani Hashem Elokeichem is not itself counted for the ramach from the yachid’s silent recitation; rather, in the tzibbur-mechanism, the yachid reaches only 246 with Emes attached (245 from the parshiyos plus Emes), and the two words Hashem Elokeichem supplied aloud by the chazan bring the count to exactly 248. The terminal Emes called out by the chazan belongs, in this understanding, not to the closing of Kriat Shema but to the opening of the berachah of Emes VeYatziv (אמת ויציב) that follows. This is the very hinge on which the great machlokes of the next chapter turns.

VI. The Shaliach Tzibbur’s Silent Recitation: The Machlokes

The Ruling of the Shulchan Aruch

The Shulchan Aruch rules that the shaliach tzibbur, in his silent Kriat Shema, likewise says Ani Hashem Elokeichem Emes — and afterward repeats Hashem Elokeichem Emes aloud for the tzibbur. On the surface, the arithmetic appears untenable: would he not then arrive at 249?

The Objection of the Rama miPano

The Rama miPano (רמ״ע מפאנו) — Rabbeinu Menachem Azaryah miPano, one of the great mekubalim of sixteenth-century Italy[18] — issues an unusually forceful protest. In his understanding, the Shulchan Aruch’s arrangement cannot stand. To avoid the count of 249, the shaliach tzibbur must say in his silent recitation only Hashem Elokeichem; the words Hashem Elokeichem Emes he calls out solely aloud, after the tzibbur has finished, and only then does he reach exactly 248. So decisively did the Rama miPano issue this ruling that he called for the correction of the Shulchan Aruch at this point. The Magen Avraham (מגן אברהם)[19] transmits this view, and the Gra (גר״א) was makpid upon it.[20]

Rav Moshe Feinstein’s Defense of the Shulchan Aruch

Rav Moshe Feinstein, zt’l, addresses the Rama miPano’s objection in a teshuvah in Shu”t Igros Moshe (אגרות משה).[21] His resolution rests on the essential character of the Gemara’s derashah in Yirmiyahu: the binding of Elokim to Emes is not a stylistic embellishment but a chiyuv. It follows that the shaliach tzibbur also must say Ani Hashem Elokeichem Emes in his silent recitation. When he subsequently calls out Hashem Elokeichem Emes aloud, the terminal Emes is to be understood bekavvanah as the opening of Emes VeYatziv, not as a word of Kriat Shema. His silent count is thus exactly 248; the aloud Emes functions as a semantic bridge into the succeeding berachah.

This resolution places a certain phenomenological demand upon the shaliach tzibbur. He is asked, in the span of moments, to say the same word twice — once silently as part of Kriat Shema, once aloud as part of Emes VeYatziv — and to sustain the interior distinction in kavvanah. The position of the Gra avoids the necessity of this mental shift by omitting Emes from the silent recitation altogether. Yet Rav Moshe’s resolution has become the practiced minhag in the great majority of communities, on the strength of the derashah’s essential force. Both positions are recorded in the poskim as living halachah; a community follows its mesorah.

VII. The Older Sephardic Minhag

The Beis Yosef’s Ruling on “Ani Hashem Elokeichem”

The Beis Yosef in Orach Chaim, Siman 61 Se’if 3,[22] brings from Sefer HaPliah (ספר הפליאה) — the mysterious kabbalistic work whose companion volume is Sefer HaKanah (ספר הקנה), and often attributed to the tanna who composed the piyut Hineni He’ani MiMa’as (הנני העני ממעש) recited on the Yamim Nora’im (ימים נוראים) and at the Hoshanos (הושענות) — a different arrangement altogether. According to this older mesorah, when the shaliach tzibbur concludes Kriat Shema, the closing call is not Hashem Elokeichem Emes but rather Ani Hashem Elokeichem — three words drawn directly from the body of Parshas Tzitzis, completing the ramach from within the parshah itself.

The Beis Yosef’s Memory of Cairo

In an unusually personal passage, the Beis Yosef recalls an episode from his years in Mitzrayim. In Cairo, a shaliach tzibbur once concluded Kriat Shema with Ani Hashem Elokeichem according to the older minhag, and the kahal answered Emes VeYatziv. The Rav of the beis knesses rose mid-tefillah and cried out: “You cannot do this. Where did you bring such a minhag from? The Zohar teaches explicitly that the ramach is completed only with Hashem Elokeichem Emes.”

The Beis Yosef’s teacher, the Mahar”i bei Rav (מהר״י בי רב),[23] held likewise. The Mahar”i bei Rav is the celebrated talmid chacham who sought to renew the semichah (סמיכה) in Klal Yisrael after the centuries-long break — an enterprise whose halachic consequence would have been immense, but which did not prevail against the opposition of his contemporaries. Some hold that he conferred semichah upon the Beis Yosef; the Beis Yosef upon the Alshich (אלשיך); and the chain of transmission continued for a generation or two thereafter. It is proposed by some that this participation in the renewed shalsheles ha’semichah (שלשלת הסמיכה) helps to account for the singular authority the Shulchan Aruch came to command in Klal Yisrael.

Rabbeinu Yitzchak ibn Ghiyyat and the Shelah HaKadosh

Rabbeinu Yitzchak ibn Ghiyyat of Lucena (רבי יצחק אבן גיאת)[24] — the great eleventh-century rishon of Andalusian Spain — offers a sober reason for rejecting the closing of Ani Hashem Elokeichem: were the shaliach tzibbur to call out Ani and then, immediately, Hashem, the phrasing could sound, chas v’shalom, as though he were proclaiming “I am God.” Better that he close with Hashem Elokeichem Emes. Rabbeinu Yeshaya HaLevi Horowitz, the Shelah HaKadosh (של״ה הקדוש),[25] transmits this consideration in the name of Rabbeinu Yitzchak ibn Ghiyyat, and it is widely cited.

VIII. The Tashbatz and the Rashbash: A Sephardic Counter-Tradition

The Pera’os of Kanav (1391) and the Setting of the Tashbatz

To locate the Tashbatz in his time: Rabbeinu Shimon ben Tzemach Duran (רבי שמעון בן צמח דוראן) was a great rav in Barcelona who fled the city in 1391, a full generation before the reign of Ferdinand and Isabella, in the massacre of that year known in our historical literature as the pera’os of קנ״א (5151). The pattern of the years preceding the massacre bears remembering. Every Monday and Thursday, another theological debate was staged between chachmei Yisrael and the Catholic clergy; the Ramban, the Ba’alei Tosafos (בעלי תוספות) of earlier generations, and other gedolim had all in their turn borne this burden. When the tension finally overflowed, the Catholic populace entered the Jewish quarter of Barcelona and slaughtered its families. The expulsions of 1492 would be the culmination; but the dispersion of Spanish Jewry to North Africa and the Middle East began in the aftermath of the events of 1391.

The Tashbatz in Algiers

The Tashbatz[26] fled to Algiers, where he served as one of its great dayanim (דיינים) and authored more than nine hundred teshuvos. He was a fifth- or sixth-generation descendant of the Ramban, and preserved a distinct family mesorah — including minhagim in Kriat Shema that differ from ours. He ruled that the shaliach tzibbur should conclude with Ani Hashem Elokeichem, and not with Hashem Elokeichem Emes.

The Rashbash: Continuity of the Mesorah

His son, Rabbeinu Shlomo ben Shimon Duran — the Rashbash (רשב״ש)[27] — succeeded his father as rav of Algiers, and is cited repeatedly by the Beis Yosef, who rules like him in many places. The Rashbash defended his father’s minhag with a structural argument that will prove central to what follows: the words that complete Kriat Shema to ramach teivos must come from Kriat Shema itself; the word Emes belongs, of its own right, to Emes VeYatziv, not to the closing of Kriat Shema. The Rashbash also authored, at his father’s request, an entire sefer called Milchemes Mitzvah (מלחמת מצוה), a systematic refutation of the arguments of a notorious apostate of the age; the story of that sefer’s composition is a chapter in the intellectual history of the pre-expulsion era of European Jewry.[28]

IX. The Maharshal and the Re’em

The Maharshal of Lublin

The Maharshal — Rabbeinu Shlomo Luria of Lublin (מהרש״ל מלובלין)[29] — writes in his teshuvos that as a yachid, he himself davened with Ani Hashem Elokeichem rather than Hashem Elokeichem Emes, for the same structural reason that governs the Rashbash: ramach teivos must come from within the body of Kriat Shema. A significant Ashkenazic voice, then, stood with the older minhag.

The Re’em — Rabbeinu Eliyahu Mizrachi

Rabbeinu Eliyahu Mizrachi — the Re’em (רא״מ)[30] — author of the classical peirush upon Rashi al ha’Chumash printed in standard chumashim, opens his Shu”t with two teshuvos dedicated to refuting the Ani Hashem Elokeichem minhag. His defense of our minhag proceeds from a kabbalistic principle: the words supplied to complete ramach must be sheimos ha’Kadosh Baruch Hu (שמות הקדוש ברוך הוא). The word Ani is not a Name; the phrase Hashem Elokeichem Emes is. We therefore yield Ani, though it stand within Kriat Shema proper, in favor of the divine Names.

Here, then, are two competing theories of what ramach teivos in fact is: for the Rashbash and the Maharshal, the requirement is structural — the completing words must belong to Kriat Shema; for the Re’em, the requirement is onomastic — the completing words must be Names. Each theory yields its practical psak. The practiced minhag of the majority of Klal Yisrael today, following the Beis Yosef, aligns with the onomastic theory of the Re’em: the divine Names are given precedence over the structural principle. The older Sephardic mesorah preserves the structural intuition and reaches an opposite psak. The machlokes is thus not merely over words but over the very metaphysical foundation of the count.

X. Synthesis

The full architecture of Kriat Shema stands at the intersection of four currents:

  1. The structural seder. Malchus precedes mitzvos; Tzitzis closes the unit because it houses the embedded zechiras yetzias Mitzrayim, which flows in turn into the berachah that follows.
  2. The covenantal flow. Kriat Shema is eidus; semichas geulah litefillah is the daily rededication of the yid as eved Hashem.
  3. The numerical structure. Ramach teivos as refuah for the ramach evarim; the addition of Kel Melech Ne’eman for the yachid; the chazan’s repetition for the tzibbur.
  4. The mesorah of the conclusion. The centuries-long machlokes over Hashem Elokeichem Emes versus Ani Hashem Elokeichem, settled lema’aseh by the Beis Yosef, defended by the Re’em on kabbalistic grounds, and reinforced in our day by Rav Moshe Feinstein, zt’l.

The tefillah that a yid recites each morning is thus the deposit of two thousand years of layered mesorah. Each detail — the order of the parshiyos, the folding of Tzitzis into the unit, the exact count of 248, the precise formulation of the chazan’s closing call — is a record of Chazal, of the rishonim, and of the acharonim teaching us how to be eved Hashem twice each day. What appears on the surface as a fixed liturgical unit is in fact a living compression of Sinaitic covenant, prophetic derashah, Zoharic revelation, and generations of psak halachah — each word bearing its history, and each history bearing the words up.

מראה מקומות  ·  Mareh Mekomos

Each note runs short citation first (for quick lookup), then an expanded elaboration. Footnotes run sequentially with the body text.

  1. Status of Kriat Shema as mid’oraysa: Rambam, Hilchos Kriat Shema 1:1; Sefer HaMitzvos, mitzvah 10. — The Rambam counts the twice-daily recitation of Kriat Shema among the mitzvos aseh of the Torah. The Ramban famously disputes and treats the recitation as mid’rabbanan, holding that only the first pasuk is a Torah-level obligation; the majority of rishonim side with the Rambam. The dispute has direct consequences for questions of doubt (safek) in Kriat Shema. ↩
  2. Berachos 13a — the baraisa of Rabbi Yehoshua ben Korchah. — תְּחִילָּה יְקַבֵּל עָלָיו עוֹל מַלְכוּת שָּמַיִם, וְאַחַר כָּך יְקַבֵּל עָלָיו עוֹל מִצְוֹת. The foundational baraisa establishing the order of the three parshiyos: Shema (malchus) → V’haya Im Shamoa (mitzvos) → Parshas Tzitzis. ↩
  3. Mechilta d’Rabi Yishmael, Parshas Yisro on Shemos 20:2; Ramban ad loc. — The famous Mechilta: when servants approached the king saying, “we will fulfill all your decrees,” the king answered, “first proclaim that you accept my kingship; then I will issue my decrees.” The Ramban uses the Mechilta to anchor the malchus-before-mitzvos structure that governs both the Aseres HaDibros and Kriat Shema. ↩
  4. Pesachim 56a. — Yaakov Avinu’s deathbed scene: his concern that a son might resemble Eisav or Yishmael; the shevatim’s simultaneous declaration of Shema Yisrael Hashem Elokeinu Hashem Echad, addressed to their father Yisrael; the textual origin of the daily eidus, prior to Moshe Rabbeinu’s writing of the parshah. ↩
  5. Berachos 12b — Rabbi Yehudah bar Chaviva. — The single halachah in Shas transmitted in his name (his classification as tanna or amora is uncertain). The five yesodos of Parshas Tzitzis: (1) mitzvas tzitzis; (2) zechiras ha’mitzvos; (3) v’lo saturu acharei levavchem, against minus; (4) v’acharei eineichem, shemiras einayim; (5) zechiras yetzias Mitzrayim. ↩
  6. Menachos 43b; Ramban al ha’Torah, Parshas Shelach. — Techeiles resembles the sea, the sea resembles the sky, the sky resembles the kisei ha’kavod: the classical chain of visual associations by which tzitzis operates as zechiras ha’mitzvos. The Ramban cites the Gemara in Menachos in his treatment of the mitzvah. ↩
  7. Rambam, Hilchos Kriat Shema, ch. 1; Kesef Mishneh ad loc. — The Rambam’s stated reason for the order of the parshiyos: Parshas Tzitzis combines kabbalas ha’mitzvos with zechiras kol ha’mitzvos. The Kesef Mishneh adds that the deeper reason Chazal built Parshas Tzitzis into Kriat Shema is the shared theme of zechiras yetzias Mitzrayim, which continues directly into the berachah of V’hotzianu me’eretz Mitzrayim. From Kriat Shema through Emes VeYatziv, the liturgy is one hemshech. ↩
  8. Berachos 4b — eizehu ben olam ha’bah — kol ha’someich geulah litefillah. — The Gemara’s structural anchor for the placement of Kriat Shema immediately before Shemoneh Esrei: the daily covenantal flow from kabbalas malchus through zechiras yetzias Mitzrayim into the avodah of tefillah. The amora who walked through his days in joy by means of semichas geulah litefillah. ↩
  9. Chiddushei HaRitva on Berachos 4b. — Critical qualification of the eizehu ben olam ha’bah formula: it is not mechanical. Were a person of thoroughly corrupt character to be someich geulah litefillah, he would not thereby inherit the World to Come. The Gemara’s meaning is that one who is careful in this particular detail is medakdek b’divrei chachamim across the board, and that quality is what earns olam ha’bah. ↩
  10. Tosefta Berachos, ch. 1. — The opening Tosefta of Shas: an ancient minhag, no longer preserved, of reciting Kriat Shema at Minchah as well — kabbalas ol malchus shamayim three times daily. The recitation of Ashrei before Minchah in our day is what remains of the practice. ↩
  11. Zohar HaKadosh — ramach teivos in Kriat Shema as refuah for the ramach evarim (general reference; principal locus in Parshas Va’eschanan). — The Zoharic doctrine that Kriat Shema contains exactly 248 words corresponding to the 248 evarim of the body. In the metaphysics of the Zohar, each evar stands in structural correspondence with a spiritual counterpart, so that the recitation of the parshiyos with kavvanah operates as a tikkun and refuah for the whole person. The doctrine drives the entire subsequent halachic apparatus: Kel Melech Ne’eman for the yachid, the chazan’s repetition for the tzibbur, and the machlokes over the closing call. ↩
  12. Bamidbar 25:7 — “va’yikach romach be’yado”; classical kabbalistic association of romach with ramach. — The word romach (רומח) — the spear taken up by Pinchas — carries the same three consonants as ramach (רמ״ח = 248), and the classical kabbalistic reading associates the two: the daily recitation of the 248 words of Kriat Shema is itself a weapon of kedushah. (The connection to Kriat Shema is a Zoharic/midrashic reading rather than a plain-peshat gloss of the Targum.) ↩
  13. Ramban on Parshas Shema; cf. Meiri on Berachos; Tur and Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chaim 61. — The institution that the yachid davening alone prepends the three words Kel Melech Ne’eman to Shema in order to complete the count to 248. Omitted in a tzibbur, where the chazan’s repetition supplies the missing words. The Meiri records a machlokes regarding the precise mechanism. ↩
  14. Shaarei Teshuvah, Orach Chaim 61 — R’ Chaim Mordechai Margolius. — The directive that one mid-Kriat Shema when the shaliach tzibbur reaches Hashem Elokeichem Emes should pause his own recitation to listen attentively, then continue. Not accounted a hefsek: the listening is itself the means by which the count is completed via shomea ke’oneh. R’ Chaim Mordechai Margolius, author of the Shaarei Teshuvah, is often associated in siddur-lore with the family of R’ Zalman Margolius of Lvov. ↩
  15. Rav Ben Tzion Abba Shaul, Shu”t Or LeTziyon (general reference; hilchos Kriat Shema). — Rav Ben Tzion Abba Shaul (1924–1998), rosh yeshivah of Porat Yosef and one of the leading Sephardic poskim of the twentieth century (not to be confused with Rav Ovadyah Yosef, zt’l). His Sephardic psak: if one realizes mid-Kriat Shema that he lacked kavvanah upon a word, he does not repeat it — except for the first pasuk (Shema Yisrael Hashem Elokeinu Hashem Echad), which is me’akev. Repeating any other word would push the count past ramach. The psak is a striking testimony to how far the Zoharic ramach teivos shapes practical halachah. (An aside: on the related Yorah Deah question — whether a shaliach tzibbur’s waiting for the Rav to finish Kriat Shema could constitute shochad — the accepted psak is that the waiting is minhag, not shochad.) ↩
  16. Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chaim, Siman 61. — The codified halachos of Kriat Shema: the yachid says Ani Hashem Elokeichem Emes at the close; the shaliach tzibbur, in his silent recitation, does likewise, then repeats Hashem Elokeichem Emes aloud. The locus of the machlokes with the Rama miPano below. ↩
  17. Yirmiyahu 10:10; Berachos 13a. — V’Hashem Elokim Emes: the verse binding Elokim to Emes. The Gemara derives from this pasuk the basis for attaching Emes immediately to Ani Hashem Elokeichem at the close of Kriat Shema. ↩
  18. R’ Menachem Azaryah miPano (the Rama miPano), Asarah Ma’amaros / Alfasi Zuta. — Sixteenth-century Italian mekubal; issues a sharp objection to the Shulchan Aruch’s psak that the shaliach tzibbur should say Ani Hashem Elokeichem Emes in his silent recitation. His psak: only Hashem Elokeichem in silent Kriat Shema; Hashem Elokeichem Emes only aloud, after the tzibbur has finished. Otherwise the chazan would have 249 words. ↩
  19. Magen Avraham, Orach Chaim 61. — Transmits the Rama miPano’s position; one of the principal acharonic vehicles for the alternative practice. ↩
  20. Ma’aseh Rav — the practices of the Vilna Gaon. — The Gra was makpid upon the Rama miPano’s psak: only Hashem Elokeichem in silent Kriat Shema. Communities that follow the Gra preserve this practice. ↩
  21. R’ Moshe Feinstein, Shu”t Igros Moshe (general reference; the teshuvah on ramach teivos). — Defense of the Shulchan Aruch’s psak. The binding of Elokim to Emes derived from Yirmiyahu 10:10 is essential; therefore the shaliach tzibbur must also say Ani Hashem Elokeichem Emes silently. The aloud Emes is taken bekavvanah as the opening of Emes VeYatziv, not as a word of Kriat Shema — thus preserving exactly 248. The practiced minhag in most communities. ↩
  22. Beis Yosef, Orach Chaim, Siman 61, Se’if 3 — citing Sefer HaPliah and its companion Sefer HaKanah. — The Beis Yosef brings the older minhag of closing Kriat Shema with Ani Hashem Elokeichem (drawn from the body of Parshas Tzitzis) to complete ramach from within the parshah; recalls the personal episode in Cairo where the Rav of the beis knesses rose mid-tefillah to protest a chazan using this minhag; sides with Hashem Elokeichem Emes on the strength of the Zohar. Sefer HaPliah and Sefer HaKanah are the companion kabbalistic works of the same corpus, often quoted together by the Beis Yosef. ↩
  23. Source-type note (biographical / historical): Mahar”i bei Rav (R’ Yaakov Beirav), c. 1474–1546 — Spanish-born, Algerian rav, eventually rav of Tzfas; teacher of the Beis Yosef. Famous for his attempt to renew semichah in Klal Yisrael after the centuries-long break — an enterprise that did not prevail against the machlokes of his contemporaries (notably the Ralbach). Some hold that he conferred semichah upon the Beis Yosef; the Beis Yosef then upon the Alshich; the chain continued for a generation or two thereafter. The renewal hypothesis is offered by some as one explanation for the unique authority commanded by the Shulchan Aruch.
    ↩
  24. Rabbeinu Yitzchak ibn Ghiyyat of Lucena (11th-century Andalusia). — The pragmatic argument against Ani Hashem Elokeichem: were the shaliach tzibbur to call out Ani followed by Hashem, the phrasing could sound, chas v’shalom, as though he were proclaiming “I am God.” Preferable that he close with Hashem Elokeichem Emes. A simple consideration that complements the kabbalistic argument. ↩
  25. The Shelah HaKadosh (Rabbeinu Yeshaya HaLevi Horowitz), Shnei Luchos HaBris; transmitting Rabbeinu Yitzchak ibn Ghiyyat’s reasoning. — The Shelah cites, in the name of Rabbeinu Yitzchak ibn Ghiyyat, the “sounds like ‘I am God’” argument as a widely-cited reinforcement of Hashem Elokeichem Emes against the older Ani Hashem Elokeichem minhag. Rabbeinu Yitzchak ibn Ghiyyat lived in Lucena c. 1038–1089; the Shelah lived c. 1555–1630; the transmission is one of citation across the centuries, not correspondence. ↩
  26. The Tashbatz (R’ Shimon ben Tzemach Duran, 1361–1444), Shu”t Tashbatz. — A great rav of Barcelona who fled in the pera’os of קנ״א (5151 / 1391 CE) to Algiers, where he became dayan and authored more than 900 teshuvos. A fifth/sixth-generation descendant of the Ramban; preserves a distinct family mesorah, including the minhag for the shaliach tzibbur to close Kriat Shema with Ani Hashem Elokeichem rather than Hashem Elokeichem Emes. Not to be confused with the Italian Tashbetz (R’ Shimshon ben Tzaddok, of Maharam MiRotenburg’s circle). ↩
  27. The Rashbash (R’ Shlomo ben Shimon Duran), son of the Tashbatz; Shu”t Rashbash. — Successor to his father as rav of Algiers; cited repeatedly by the Beis Yosef, who rules like him in many places. Defends the family minhag with the structural argument: the words that complete ramach must come from within Kriat Shema itself; the word Emes belongs to Emes VeYatziv, not to Kriat Shema. ↩
  28. Source-type note (biographical / historical): The Rashbash’s Sefer Milchemes Mitzvah was written at his father’s request as a systematic refutation of the arguments of an apostate of the age — in tradition called HaMagdef (המגדף): the former talmid chacham Yehoshua HaLorki, who followed his teacher R’ Shlomo HaLevi of Burgos (later Pablo de Santa Maria, appointed Bishop of the city in which he had served as rav) into conversion, and thereafter proceeded to attempt the conversion of thousands of others. The Beis Yosef’s Cairo memory and the composition of Milchemes Mitzvah both belong to the same historical arc — the fifteenth-century upheaval of Spanish Jewry — whose spiritual dangers, in the Rebbe’s cautionary aside, warrant care even when the material is engaged for the sake of refutation. (Corresponding scholarly literature exists on the wife of Shlomo HaLevi, who remained an agunah until her death; her family origins and the details of the case await verification against the specific published sources.)
    ↩
  29. The Maharshal (R’ Shlomo Luria of Lublin), Shu”t Maharshal. — As a yachid, the Maharshal davened with Ani Hashem Elokeichem rather than Hashem Elokeichem Emes — siding structurally with the Rashbash: ramach teivos must come from within the body of Kriat Shema. A significant Ashkenazic voice for the alternative minhag. ↩
  30. Rabbeinu Eliyahu Mizrachi (the Re’em), Shu”t HaRe’em — opening two teshuvos on Kriat Shema. — Author of the classical peirush on Rashi al ha’Chumash printed in standard chumashim. His Shu”t opens with two responsa dedicated to refuting the Ani Hashem Elokeichem minhag. From a kabbalistic standpoint, the words supplied to complete ramach must be sheimos ha’Kadosh Baruch Hu: Ani is not a Name; Hashem Elokeichem Emes is. We therefore yield Ani in favor of the divine Names. ↩
