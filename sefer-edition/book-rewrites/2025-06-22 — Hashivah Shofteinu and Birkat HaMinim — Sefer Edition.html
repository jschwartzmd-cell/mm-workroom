הָשִׁיבָה שׁוֹפְטֵינוּ וּבִרְכַּת הַמִּינִים

The Berachah of Judges and the Nineteenth Berachah — Malchus, Mishpat, and the Separation from the Minim
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Sunday Morning Series on Tefillah
Recorded 22 June 2025  ·  Sefer Edition

Sponsored by Reb Abish and Mrs. Mendel upon the commemoration of תח"ת (1648), on which day a community was spared the sword of the Crusades and established half a day as a תענית and half as a יום טוב. לעילוי נשמת אסתר בת ינקל שלום and her relatives who perished על קידוש השם in Auschwitz, and לעילוי נשמת Reb Mulch Yitzchak Akiva (Reb Tommy Herman) and his sisters, aged four and seven, killed in Auschwitz — השם ינקום דמם. זמן תשובה for יהודי הונגריה.

Two consecutive berachos of Shemoneh Esrei stand at the heart of the bakashos for the ge’ulah. The first bears a signature found nowhere else in the Amidah; the second was never part of the original eighteen at all.

I. The Structural Place and the Restoration of Sanhedrin

The berachah of Hashivah Shofteinu (הָשִׁיבָה שׁוֹפְטֵינוּ) sits within the sequence of petitions for the future ge’ulah that begins with Teka b’Shofar (תְּקַע בְּשׁוֹפָר). The order is itself the order of the ge’ulah: the Ribbono Shel Olam gathers the exiles; the great shofar sounds; and the Sanhedrin (סַנְהֶדְרִין) is reconstituted so that mishpat (מִשְׁפָּט) may once again be delivered under malchus (מַלְכוּת). Three times each day Klal Yisrael places the request upon its lips: Hashivah shofteinu k’varishonah, v’yo’atzeinu k’vatechilah — restore our judges as in the beginning and our counselors as of old.

The Historical Precedent — The Mahari Beirav

The most consequential historical attempt to reconstitute the Sanhedrin was undertaken in sixteenth-century Tzefas by the Mahari Beirav (מהר"י בי רב), a contemporary of the Beis Yosef and one of his teachers.[1] The halachic ground of the attempt was the ruling of the Rambam in Hilchos Sanhedrin that the unanimous consent of the chachmei Eretz Yisrael may reconstitute the broken chain of semichah (סְמִיכָה). In the year 1538 the Mahari Beirav acted upon this ruling and conferred semichah upon a small circle of talmidim. The Ralbach (רלב"ח), then Rav of Yerushalayim, opposed the project on halachic grounds, and the semichah chain was not effectively renewed. Contemporary attempts to reconstitute a Sanhedrin in Eretz Yisrael have had still less success. The berachah, accordingly, remains active as a daily petition.

II. The Unique Chasimah — The Only “Melech”

Rav Saadia Gaon (רַב סַעֲדְיָא גָּאוֹן) is credited with the observation that Hashivah Shofteinu is the single berachah in all of Shemoneh Esrei whose chasimah (חֲתִימָה) contains the word melech (מֶלֶךְ): Baruch Atah Hashem, Melech oheiv tzedakah u’mishpat (בָּרוּךְ אַתָּה ה’ מֶלֶךְ אוֹהֵב צְדָקָה וּמִשְׁפָּט).[2] The observation is the gateway to the Tur’s famous kushya.

The Halachic Background

The Gemara in Berachos[3] establishes the foundational din of every berachah: kol berachah she’ein bah hazkaras ha’Shem u’malchus einah berachah (כל ברכה שאין בה הזכרת השם ומלכות אינה ברכה) — without the divine Name and without malchus, the formula is structurally not a berachah. There is a single exception: berachah ha’semuchah la’chaverta (בְּרָכָה הַסְּמוּכָה לַחֲבֶרְתָּהּ) — a berachah whose opening adjoins the chasimah of the berachah before it borrows the preceding malchus and requires no new one of its own. The middle berachos of Shemoneh Esrei are the canonical application of this exception: none opens with Baruch Atah Hashem Elokeinu Melech ha’Olam, each resting upon the chasimah of the one before it.

The Tur’s Kushya

Upon this background the Tur raises his question in Siman 118.[4] Every middle berachah of Shemoneh Esrei sits inside the chain of berachos ha’semuchos. Magen Avraham, Mechayei HaMeisim, HaKel HaKadosh — none bear “melech” in the chasimah; each relies on the chain. The phrase Melech oheiv tzedakah u’mishpat contains the word melech within the body of the chasimah itself, something no other middle berachah does. Why, then, did the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah single out this berachah and insert melech into its closing formula?

III. The Answer in the Name of Rabbeinu Yechiel

The Tur — Rabbeinu Yaakov Ba’al HaTurim, son of the Rosh — brings the answer from his oldest brother. It is uncharacteristic of the Tur to cite a sibling by name within the Arba’ah Turim, and the citation itself is a mark of regard: my older brother רַבֵּינוּ יְחִיאֵל spoke of this with my father many times in my presence. The Rosh is known to have had several sons — Rabbeinu Yechiel, the oldest; Rabbeinu Yaakov, the Tur; Rabbi Yehudah, author of Zichron Yehudah; and others — and the oldest, Rabbeinu Yechiel, received the family’s unanimous recognition as its outstanding scholar, succeeding his father on the rabbinate of Toledo.[5]

A Measure of Rabbeinu Yechiel’s Stature

Very little of Rabbeinu Yechiel’s Torah survives independently of the Tur’s citations in his father’s name. Perhaps the clearest trace of his stature is a mesorah preserved by the Chida in his Birkei Yosef on Hilchos Kibbud Av va’Em.[6] The Chida addresses the question whether a father must rise for his son when the son has reached the madreigah of a great talmid chacham — zakein in Kiddushin being read as zeh kanah chachmah (זֶה קָנָה חָכְמָה). His decisive evidence is mesorah: the Rosh, gadol ha’dor of his time, would rise fully each time his son Rabbeinu Yechiel entered the room. The Torah of Rabbeinu Yechiel stood at that height.

The Yesod — Melech b’Mishpat Ya’amid Aretz

The answer Rabbeinu Yechiel received from the Rosh is rooted in a pasuk in Mishlei:[7]

מֶלֶךְ בְּמִשְׁפָּט יַעֲמִיד אָרֶץ, וְאִישׁ תְּרוּמוֹת יֶהֶרְסֶנָּה.
A king through mishpat establishes the land; a man of exactions destroys it.
Mishlei 29:4

Mishpat is specifically the middah that only a melech can deliver. True mishpat requires an authority above all parties, uncontested, with the power to enforce. In this berachah — and in this berachah alone among the middle berachos — the structural requirement of the content requires malchus to be present in the chasimah itself. The general rule of berachah ha’semuchah is not displaced by the chachomim’s insertion; the insertion rather signals that the subject-matter of this berachah cannot stand without an explicit malchus.

The Load-Bearing Reach of the Yesod

The yesod is load-bearing well beyond the uniqueness of the chasimah. During Aseres Yemei Teshuvah the chasimah is modified to HaMelech ha’mishpat (הַמֶּלֶךְ הַמִּשְׁפָּט) — a sharpening by which the Melech Himself is understood to be presiding over the mishpat of the Yamim Nora’im. If one erred and said the year-round Melech oheiv tzedakah u’mishpat, the Rama[8] rules that he need not return. The reason follows directly from Rabbeinu Yechiel’s yesod: the year-round chasimah already carries melech, satisfying the structural requirement of the season b’dieved. The Aruch, working from older nuschaos in which the year-round chasimah did not include melech, holds that one must repeat.[9] The pesak follows the Rama. The Aruch’s stricter view bears witness, from the opposite direction, to the same reality: the yesod that mishpat structurally requires malchus was already pressing on the nusach of this berachah in the gaonic period.

A parallel observation deepens the pshat. During Aseres Yemei Teshuvah, the chasimah of HaKel HaKadosh is likewise modified to HaMelech HaKadosh, and omission requires repetition. Year-round, mishpat alone carries melech in its chasimah; kedushah does not. During the Yamim Nora’im, both do. The asymmetry suggests that mishpat is operational-of-malchus in a way kedushah is not: a king’s kedushah is a predicate; his mishpat is the exercise of his sovereignty. The Rabbeinu Yechiel yesod therefore does load-bearing work year-round for one berachah and seasonally for a second, and the two together sharpen the pshat rather than complicate it.

IV. The Pasuk in Yeshayahu and the Rambam’s Mechanism

The language of the berachah is drawn almost verbatim from the pasuk in Yeshayahu[10] read as the haftarah of Chazon on the Shabbos before Tishah b’Av:

וְאָשִׁיבָה שׁוֹפְטַיִךְ כְּבָרִאשׁוֹנָה, וְיֹעֲצַיִךְ כְּבַתְּחִלָּה.
And I will return your judges as in the beginning, and your counselors as of old.
Yeshayahu 1:26

The Anshei Knesses HaGedolah made a precise and telling change. Yeshayahu speaks in the first person of the Ribbono Shel Olam: v’ashivah — and I will return, in the future. The berachah turns the pasuk around and places it as a petition in the mouth of Klal Yisrael: hashivah — return. The havtachah of the navi becomes the daily bakashah of the mispallel, who thereby participates in the fulfillment of what the navi has already assured. The pasuk’s subject becomes the berachah’s addressee.

The Rambam — Sanhedrin Before Mashiach

The Rambam in his Perush ha’Mishnah on Perek Chelek[11] develops two principles with direct bearing upon the berachah. First: the precise sequence of events in yemos ha’Mashiach is unclear in the sources of Chazal; theories conflict, and no single pesak as to order is forced. Second, and more decisively: the arrival of Mashiach ben David structurally requires a functioning Sanhedrin. The Sanhedrin must therefore precede the arrival of Mashiach, not follow it. The Rambam’s position is not uncontested — the Raavad and others in different parts of the literature allow the possibility that Mashiach himself will restore semichah — but it is the Rambam’s pesak upon which the Mahari Beirav’s project rested, and it is the position that gives Hashivah Shofteinu its full operational force: the berachah is not a wish but the pursuit of a precondition.

In his Hakdamah to the Perush ha’Mishnah, the Rambam traces the chain of Torah she’b’al peh through a particular link crucial to the mechanism: Achiyah ha’Shiloni (אֲחִיָּה הַשִּׁילוֹנִי), navi in the days of David and Shlomo, received the mesorah from his teachers; Eliyahu ha’Navi (אֵלִיָּהוּ הַנָּבִיא) received from Achiyah. The chain of semichah, broken in the external world across the long galus, is thus preserved intact in the person of Eliyahu. When Eliyahu returns, he arrives with semichah — and is thereby able to confer it, reopening the chain. V’ashivah shofetayich is spoken by the One who has already arranged the mechanism of its fulfillment.

V. Rav Schwab — V’Haser Mimenu Yagon va’Anachah

The chasimah is preceded by a two-word phrase whose connection to the restoration of judges is not immediately transparent: v’haser mimenu yagon va’anachah (וְהָסֵר מִמֶּנּוּ יָגוֹן וַאֲנָחָה) — and remove from us יָגוֹן and אֲנָחָה. Yagon is the deepest grade of grief; anachah is its outward expression in sighing. What is the connection between the restoration of the Sanhedrin and the removal of this particular pair?

Rav Shimshon Schwab, zt”l,[12] develops the answer in Iyyun Tefillah. The Gemara in Yevamos[13] derives from lo tisgodedu (לֹא תִתְגֹּדְדוּ) — read in its peshat as a prohibition against self-laceration in mourning — an additional issur: lo sa’asu agudos agudos (לֹא תַעֲשׂוּ אֲגֻדּוֹת אֲגֻדּוֹת), do not divide into separate halachic factions. In the absence of a central authority, Klal Yisrael inevitably fragments. Every beis ha’knesses develops its own minhagim; every kehillah its own pesak; every chug its own da’as Torah. The Gemara records that in the generations before the talmidim of Shammai and Hillel there were no major machlokesos in Klal Yisrael; a single path of avodas Hashem was walked by all. The fragmentation that followed is a mazkeres of the loss of central authority, and the mazkeres itself is the yagon and the anachah. The chasimah of the berachah is not an addendum to the request for judges; it is the operational heart of the request. We ask that the fragmentation be dissolved under one Sanhedrin and one malchus beis David.

The Kavvanah

Rav Schwab therefore instructs that this berachah be davened with a specific kavvanah: Ribbono Shel Olam — we are scattered, divided, broken into אֲגֻדּוֹת אֲגֻדּוֹת. Dissolve the יָגוֹן and the אֲנָחָה of our scatteredness; return us to the unity of סַנְהֶדְרִין and מַלְכוּת בֵּית דָּוִד. The three-step argument — mishpat requires melech; melech presupposes a Sanhedrin under his authority; the absence of both is the yagon itself — is the structural reading of the berachah entire.

VI. Derech Agav — The Chet ha’Meraglim and the Chisaron of Leaderlessness

The reading of Rav Schwab is anchored, by a path not immediately obvious, in parshas ha’shavua. The meraglim (מְרַגְּלִים) describe themselves in their report with a pasuk that Chazal treat as foundational:

וַנְּהִי בְעֵינֵינוּ כַּחֲגָבִים, וְכֵן הָיִינוּ בְּעֵינֵיהֶם.
We were in our own eyes as grasshoppers, and so we were in their eyes.
Bamidbar 13:33

The Kotzker Rebbe’s famous reading[14] extracts a mussar point from the sequencing: because they saw themselves as chagavim (חֲגָבִים), they were seen that way by others. The self-perception is causally prior. “If you look at yourself as a חָגָב, you are a חָגָב.”

Rashi’s Rendering — Nemalim

The pasuk says chagavim, grasshoppers. Rashi, however, renders nemalim (נְמָלִים) — ants. The source is the Gemara in Sotah:[15] the anakim in the vineyards, observing the meraglim, remarked that they looked like insects (kamsi, in the Aramaic). The term is ambiguous between ants and grasshoppers, and Rashi selects nemalim.

Why? Two pesukim in Mishlei couple the nemalah and the arbeh under one specific chisaron: both species have no melech. Shlomo ha’Melech states it of the nemalah directly — asher ein lah katzin, shoter u’moshel (אֲשֶׁר אֵין לָהּ קָצִין שֹׁטֵר וּמֹשֵׁל) — and of the arbeh implicitly: melech ein la’arbeh (מֶלֶךְ אֵין לָאַרְבֶּה). The Gemara in Shabbos[16] preserves Rabbi Shimon ben Chalafta’s famous experiment: he covered an ant nest to simulate shade, watched one ant exit and return to report, and observed that upon the removal of the shade the reporting ant was killed by the colony. The experiment was intended to test whether ants have a king; the Gemara’s treatment resolves the question in deference to Shlomo ha’Melech’s testimony: ants have no king and no central authority at all. Both species that Rashi’s rendering joins — nemalim and chagavim — share, in Shlomo’s classification, the single chisaron of leaderlessness.

The Punchline — Rejection of Leadership

Upon this reading, the chet ha’meraglim is read as a rejection of leadership. In their own eyes they were as insects; and the insects to which they likened themselves are precisely the species that live without a melech, each creature acting on its own impulse. The immediate historical sequel confirms the reading: the ma’apilim (מַעְפִּילִים) rose up the following day and attempted to ascend to Eretz Yisrael without Moshe Rabbeinu at their head, and were destroyed. The chisaron that produced the meraglim’s self-perception produced the ma’apilim’s uprising. When leadership is rejected or absent, Klal Yisrael descends into the pits. This is the parshah’s support for Rav Schwab’s reading of the berachah: the yagon va’anachah of leaderlessness is not a byproduct but the inner structure of a Klal Yisrael left to its own agudos agudos.

VII. The Diminished Sidrei Kavod

Rav Schwab draws from the same berachah a further observation that reaches into the halachah of the birchos ha’shevach on seeing a chacham and on seeing a king. The classical berachah on seeing a chacham from the umos ha’olam is Baruch Atah Hashem Elokeinu Melech ha’Olam she’nasan mei’chochmaso l’basar va’dam. Rav Schwab is mesupak whether the berachah is sayable in our generation. His concern is that the arc of chochmas ha’teva in the modern period is overwhelmingly one of successive revision: each generation’s wisdom is reopened by the next.

His one acknowledged exception is Albert Einstein, whose foundational work in physics has continued to be confirmed rather than overturned.[17] Figures of comparable popular reputation in subsequent generations — Rav Schwab names Stephen Hawking as the illustration — are more likely to be partially superseded. It should be noted, in sefer-register fairness, that the halachic criterion of the berachah on seeing a chacham does not strictly turn on whether the chacham’s theories will survive the next century; it turns on contemporary recognition of chochmah. On a strictly halachic reading no chacham would ever warrant the berachah, since every generation’s science is in principle subject to revision. Rav Schwab’s point is best read as a hashkafic observation about the character of the age dressed in halachic language, not as a halachic safek in the technical sense. Read that way it stands.

Rav Schwab extends the same observation to the berachah on seeing a king and to the berachah on seeing a gadol ha’dor. The Satmar kehillah famously said the berachah on the Queen of England; the applicability of the berachah on a modern constitutional president is a live machlokes. Even concerning the berachah on a gadol ha’dor, Rav Schwab was mesupak whether a figure of universal, uncontested stature can be found in our generation. He grounded the right to raise the question in a biographical experience: he had spent a Shabbos in the presence of the Chofetz Chaim in 1930, and in his own telling never stopped speaking of it for the rest of his life. He accordingly held that he knew what the berachah was for — and that since the Chofetz Chaim’s petirah, no figure had risen to that level of unanimous reception.

The register is uncomfortable but internally consistent. All three sidrei kavod — the halachic authority of a central beis din, the political authority of a melech, and the intellectual authority of a chacham whose chochmah is unassailable — share the same impoverishment. Ours is in this sense a yasmi d’yasmi generation (יְתָמֵי דְיַתְמֵי) — an orphan generation of orphans, as the Gemara speaks of its own. Hashivah Shofteinu, in Rav Schwab’s reading, is a daily petition for all three to be restored at once.

VIII. Birkat HaMinim — The Nineteenth Berachah

The berachah that follows Hashivah Shofteinu — v’la’malshinim (וְלַמַּלְשִׁינִים) — is unique among the berachos of Shemoneh Esrei in a basic respect: for the first centuries of Shemoneh Esrei’s history as a fixed nusach it did not exist. The Rambam at the opening of Hilchos Tefillah[18] writes that Ezra ha’Sofer and the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah in the early Bayis Sheini standardized the Shemoneh Esrei at eighteen berachos. The nineteenth was added later, in the days of Rabban Gamliel in Yavneh. Across all the centuries since, Klal Yisrael has continued to call the tefillah Shemoneh Esrei, retaining the original name of the eighteen even as a nineteenth stands within it. The retention is itself a tefillah: for the day on which the minim disappear and Shemoneh Esrei becomes Shemoneh Esrei again.

Shimon ha’Pakuli in Yavneh

The Gemara in Berachos and in the parallel in Megillah[19] records one of the most consequential takkanos in the history of Klal Yisrael. Shimon ha’Pakuli (שִׁמְעוֹן הַפָּקוּלִי) — a tanna named after the trade (cotton) in which he supported himself — arranged the Shemoneh Esrei in the fixed order in which it is said to this day. Until then, as the Rambam describes, every mispallel davened in his own words, and the nusachos had drifted into a chaos of competing formulations. Shimon ha’Pakuli unified the nusach. He then presented the arrangement before Rabban Gamliel. Rabban Gamliel stood and said: “Rabbosai — we require another בְּרָכָה.” The Anshei Knesses HaGedolah had set eighteen; Rabban Gamliel required a nineteenth. Why?

The Minim and the Crisis of Indistinguishability

The decades following the churban Bayis Sheini saw the emergence of a group within Klal Yisrael that posed a danger of a specific kind. The first Christians were yehudim: Jews persuaded that the reading of Tanach advanced by Yeshu and his talmidim was the truth and the reading of Chazal was not. They set up to be meisis u’madiach (מֵסִית וּמַדִּיחַ), to persuade yehudim in town squares and in the lobbies of batei knesses, distorting pesukim in Tanach to support their claims. The theological pressure within the Christian camp drove the mission further still, as the Second Coming was held to require the conversion of all unbelievers.

The crisis the kehillos faced was a crisis of indistinguishability. The minim wore tzitzis; they attended the minyanim; they recited the tefillos. In the terminology of the period they were called nozrim yehudim (נוֹצְרִים יְהוּדִים) — Jewish Christians. There was no visible seam by which the kahal could distinguish them from any other mispallel. Rabban Gamliel understood that a sharp halachic instrument was required to draw the line.

The Takkanah

Rabban Gamliel asked his talmidim: who can compose a בְּרָכָה for the מִינִים? The original nusach was v’la’minim (וְלַמִּינִים). Printed editions of the siddur from the sixteenth century onward routinely replaced the original la’minim with la’malshinim, as Christian censors could not tolerate the direct naming; manuscript evidence, including from the Cairo Geniza, preserves the earlier form.[20] The scatter of nusachos that survives in various Rishonim — malchus ha’rish’ah (מַלְכוּת הָרִשְׁעָה), kol ha’resha’im (כָּל הָרְשָׁעִים), malchus zadon (מַלְכוּת זָדוֹן) — is almost entirely the product of external pressure across the generations, not of internal variation.

The Operational Mechanism

The mechanism by which the berachah did its work is halachic machinery, not pastoral practice. Every min — every Christian-leaning yehudi — would skip v’la’minim upon reaching it in the silent Shemoneh Esrei: he would not pronounce a curse upon himself and his community. The shaliach tzibbur stood as the bodek. A mispallel who skipped was thereby flagged as a min; a formal procedure of expulsion followed. The structure is a species of bedikah b’darkei amitah (בְּדִיקָה בְּדַרְכֵי אֲמִתָּהּ) — a halachic test in which the behavior of the subject under the test itself constitutes the evidence — applied not to minus in the abstract but to a chezkas minus that the subject’s own conduct establishes. The takkanah succeeded: the bedikah produced the clean separation Rabban Gamliel required.

IX. Shmuel ha’Katan

The one who arose from the talmidim and composed the berachah was Shmuel ha’Katan (שְׁמוּאֵל הַקָּטָן). He is one of the great tannaim of the generation of Rabban Gamliel, yet he is cited only rarely in Chazal — once in the Mishnah, a handful of times in the Gemara. The Yerushalmi at the close of Sukkah[21] offers several accounts of the epithet ha’katan:

  • For his anavah (עֲנָוָה). The Gemara in Sanhedrin records that a chacham was about to be publicly embarrassed; Shmuel ha’Katan accepted the blame upon himself to shield him, and would not admit afterward that it was not he.
  • For comparison to Shmuel ha’Navi — Shmuel ha’Gadol. This tanna approached the madreigah of Shmuel ha’Navi, and was accordingly a katan-sized Shmuel ha’Gadol.
  • For his tznius.

The Gemara in Berachos preserves a further detail of signal weight: Shmuel ha’Katan composed the berachah and then himself forgot its text the following year.[22] He stood before the amud and labored to recover it. The Tosafos there discuss why he was nevertheless not removed from the amud as a stumbling chazzan would be: his character stood as his credential, and the stumble over this particular berachah, authored by his own hand, bore its own kind of testimony. The author of v’la’minim was uniquely bound to it — and uniquely at risk of forgetting it.

X. Binfol Oyivcha Al Tismach — One Arc, Two Readings

Shmuel ha’Katan is cited one time in the Mishnah, in Pirkei Avos perek daled. Every other tanna in Pirkei Avos states a torah of his own; Shmuel ha’Katan, uniquely, states nothing of his own and instead quotes a pasuk of Shlomo ha’Melech:

שְׁמוּאֵל הַקָּטָן אוֹמֵר: בִּנְפֹל אוֹיִבְךָ אַל תִּשְׂמָח, וּבִכָּשְׁלוֹ אַל יָגֵל לִבֶּךָ.
Shmuel ha’Katan says: “At your enemy’s fall do not rejoice, and at his stumble let your heart not exult.”
Mishlei 24:17; Avos 4:19

The placement is striking. The tanna who composed the berachah directed most sharply against enemies of Klal Yisrael is the tanna who in Avos warns against simchah at an enemy’s downfall. Two peshatim have been offered. In the sefer-register they are best read not as alternative explanations but as two faces of a single arc.

Peshat Aleph — The Warning

The first reading reads the pasuk as a counterweight that Shmuel ha’Katan attaches to his own berachah. The takkanah succeeded so completely in flushing the minim from the beis ha’knesses that the author of the berachah felt compelled to publish a disclaimer alongside it. I am the one who composed וְלַמִּינִים; I am the one who brings down enemies through תְּפִילָה. Remember, nonetheless: בִּנְפֹל אוֹיִבְךָ אַל תִּשְׂמָח. Rejoice that הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא answers your תְּפִילָה; do not rejoice in the destruction itself. The exception for b’avod resha’im rinah (בַּאֲבֹד רְשָׁעִים רִנָּה) covers particular enemies of Klal Yisrael whose fall is itself a yeshuah; the general rule, published by Shmuel ha’Katan as his single mishnah-contribution, stands.

Peshat Beis — Rav Kook on the Credential

Rav Avraham Yitzchak ha’Kohen Kook, zt”l,[23] reverses the direction of the inference. The pasuk is not a warning Shmuel ha’Katan attached after composing the berachah; it is the credential that Rabban Gamliel relied upon when selecting him beforehand. A berachah directed against minim can safely be composed only by one who already inhabits the antithesis of sinah. The author must be a man whose tefillah against enemies springs purely from kinas Hashem and not from personal animus. The middah that found expression in the mishnah in Avos — the middah of anavah and of refusal to exult at any mapalah — was the middah that qualified Shmuel ha’Katan, uniquely among the talmidim, to author the berachah.

The Two as One Arc

The two peshatim do not stand in machlokes. They are the same middah viewed from the two ends of a single arc. The credential that qualifies the author in advance is the warning that the author publishes afterward; the warning that frames the berachah from Avos is the credential that the Rosh Yeshiva in Yavneh recognized at the moment of appointment. The author who stands at the amud composing the berachah and the tanna who steps forward in Avos to say binfol oyivcha are the same man moving across the same middah, pre- and post-takkanah. The berachah and the mishnah are two chapters of one sefer.

XI. The Two Berachos as a Single Petition

The two berachos stand together as a single petition. Hashivah Shofteinu asks for the restoration of the central authority whose absence is the yagon of our scatteredness; v’la’malshinim asks for the dissolution of the internal pressures that have historically splintered Klal Yisrael from within. The Shemoneh Esrei that holds a nineteenth berachah is itself a tefillah for its own return to eighteen — the day on which the minim have vanished and the takkanah of Rabban Gamliel is retired as a monument no longer required. On that same day, the sidrei kavod whose impoverishment Rav Schwab lamented will have been restored: the Sanhedrin reconstituted under Eliyahu’s semichah, the malchus beis David seated upon its throne, and the chachmei Yisrael once again speaking with the authority that no subsequent generation unseats. Hashem oz l’amo yitein; Hashem yevareich es amo va’shalom.

  1. The Mahari Beirav’s renewal of semichah in Tzefas, 1538. — Rabbi Yaakov Beirav (c. 1474–1546), born in Maqueda, Spain, expelled in 1492, settled in Tzefas after stops in Fez and Egypt. The halachic basis for his project was the Rambam in Hilchos Sanhedrin 4:11, which rules that unanimous consent of the chachmei Eretz Yisrael may reconstitute a broken chain of semichah. In 1538 he conferred semichah upon a small circle of talmidim: the Beis Yosef (Rabbi Yosef Karo) and the Mabit (Rabbi Moshe mi’Trani) are the two universally agreed upon; the standard lists also name Rabbi Yosef Sagis and a fourth variously identified as Rabbi Yisrael de Curiel or (less probably on chronological grounds) the Ramak. The Ralbach (Rabbi Levi ibn Chaviv), then Rav of Yerushalayim, opposed the project halachically, and the semichah chain was not effectively renewed. The episode is the principal historical precedent for subsequent attempts to reconstitute the Sanhedrin and the inevitable backdrop to the Rambam-Eliyahu mechanism later in this chapter. ↩
  2. The observation that Hashivah Shofteinu alone bears “melech” in its chasimah — attributed to Rav Saadia Gaon. — Rav Saadia Gaon (Rasag, 882–942), Rosh Yeshivah of Sura. The observation is cited in the Rebbe’s name as Rasag’s; whether the uniqueness-remark is preserved in Siddur Rav Saadia Gaon itself or in a later commentator upon the Siddur or elsewhere in the Rasag corpus requires verification against a critical edition before print. The Tur’s kushya in Siman 118 presupposes the observation and treats it as settled, which is itself testimony to its gaonic provenance. ↩
  3. Berachos 12a — “kol berachah she’ein bah hazkaras ha’Shem u’malchus einah berachah”; berachah ha’semuchah la’chaverta. — The sugya in Berachos establishes the foundational din: every berachah requires both hazkaras ha’Shem and malchus; without both it is structurally not a berachah. The exception of berachah ha’semuchah la’chaverta, under which a berachah adjoining a prior berachah borrows the prior malchus, is the canonical structure of the middle berachos of Shemoneh Esrei. ↩
  4. Tur Orach Chaim Siman 118 — the kushya on “melech” and the answer in the name of Rabbeinu Yechiel. — The Tur (Rabbeinu Yaakov Ba’al HaTurim, c. 1269–1343, son of the Rosh) raises the kushya in Siman 118: why does only Hashivah Shofteinu contain “melech” in its chasimah when berachah ha’semuchah would not require it? He answers in the name of his older brother Rabbeinu Yechiel, drawn from the Rosh’s own exposition. The Tur’s discussion of the Aseres Yemei Teshuvah change to HaMelech ha’mishpat and the b’dieved ruling appears in parallel in Siman 118 and — for the hilchos of Aseres Yemei Teshuvah proper — in Siman 582. ↩
  5. The sons of the Rosh — Rabbeinu Yechiel as the eldest. — The Rosh (Rabbeinu Asher ben Yechiel, c. 1250–1327) is known to have had at least four sons who entered the rabbinic record: Rabbeinu Yechiel, who succeeded his father on the rabbinate of Toledo and is universally identified as the eldest; Rabbeinu Yaakov, the Tur; Rabbi Yehudah, author of Zichron Yehudah; and Rabbi Hillel. The Tur’s citation in Siman 118 is to Rabbeinu Yechiel as his “older brother.” Transcript variants that name Rabbi Hillel as the oldest son appear to reflect a shiur-moment slip; the historical record and the standard biographical sources place Rabbeinu Yechiel at the head of the siblings. ↩
  6. Chida, Birkei Yosef on Shulchan Aruch Yoreh De’ah Siman 240 — the Rosh stood for his son Rabbeinu Yechiel. — Rabbi Chaim Yosef David Azulai (the Chida, 1724–1806), in Birkei Yosef on Yoreh De’ah at the hilchos kibbud av va’em, addresses the din of when a father must rise before his talmid chacham son. His decisive evidence is mesorah: the Rosh, gadol ha’dor of his time, rose fully each time his son Rabbeinu Yechiel entered the room. The episode testifies to the madreigah of Rabbeinu Yechiel as a talmid chacham, and is one of the principal traces of his stature, since little of his Torah survives independently. The underlying halachic principle — that kibbud av va’em is taught not through derashos but through ma’aseh — is familiar from every generation of a serious Jewish home. ↩
  7. Mishlei 29:4 — “melech b’mishpat ya’amid aretz v’ish terumos yeharsenah.” — Shlomo ha’Melech’s pasuk in Mishlei: a king establishes the land through mishpat; one who exacts unwarranted tributes destroys it. The pasuk binds malchus to mishpat as its proper instrument — the king’s authority is actualized only through the delivery of mishpat. The Rosh, and the Tur in his name through Rabbeinu Yechiel, reads the pasuk as the structural ground for inserting “melech” uniquely into the chasimah of Hashivah Shofteinu. ↩
  8. Rama, Shulchan Aruch Orach Chaim Siman 118 (and Siman 582 for the hilchos of the season) — b’dieved saying “melech oheiv tzedakah u’mishpat” in Aseres Yemei Teshuvah. — The Rama rules that one who erred and said the year-round chasimah in place of “HaMelech ha’mishpat” during the Aseres Yemei Teshuvah need not return — because the berachah’s chasimah already contains “melech,” which satisfies the structural requirement of the season b’dieved. The pesak is the standard practice today and is itself a halachic application of the Rabbeinu Yechiel yesod. ↩
  9. The Aruch’s contrary view — older nusach without “melech.” — The Aruch (Rabbi Nassan bar Yechiel of Rome, c. 1035–1106), working from older nuschaos in which the year-round chasimah of Hashivah Shofteinu read “oheiv tzedakah u’mishpat” without “melech,” rules that one who said the year-round chasimah in Aseres Yemei Teshuvah must repeat. The pesak follows the Rama; the Aruch’s reading bears witness to the development of the nusach across the gaonic period, and from the opposite side to the same yesod: that mishpat structurally requires malchus in the chasimah and the nusach gradually crystallized to reflect it. ↩
  10. Yeshayahu 1:26 — “v’ashivah shofetayich k’varishonah v’yo’atzayich k’vatechilah.” — Yeshayahu’s pasuk in the opening perek, read as the haftarah of Chazon Yeshayahu on the Shabbos before Tishah b’Av. The pasuk’s first-person future-tense havtachah — “And I will return” — is turned by the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah into the daily bakashah of Klal Yisrael addressed back to its speaker: “Return.” The transformation is itself a yesod: the mispallel daily participates in the fulfillment of a prophecy whose fulfillment is already guaranteed. ↩
  11. Rambam, Perush ha’Mishnah on Sanhedrin perek 10 (Perek Chelek) and Hakdamah l’Perush ha’Mishnah — Mashiach ben David after Sanhedrin; Achiyah ha’Shiloni as the link to Eliyahu. — In Perek Chelek the Rambam rules that the sequence of events in yemos ha’Mashiach is not fully specified in the sources of Chazal, but that Mashiach ben David structurally requires a functioning Sanhedrin, so that “v’ashivah shofetayich” precedes “u’malkam Hashem oseh es ha’shem.” In the Hakdamah, the Rambam traces the chain of Torah she’b’al peh through Achiyah ha’Shiloni — a navi who left Mitzrayim with Klal Yisrael and lived through David and Shlomo — to Eliyahu ha’Navi. The chain is thereby preserved intact in Eliyahu’s person; when he returns, he arrives with semichah and may renew it. The Rambam’s position on Sanhedrin-before-Mashiach is not universally held — the Raavad and others allow the alternative that Mashiach himself will reconstitute semichah — but it is the position upon which the Mahari Beirav’s project rested and which gives the berachah its operational force. ↩
  12. Rav Shimshon Schwab, zt”l, Iyyun Tefillah — “v’haser mimenu yagon va’anachah” as the tefillah for the end of agudos agudos. — Rav Shimshon Refael Schwab, zt”l (1908–1995), Rav of Kehal Adas Yeshurun in Washington Heights, in Iyyun Tefillah (English: Rav Schwab on Prayer), reads the chasimah-phrase as structurally the operational heart of the berachah: the absence of central halachic authority produces fragmentation into agudos agudos; fragmentation produces yagon and anachah by definition; the berachah accordingly asks for the dissolution of fragmentation through the restoration of one Sanhedrin. ↩
  13. Yevamos 13b — “lo tisgodedu — lo sa’asu agudos agudos.” — The sugya in Yevamos derives from “lo tisgodedu” (Devarim 14:1) — read in its peshat as a prohibition against self-laceration in mourning — the additional issur of “lo sa’asu agudos agudos”: do not divide into separate halachic factions. The issur targets the structural chilluk of Klal Yisrael into competing halachic communities, the very matzav Rav Schwab identifies with the yagon va’anachah the berachah seeks to dissolve. The Gemara adds the historical observation that in the generations before the talmidei Shammai v’Hillel, no major machlokos are recorded in Klal Yisrael — the fragmentation of pesak is itself a product of the broken central authority. ↩
  14. The Kotzker on “ka’chagavim b’eineinu”. — Rabbi Menachem Mendel of Kotzk (1787–1859), in the chassidishe reading preserved across his divrei Torah (Emes v’Emunah and parallel collections), reads the double formulation of the pasuk — “in our own eyes… and so in their eyes” — as teaching that self-perception precedes external perception. The meraglim’s own diminishment of themselves became the ground upon which the anakim diminished them. The Rebbe’s punchline: “If you look at yourself as a חָגָב, you are a חָגָב.” ↩
  15. Sotah 35a — the anakim’s observation; the Aramaic “kamsi.” Rashi’s rendering of chagavim as nemalim. — The Gemara in Sotah records that the anakim, observing the meraglim in the vineyards, remarked that they appeared as insects. The Aramaic term “kamsi” is ambiguous between ants and grasshoppers. Rashi on the pasuk in Bamidbar renders chagavim as nemalim. The preference is tracked by later mefarshim to two pesukim in Mishlei that couple the two species under the single chisaron of leaderlessness: of the nemalah, “asher ein lah katzin, shoter u’moshel” (Mishlei 6:7); of the arbeh, “melech ein la’arbeh” (Mishlei 30:27). The chassidishe gilyonos of Shalal Alter (distributed in the Flatbush shul the week of the shiur) crystallize the linkage. ↩
  16. Shabbos 77b (and the parallel in Chullin 57b) — Rabbi Shimon ben Chalafta’s experiment on ants. — The Gemara preserves the famous experiment of Rabbi Shimon ben Chalafta: he covered an ant nest to simulate the shade of evening, watched a single ant exit and return to report to the colony, and — upon lifting the cover — observed that the reporting ant was killed by the colony for its false report. The experiment was intended to test whether ants have a king who would prevent an unsanctioned execution. The Gemara’s treatment ultimately defers to Shlomo ha’Melech’s own testimony: ants have no katzin, shoter u’moshel. The reading supports Rashi’s rendering of chagavim as nemalim: the meraglim likened themselves to the species that lives without a melech, and the chet was the rejection of leadership itself. The ma’apilim’s immediate uprising — ascending to Eretz Yisrael without Moshe Rabbeinu — carries the same chisaron to its destruction. ↩
  17. Rav Schwab’s contemporary examples — Einstein, Hawking, and the berachos on chachamim, melachim, and gedolim. — Albert Einstein (1879–1955) is cited by Rav Schwab as arguably the single contemporary chacham whose foundational work has continued to be confirmed rather than overturned, and therefore the rare figure on whom “she’nasan mei’chochmaso l’basar va’dam” would stand without retrospective embarrassment. Rav Schwab’s broader framing — that the arc of chochmas ha’teva in the modern period is one of successive revision — is best read as a hashkafic observation about the age dressed in halachic language; the strict halachic criterion of the berachah turns on contemporary recognition of chochmah, not on retrospective durability. The point is likewise extended to the berachah on seeing a king (Satmar on the Queen of England as a known precedent; the safek on a modern constitutional president) and even to the berachah on seeing a gadol ha’dor. Rav Schwab grounded the right to raise the question on contemporary gedolim in the biographical experience of having spent a Shabbos with the Chofetz Chaim in 1930: he never stopped speaking of it, and held that no figure since the Chofetz Chaim’s petirah had attained the universality of reception the berachah presupposes. The register in which Rav Schwab delivers all three observations is the register of yasmi d’yasmi: ours is an orphan generation of orphans with respect to all three sidrei kavod — halachic, royal, and intellectual — and “Hashivah Shofteinu” is a tefillah for the restoration of all three. ↩
  18. Rambam, Hilchos Tefillah 1:4–6 — Ezra and the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah instituted eighteen berachos; the nineteenth added later. — The Rambam at the opening of Hilchos Tefillah describes the historical evolution. Moshe Rabbeinu instituted the three daily tefillos; Ezra ha’Sofer and the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah in early Bayis Sheini standardized the eighteen berachos after a long period in which Klal Yisrael davened without fixed nusach; Rabban Gamliel and his beis din in Yavneh later added the nineteenth against the minim. The Rambam’s own continued use of the name Shemoneh Esrei is itself an act of Chazal’s preservation of the original shem — a tefillah that the nineteenth become unnecessary. ↩
  19. Berachos 28b and Megillah 17b — Shimon ha’Pakuli and Rabban Gamliel; the takkanah of the nineteenth berachah. — The parallel sugyos record the takkanah: Shimon ha’Pakuli arranged the eighteen berachos in their fixed order before Rabban Gamliel in Yavneh; Rabban Gamliel then asked his talmidim to compose a nineteenth. Shmuel ha’Katan arose, composed it, and — the following year — forgot it himself and labored at the amud to recover it; the Tosafos ad loc discuss why he was not removed. The Gemara further records the operational mechanism: a min would not say the berachah against himself; skipping it identified him publicly. ↩
  20. Censorship of “la’minim” to “la’malshinim”; the scatter of nusachos. — Printed editions of the siddur from the sixteenth century onward routinely replaced the original “la’minim” with “la’malshinim,” and in some editions substituted still further with “malchus zadon,” “malchus ha’rish’ah,” or “kol ha’resha’im.” Christian censors across the generations could not tolerate the direct naming of minim, which they correctly identified as targeting the nozrim yehudim who had become the ancestors of their faith. Manuscript evidence — including the Cairo Geniza — preserves the original “la’minim”; some contemporary siddurim have restored it. The scatter of surviving nusachos is almost entirely the product of external pressure across generations, not of internal halachic variation. ↩
  21. Yerushalmi Sukkah at the end of the masechta; Sanhedrin 11a; Mishnah Avos 4:19 — Shmuel ha’Katan. — Shmuel ha’Katan was a tanna of the generation of Rabban Gamliel in Yavneh. The sources offer competing accounts of the epithet: the Gemara in Sanhedrin 11a records his anavah in shielding another chacham from public embarrassment by taking the chet upon himself; the Yerushalmi at the close of Sukkah reads the title as comparison to Shmuel ha’Navi — a katan-sized Shmuel ha’Gadol, signaling the highest madreigah; parallel sources cite his tznius. He is cited once in the Mishnah, in Avos 4:19, where uniquely he states no original Torah and quotes only the pasuk in Mishlei 24:17. ↩
  22. Berachos 28b–29a — Shmuel ha’Katan forgot his own berachah; Tosafos on his not being removed from the amud. — The Gemara records that Shmuel ha’Katan stood before the amud and, in the course of davening, could not recover the text of the very berachah he had composed. He paused, labored to retrieve it, and ultimately succeeded. The Tosafos address why he was not removed from the amud under the general rule that a chazzan who stumbles in a berachah is replaced: his character stood as his credential, and the stumble bore its own kind of testimony — the author uniquely bound to the berachah was also uniquely at risk of its loss. ↩
  23. Rav Avraham Yitzchak ha’Kohen Kook, zt”l — the inverse reading of binfol oyivcha. — Rav Kook (1865–1935), in his collected writings on Pirkei Avos and on tefillah (Ein Ayah and parallel peirushim), develops the reading opposite to the standard one: Shmuel ha’Katan’s quoting of “binfol oyivcha al tismach” is not a self-warning attached after the berachah; it is the credential that qualified him beforehand. A berachah directed against minim requires authorship by one who already inhabits the antithesis of sinah — a tanna whose tefillah against enemies springs purely from kinas Hashem, not from personal animus. The two peshatim are best read together as two faces of a single arc, pre- and post-takkanah, around the one middah of Shmuel ha’Katan’s anavah. ↩
