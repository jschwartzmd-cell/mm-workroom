קְרִיאַת הַתּוֹרָה, קְדֻשָּׁה דְסִדְרָא, וְלַמְנַצֵּחַ מִזְמוֹר לְדָוִד

The Torah Reading, Kedushah DeSidra, and Lamnatzeach: Birkas HaGomel, Ashrei, and the Earliest Source
Studies in Tefillah (תפילה) — From the Open Ark to the Close of Davening
17 Kislev 5786 · 7 December 2025 · Sefer Edition

This discourse leaves the opened ark of the previous week, the Brich Shmei (בריך שמיה), and takes up the reading of the Torah itself: why every oleh (עולה) makes a blessing of his own before and after his portion; why Birkas HaGomel (ברכת הגומל) is said at the reading; and the Monday-and-Thursday Yehi Ratzons (יהי רצון), one of which was lost to censorship.

It then passes to Kedushah DeSidra (קדושה דסידרא): why Ashrei (אשרי) is said three times a day and why it is said again here, before Uva LeTzion (ובא לציון); the contrast between the Aruch HaShulchan (ערוך השלחן) and the Mishnah Berurah (משנה ברורה); and the earliest source of Lamnatzeach (למנצח), in a responsum of Rav Natronai Gaon (רב נטרונאי גאון) to the Jews of Lucena, with the picture of the Beis Rabbeinu SheBeBavel (בית רבינו שבבבל) behind it.

The discourse stops, as the lecturer says, in the middle of Lamnatzeach (למנצח), with the promise to continue the following week.

I. The Blessings of the Oleh

The Question

A person called up to the Torah blesses before and after his portion. The question arises at once: a blessing over the Torah (Birkas HaTorah (ברכת התורה)) was already said that morning, as every morning. Why must a second blessing be made at the reading?

Originally Every Oleh Read for Himself

In the days of the Mishnah (משנה) and the Gemara (גמרא)[1] every person called up read his own portion. Whoever received an aliyah (עליה) had to know it, including the cantillation (taamim (טעמים)) and the grammar of every word, and some communities still keep this practice. The law is that one who is not prepared may not read. The lecturer related that on the previous day he himself had given the reading to another because he had not prepared.

Two Verses and One Targum as Preparation

How could a person prepare when he did not know whether he would be called? The authorities answer[2] that one who completes the portion each week, shnayim mikra v’echad targum (שנים מקרא ואחד תרגום), is sufficiently prepared. The lecturer remarks that he does not believe those who review the portion twice today could take any unprepared aliyah (עליה) without further practice. But today the baal korei (בעל קורא) reads everything and the oleh (עולה) only stands; it is the baal korei (בעל קורא) who must prepare.

The Reason for the Extra Blessing: Kavod HaTorah

Why, then, the additional blessing? Because of the honor of the Torah (kavod haTorah (כבוד התורה)). The morning blessings are for one’s private study. When the Torah is read for the entire congregation, a fresh blessing is required out of respect for it.

II. Why Every Oleh: The Decree on Account of Those Entering and Leaving

The Older Practice

The Gemara (גמרא) near the end of Maseches Megillah (מסכת מגילה)[3] teaches that originally the first oleh (עולה) blessed before his portion but made no blessing after it; those who followed relied on his opening blessing; and only the last oleh (עולה) made the closing blessing, asher nasan lanu Toras emes (אשר נתן לנו תורת אמת). On Monday and Thursday there are three aliyos (עליות), on Shabbos (שבת) seven, and the blessings bracketed only the beginning and the end.

The Enactment

Today the Sages have ordained that every oleh (עולה) blesses before and after. The reason is a gezeirah mipnei haniknasim vehayotzim (גזירה מפני הנכנסים והיוצאים): those who enter in the middle of the reading, say during the third aliyah (עליה), did not hear the opening blessing, and those who leave early do not hear the closing one, and they might conclude that a blessing was omitted. The lecturer draws attention to the sensitivity this shows. A latecomer is commonly dismissed as careless; the Sages instead instituted a whole series of blessings for his sake. He adds that one who sees a man arrive late should therefore not rebuke him, and recalls what the Sages did for him.

The reading is itself an obligation of the community: on Monday, on Thursday, and above all on Shabbos (שבת), Israel may not go three days without Torah.[4] When the oleh (עולה) says Barchu es Hashem hamevorach (ברכו את ה׳ המבורך), he calls everyone to take part in discharging that obligation together, and the reader and the congregation fulfil it as one.

Baruch Hu U’Varuch Shemo

The lecturer mentions in passing the confusion among the later authorities as to why the custom is to answer Baruch Hu uvaruch Shemo (ברוך הוא וברוך שמו) at certain blessings and not at others. The answer is not a matter of strict law and is not found in the Gemara (גמרא); the Rishonim (ראשונים) wrote that it is said whenever the Name is heard, yet practice has narrowed. A comprehensive responsum on the subject, with a long tract, is found in Mishnah Halachos (משנה הלכות) of Rav Menashe Klein (רב מנשה קליין).[5] The lecturer adds an admonition: the title of that work was mocked in some quarters of the yeshivah (ישיבה) world during the dispute over the eruv (עירוב) of Boro Park, in which his wife’s grandfather, ר׳ ישראל בעלסקי, stood on the other side. Such mockery is not the way of Israel toward its Torah scholars; whoever has a halachic argument may make it.

III. Birkas HaGomel: Why at the Reading of the Torah

The Four Categories

The Gemara (גמרא)[6] derives from Tehillim (תהלים) 107 four categories of persons who must give thanks after deliverance, and the Tur (טור)[7] gives the mnemonic חיים:

  1. ח — chavush (חבוש), one imprisoned.
  2. י — yam (ים), one who crosses the sea.
  3. י — yissurim (ייסורים), one who was ill and recovered.
  4. מ — midbar (מדבר), one who travels through the wilderness.

In the days of the Beis HaMikdash (בית המקדש) the thanks took the form of a thanksgiving offering (korban todah (קרבן תודה)); today HaGomel (הגומל) is said in its place.

A DedicationThe lecturer dedicated the discourse, though not as its sponsor, to the complete recovery of ר׳ יוסף נחמן זעליג שליט״א, who stands in the second י of חיים, yissurim (ייסורים), and who was then preparing a wedding for his granddaughter; it is a mitzvah (מצוה) to call him and visit him, among the other sick of Israel.

Why at the Reading

The verse reads וירוממוהו בקהל עם ובמושב זקנים יהללוהו (Tehillim 107:32). Bikhal am (בקהל עם) means a minyan (מנין); uvemoshav zekeinim (ובמושב זקנים) means at least two talmidei chachamim (תלמידי חכמים). Ideally, then, one blesses HaGomel (הגומל) before ten persons, two of them scholars; some say the two may be counted within the ten. Why is this not demanded in practice? Because scholars are not always to be found, and the Sages’ remedy was to say it at the reading of the Torah: the scroll that lies open before the person stands, in the lecturer’s words, for the two scholars. That is why the blessing is said specifically at the reading, whether or not one was called up.

A Pearl from Shalmei Nedarim

The previous afternoon, at the third meal, a member of the congregation had shared a pearl from Shalmei Nedarim (שלמי נדרים), a well-known work on Maseches Nedarim (מסכת נדרים).[8] Its author served as rabbi of Kupishok, a city in northern Lithuania near Ponevezh, for forty years; the lecturer remarks that without the book he would be unknown. In the introduction the author writes that he had been gravely ill and had recovered, that he was himself the second י of חיים, and that the book was his thanksgiving.

There he asks why HaGomel (הגומל) differs from every other blessing. Elsewhere the congregation answers Amen (אמן) and the matter ends; here a whole sentence is added, מי שגמלך כל טוב הוא יגמלך כל טוב סלה, for which there is no parallel. His answer: one who has been saved by a miracle fears that Heaven drew upon his account of merits to perform it, and that the account now stands lower. He is grateful and anxious together, and so the congregation reassures him: He who has given you good will continue to give you only good.

A member of the congregation raised the parallel of the response after a circumcision, כשם שנכנס לברית כן יכנס לתורה ולחופה ולמעשים טובים.[9] The lecturer accepted the point and distinguished the two: that response is a prayer of mercy for the parents, that the child be raised to Torah, marriage and good deeds, whereas the response to HaGomel (הגומל) comforts the one who blessed.

An Additional Reason: Reading as an Offering

The Rosh (רא״ש)[10] is cited for a further reason: as prayer stands in place of the offerings, so the reading of the Torah stands in place of offerings, and one who cannot bring a korban todah (קרבן תודה) discharges that wish through the reading itself.

A Question on the Response (restored from the recording)

The lecturer then recounted a question on this very point, found in the work Hisorerus Teshuvah (התעוררות תשובה), a responsa collection by a grandson of the Chasam Sofer (חתם סופר).[11] The Chasam Sofer (חתם סופר) was succeeded in Pressburg by his eldest son, the Ksav Sofer (כתב סופר), whose son, Rav Shimon Sofer (רב שמעון סופר), was the rabbi of Erlau (Eger) in Hungary. The lecturer once met the grandson of that rabbi, a prominent collector of antique Judaica, who told him that his grandfather was ninety-five years old, after sixty years in the rabbinate, when he was taken to Auschwitz. The question in the book is whether one should answer Amen (אמן) to HaGomel (הגומל) at all, or say only מי שגמלך כל טוב. The lecturer notes that the practice is to say both.

IV. The Yehi Ratzons of Monday and Thursday, and the Censorship of the Siddur

When They Are Said

After the reading on Monday and Thursday, during the rolling of the scroll, several Yehi Ratzons (יהי רצון) are said. Their origin is unknown; they appear in early prayer books of the Geonim (גאונים). Their timing is disputed. Ashkenazim (אשכנזים) say them every Monday and Thursday; Sefardim (ספרדים) say them only on Rosh Chodesh (ראש חודש), when they bless the new month; and the Machzor Vitry (מחזור ויטרי)[12] writes that they are to be said only at Shabbos Minchah (שבת מנחה), a practice, to the lecturer’s knowledge, that no one follows today. Why they are said from the bimah (בימה), and why they follow the reading, the Rishonim (ראשונים) do not say.

The Yehi Ratzon That Was Removed

One Yehi Ratzon (יהי רצון) is missing from our prayer books. It began יהי רצון מלפני אבינו שבשמים להשמיד את אויבינו and continued at length, ending with amen (אמן). The lecturer’s explanation is censorship: the hostile authorities of earlier centuries scrutinized every word of the prayer book, and did not welcome Jews praying twice weekly for the destruction of their enemies. When and by whom it was removed is not known; the lecturer says that this is what seems to have happened.

V. Ashrei Three Times a Day

The Gemara

The main section of prayer is finished with Shemoneh Esrei (שמונה עשרה), the repetition, Tachanun (תחנון), and the reading, which is bound up with prayer without being strictly part of it. A new section now begins, Kedushah DeSidra (קדושה דסידרא). It is so called because it contains ואתה קדוש יושב תהלות ישראל. But before it comes Ashrei (אשרי), which was already said in Pesukei DeZimrah (פסוקי דזמרה). Why again, and why here?

The Gemara (גמרא) at the beginning of the Talmud Bavli (תלמוד בבלי)[13] states: כל האומר תהלה לדוד בכל יום שלש פעמים מובטח לו שהוא בן עולם הבא. It is, as the lecturer puts it, an entry ticket to the World to Come.

Two Reasons

The Gemara (גמרא) points to two features. The psalm is alphabetical, from א to ת, expressing a world in order, without chaos; and it contains פותח את ידך ומשביע לכל חי רצון, which declares that God provides each one with what he needs. Sephardim open their arms at Posei’ach es Yadecha (פותח את ידך); Ashkenazim have adopted the gesture, opening the channels, ready to receive.

The Requirement of Kavanah

It is not a trick by which three recitations ensure a place in the World to Come. All the Rishonim (ראשונים) write that it must be said with intention, especially the verse פותח את ידך, and the law is that one who did not concentrate there returns and says it again.[14]

Why Twice at Shacharis

Three times a day corresponds to three prayers. Yet Ashrei is said twice at Shacharis (שחרית), once in Pesukei DeZimrah (פסוקי דזמרה) and once before Uva LeTzion (ובא לציון), once at Minchah (מנחה), and not at Maariv (מעריב). The Rishonim (ראשונים) explain that Maariv (מעריב) was originally optional (reshus (רשות)); one who for some reason did not pray it would have lost his third Ashrei, and so two were placed at Shacharis (שחרית).

Ashrei and Kedushah

Why here? The Rishonim (ראשונים) connect Ashrei with Kedushah (קדושה): it is said before the Kedushah (קדושה) of Yotzer Or (יוצר אור), and before the Kedushah (קדושה) of Uva LeTzion (ובא לציון), and at Minchah (מנחה) before the Kedushah (קדושה) of the repetition. Maariv (מעריב) has no Kedushah (קדושה) and so no Ashrei. The lecturer says that the precise connection is beyond him, a matter of Kabbalah; his own reading is that kadosh (קדוש) means separate, set apart and far above us, and Ashrei, with its ארוממך אלוהי המלך, expresses that elevation and so fittingly precedes Kedushah (קדושה).

A Note of Rabbi Akiva Eiger in the Gilyon HaShasThe lecturer observes that in the margins of his own Gemara (גמרא), the Gilyon HaShas (גליון הש״ס) printed in the Vilna Shas (ש״ס), Rabbi Akiva Eiger (רבי עקיבא איגר) wrote notes, some of them questions and some references, and that this is among the first of them.[15] He points out that when the Rosh (רא״ש) quotes this Gemara (גמרא), his text does not read שלש פעמים בכל יום, and that once a day would suffice. The lecturer adds that we do not rely on this: we are stringent, we all want livelihood, and we say it three times.

VI. The Aruch HaShulchan and the Mishnah Berurah

Two Approaches to Practical Halachah

The Aruch HaShulchan (ערוך השלחן)[16] of Rabbi Yechiel Michel Epstein (רבי יחיאל מיכל הלוי עפשטיין), rabbi of Navahrudak (Novardok) in Lithuania, is quoted often and frequently disagrees with the Mishnah Berurah (משנה ברורה). The lecturer reports that some leading authorities rely on the Aruch HaShulchan (ערוך השלחן) more than on the Mishnah Berurah (משנה ברורה) for practical rulings. The reason given: the Chofetz Chaim (חפץ חיים), though the leader of his generation, never held the rabbinic office of a city, whereas the Aruch HaShulchan (ערוך השלחן) had, by the lecturer’s figure, forty-five to fifty years as a communal rabbi, deciding practical questions. The Aruch HaShulchan (ערוך השלחן) is often the more lenient, the Mishnah Berurah (משנה ברורה) the more stringent, and which to follow remains debated.

Of the family, the lecturer notes that the author’s son, Rav Baruch Epstein (רב ברוך הלוי עפשטיין), the author of Torah Temimah (תורה תמימה), writes in his book Baruch SheAmar (ברוך שאמר) that the family traces itself to Ben Abishi (בני אבישי), with Sephardic origins. By way of aside he observes that many who consider themselves pure Lithuanian, Hungarian or Polish stock in fact descend from Spain: his own family, by tradition ten generations in Ashkenaz, goes back eleven generations to a Sephardic family from Portugal or Spain, as his older brother’s research found. He adds that the family name was Weiss for some four hundred years until his grandfather changed it to Ephrati.

The Aruch HaShulchan on the Closing Ashrei

The lecturer then gives the Aruch HaShulchan (ערוך השלחן)’s approach to the Ashrei that closes the davening. Some run through Uva LeTzion (ובא לציון), already removing their tefillin (תפילין). A friend, he relates, once visited a minyan (מנין) at the federal prison in Otisville and saw the inmates removing their tefillin (תפילין) at Uva LeTzion (ובא לציון) as if hurrying somewhere. The Aruch HaShulchan (ערוך השלחן) says that Ashrei is placed at the end precisely as people are about to leave: during the davening we fed the soul; now we go out to feed the body, to earn a living for the sake of Heaven, to support the family, to pay tuition, to buy mitzvos (מצוות). Here Ashrei is no longer pure praise but a real request, פותח את ידך. The Yesod VeShoresh HaAvodah (יסוד ושורש העבודה)[17] writes that this Ashrei is to be said with more intention than the Ashrei of Pesukei DeZimrah (פסוקי דזמרה).

VII. Lamnatzeach: From Sura to Lucena

The Academies of Babylonia

Why is Lamnatzeach (למנצח), the twentieth chapter of Tehillim (תהלים), said here? The earliest source in the old prayer books is a responsum of Rav Natronai Gaon (רב נטרונאי גאון), head of the academy at Sura (סורא). Sura, with Pumbedisa (פומבדיתא) and Nehardea (נהרדעא), was one of the three great centers of Torah in Babylonia, where ki miTziyon teitzei Torah (כי מציון תצא תורה) became, for many centuries, ki miBavel teitzei Torah (כי מבבל תצא תורה). The lecturer places Sura in what is now southern Iraq, near the Saudi border, and recounts that Rav (רב) moved his academy there from Nehardea to renew the community.[18] There followed the Amoraim (אמוראים), through Ravina (רבינא) and Rav Ashi (רב אשי), and then the Geonim (גאונים).

Rav Natronai Gaon

Rav Natronai (רב נטרונאי) headed Sura in the middle of the ninth century and wrote more responsa than any other Gaon (גאון), although Rav Hai (רב האי), Rav Sherira (רב שרירא) and Rav Amram (רב עמרם) are better known.[19] The lecturer adds, in a digression, that before the discovery of the Cairo Genizah, a single room containing some 150,000 pages unseen for a thousand years, little of the Geonic literature was known, and that a project to digitize the whole is under way.

Lucena

His correspondents included the communities of Spain, then at the beginning of its history; the golden age of the Rambam (רמב״ם), Ramban (רמב״ן), Rashba (רשב״א) and Rosh (רא״ש) came later. The lecturer says the Spanish Jews of that time were somewhat ignorant of the order of prayer. Rav Natronai (רב נטרונאי) gave guidance to the community of Lucena, in the region of Córdoba, a city that Jews had settled since the destruction of the Second Temple and in which, for many centuries, they were the majority. The Rishonim (ראשונים) call it אליסנה, which, the lecturer explained, is the Spanish way of saying קהלי הושענא, אלי הושענא. The community asked: tell us how to pray. He sent them many letters, one of which, known already in the nineteenth century, before the Genizah was found, describes a custom that is the key to what follows.

VIII. Beis Rabbeinu SheBeBavel

The Little Sanctuary

The Gemara (גמרא) in Megillah (מגילה) 29a[20] explains the verse ואהי להם למקדש מעט as referring to the synagogues and study halls of Babylonia, and according to another view to Beis Rabbeinu SheBeBavel (בית רבינו שבבבל), which the lecturer, who notes the phrase is familiar to Chabad chassidim (חסידים) from 770, identifies with the house of Rav (רב) in Sura. From that original synagogue, he says, our synagogues and prayers derive their character.

The Order of Prayer in Rav’s Synagogue

The Mishnah (משנה) in Berachos (ברכות)[21] records that the early pious (chassidim harishonim (חסידים הראשונים)) would wait an hour before praying, and they also stayed an hour after prayer; so the Rambam (רמב״ם) codifies.[22] The lecturer notes that we do not do this today: we must hurry to the train. But in Rav (רב)’s academy the students remained in the synagogue, still in tallis (טלית) and tefillin (תפילין), opened Gemaras (גמרות) and Mishnayos (משניות), and learned for hours. Only when ready to leave did they rise and say Ashrei (אשרי), Lamnatzeach (למנצח), Uva LeTzion (ובא לציון) and Kaddish (קדיש), and depart.

The First Mention of Lamnatzeach

This, says the lecturer, is the first appearance of Lamnatzeach (למנצח), and it was not connected to the prayer proper at all. There was a long break, perhaps even Kiddush (קידוש) and something to eat, and then, as they removed tallis (טלית) and tefillin (תפילין) on their way out, they said Ashrei (אשרי), Lamnatzeach (למנצח) and Uva LeTzion (ובא לציון). That is what Rav Natronai (רב נטרונאי) wrote to Lucena: do the same.

A Legend of the Gaonic EraThe lecturer observes that stories of the great did not begin with the Baal Shem Tov (בעל שם טוב); even the Geonim (גאונים) have legends. Rav Natronai (רב נטרונאי) was so close to the communities of Spain that after his death a story spread that he had once appeared among them suddenly, by kefitzas haderech (קפיצת הדרך), from Babylonia, taught Torah, and returned. A century later Rav Hai Gaon (רב האי גאון) received a letter from Spain asking whether it was true, and replied that it never happened: he did not know who had fooled them; there are wonders, and Jacob our father had kefitzas haderech (קפיצת הדרך), but not this. Someone had appeared in Spain claiming to be Rav Natronai (רב נטרונאי) and invented the tale.[23]

The End of Lucena

Lucena was so significant that the Rif (רי״ף), Rabbeinu Yitzchak Alfasi (רבינו יצחק אלפסי), whose work stands at the back of every Gemara (גמרא), was its rabbi; so were Rabbi Yitzchak ibn Ghayyat (ר״י גאת) and Rabbi Yosef ibn Migash (רי מיגאש). Today there are no Jews in Lucena. About twenty years earlier, the lecturer reports, Spanish newspapers in Córdoba told that road works at Lucena had uncovered an ancient cemetery with more than three hundred graves, proving at last the size of the community. Only two of the three hundred gravestones remained intact and legible. The reason, he explains, is that Spanish law for many years allowed gravestones of a Jewish cemetery to be taken for building; who knows how many Spanish homes were built from the stones of our forefathers.

The lecturer recalls a similar sight in Nikolsburg, in Czechoslovakia, where a fence in the city, not far from the cemetery, is built entirely of gravestones, a sight he found heartrending. Such is the story of Israel in exile; but the Torah remains. The gravestones are gone, and we are alive and learning the same Torah the Rif learned.

IX. Why Lamnatzeach Here, and What It Means

The Eighteen Chapters

In the structure of Tehillim (תהלים), the Gemara (גמרא)[24] states several times that chapters one and two were originally a single chapter. Our twentieth chapter is therefore, in the older count, the nineteenth. And the Gemara (גמרא) counts the eighteen blessings of Shemoneh Esrei (שמונה עשרה) against the first eighteen chapters. After the eighteen comes, in that count, Lamnatzeach Mizmor LeDavid (למנצח מזמור לדוד). After our eighteen blessings, the lecturer concludes, we say Lamnatzeach (למנצח).

What the Chapter Says

יענך ה׳ ביום צרה[25]: Master of the universe, answer the prayers we have just begged for.

A Blessing Rooted in the Yerushalmi

The lecturer reports something he had come across the night before. The custom today, especially among chassidim (חסידים), at the graves of the righteous or after the prayers of the Yamim Noraim (ימים נוראים), is that one says to another מ׳האט געפועלט אלעס גוט, in Yiddish, that all one prayed for should come true; it has even been set to a song. The Yerushalmi (ירושלמי) in Berachos (ברכות), he says, is the source.[26] On the verse יענך ה׳ ביום צרה it teaches that when one sees a person praying, crying and asking, then after he finishes it is fitting to go to him and say that the Holy One should accept his prayer, and that this should be done every day.

The Communal Dimension

We do this every day. The Rishonim (ראשונים), on the basis of the Yerushalmi (ירושלמי), give this as the simple sense of reciting Lamnatzeach (למנצח) at the close of davening: one is not speaking to oneself but to the congregation. Saying יענך ה׳ ביום צרה, one blesses the person on one’s right and the person on one’s left, that God should answer all their prayers. It is not a selfish request. The lecturer calls it a beautiful intention for Lamnatzeach (למנצח).

X. Closing

There is much more to say on Lamnatzeach (למנצח), which is full of historical depth, and the lecturer promised to continue the following week. The discourse closed with the Mishnah (משנה): רבי חנניה בן עקשיא אומר רצה הקדוש ברוך הוא לזכות את ישראל.[27]

מראה מקומות  ·  Mareh Mekomos

  1. משנה מגילה ד׳:א׳; גמרא מגילה כ״א ע״א–ב. The Mishnah (Sefaria) fixes three readers on Monday, Thursday and Shabbos Minchah (שבת מנחה), with a blessing only for the first and the last. The Gemara on 21b takes up the later enactment (see the note following). The lecturer’s statement that one not prepared may not read is his own ruling and was not located in the passage consulted. ↩
  2. שלחן ערוך, אורח חיים רפ״ה; ברכות ח׳ ע״א. The obligation of shnayim mikra v’echad targum (שנים מקרא ואחד תרגום) is from Berachos (ברכות) 8a; its function as preparation for an aliyah (עליה) is as the lecturer reports the authorities. The text of the passage was not retrieved for this edition. ↩
  3. מגילה כ״א ע״ב — גזירה מפני הנכנסין ומפני היוצאין. Confirmed on Sefaria: the Gemara explains that the decree is on account of those who enter in the middle of the reading and those who leave early, lest they conclude that one blessing suffices. The earlier practice, that the first blessed before and the last after, is implied by the Gemara and stated in the Mishnah. ↩
  4. בבא קמא פ״ב ע״א. On Sefaria the Gemara derives the reading on Monday, Thursday and Shabbos (שבת) from וילכו שלשת ימים במדבר ולא מצאו מים (Shemos 15:22), water being Torah, and ascribes the original enactment to “the prophets among them”; Ezra (עזרא) instituted that three persons read, ten verses in all. The lecturer’s attribution to Moshe Rabbeinu (משה רבינו) was not located in this passage (Open Questions, no. 1). ↩
  5. שו״ת משנה הלכות, ר׳ מנשה קליין. Rabbi Menashe Klein, the Ungvarer Rav of Boro Park, is the author of the multi-volume Mishnah Halachos (משנה הלכות). The dates and the details of his biography, and the content of the tract on Baruch Hu uvaruch Shemo (ברוך הוא וברוך שמו), have not been verified here (Open Questions, no. 2). The remarks on the eruv (עירוב) dispute are the lecturer’s report. ↩
  6. ברכות נ״ד ע״ב. Confirmed on Sefaria. Four must give thanks: seafarers, desert travelers, one who recovered from illness, and one released from prison. Abaye derives the need for ten from בקהל עם and Mar Zutra the need for two sages from ובמושב זקנים (Tehillim 107:32); Rav Ashi’s objections are left unresolved. That a Torah scroll stands in for the two sages is the lecturer’s report and was not located in the Gemara. ↩
  7. טור, אורח חיים רי״ט. Confirmed on Sefaria: ארבעה צריכים להודות, before ten, with two sages, and the mnemonic וכל החיים יודוך סלה. The Rosh on Berachos (ברכות) ch. 9 likewise notes that the question of ten and two sages is unresolved and that one follows the stringent view. ↩
  8. Shalmei Nedarim (שלמי נדרים), introduction. The author’s name is uncertain: the recording renders it as “Alexander Sender HaKohen” followed by a garbled surname (Kaplan in one transcript, Shefelin in the summary). The rabbinate in Kupishok for forty years and the content of the introduction are as the lecturer reports; the book was not located on Sefaria (Open Questions, no. 3). ↩
  9. שבת קל״ז ע״ב — the blessing after a circumcision. Confirmed on Sefaria: those standing there say, “Just as he has entered into the covenant, so may he enter into Torah, marriage, and good deeds.” The lecturer first cited the page as 139; it is 137b. ↩
  10. רא״ש on ברכות, ch. 9. As the lecturer reports. On Sefaria the Rosh on Berachos (ברכות) 9:3 discusses the ten and two sages of HaGomel (הגומל); the comparison of the reading to a korban todah (קרבן תודה) was not found there and is unlocated (Open Questions, no. 4). That prayer stands in place of the offerings is Berachos (ברכות) 26b, confirmed. ↩
  11. Hisorerus Teshuvah (התעוררות תשובה), ר׳ שמעון סופר, rabbi of Erlau. Restored from the recording; the work and its author are as the lecturer reports, and the biographical details (age, years in the rabbinate, death in Auschwitz) are the grandson’s, relayed. Not located on Sefaria (Open Questions, no. 5). The summary garbled the title as Eretz Truva (אריץ טרובא) and the author’s work as Ateres Tzvi (עטרת צבי); neither is accepted here. ↩
  12. Machzor Vitry (מחזור ויטרי), ר׳ שמחה מויטרי. Rabbi Simcha of Vitry, a student of Rashi. The statement that the Yehi Ratzons (יהי רצון) are said only at Shabbos Minchah (שבת מנחה) is as the lecturer reports; the passage was not located (Open Questions, no. 6). ↩
  13. ברכות ד׳ ע״ב. Confirmed on Sefaria: Rabbi Elazar said in the name of Rabbi Avina, “Anyone who recites Tehillah LeDavid (תהלה לדוד) three times every day is assured of a place in the World to Come.” The Gemara concludes that the preeminence of the psalm comes from combining its alphabetical order with its mention of God’s sustaining all creation. ↩
  14. שלחן ערוך, אורח חיים נ״א:ז׳. Confirmed on Sefaria: one must concentrate on the verse פותח את ידך, and if one’s attention wandered one must return and say it again. The reference to the Mishnah Berurah (משנה ברורה) 51:16 in the working text was not retrieved and is not relied on (Open Questions, no. 7). ↩
  15. Gilyon HaShas (גליון הש״ס), Rabbi Akiva Eiger (רבי עקיבא איגר), on ברכות ד׳ ע״ב. The recording names Rabbi Akiva Eiger (רבי עקיבא איגר); the summary had misrendered it as the Ketzos HaChoshen (קצות החושן), an error carried into the working text and corrected here. Confirmed on Sefaria: the Gilyon HaShas on 4b has the comment beginning שם בכל יום ג״פ, to the effect that the Rosh lacks “three times” and that the Tur and the Rokeach (siman 320) likewise have the saying without it. The Rosh’s own text was not located (Open Questions, no. 8). ↩
  16. Aruch HaShulchan (ערוך השלחן), ר׳ יחיאל מיכל הלוי עפשטיין. Rabbi Yechiel Michel Epstein served in Navahrudak (Sefaria: composition 1865–1905). The lecturer’s figure of forty-five to fifty years as a rabbi is his own and is higher than the working text’s “over 35 years”; the dates 1829–1908 were not confirmed on Sefaria (Open Questions, no. 9). ↩
  17. Yesod VeShoresh HaAvodah (יסוד ושורש העבודה), ר׳ אלכסנדר זיסקינד מהורודנא. As the lecturer reports; the passage was not located. The passage of the Aruch HaShulchan (ערוך השלחן) on the closing Ashrei was likewise not retrieved (Open Questions, no. 9). ↩
  18. Sura (סורא) and the academy of Rav (רב). As the lecturer reports. That Rav (רב) moved his academy from Nehardea to Sura is not established from the sources consulted and is left as his report (Open Questions, no. 10). ↩
  19. תשובות רב נטרונאי גאון, ed. Brody (Jerusalem 1994). Rav Natronai bar Hilai headed Sura c. 853–858. The edition by Robert Brody is the modern critical one; the publisher named in the working text (Yad HaRav Herzog) was not confirmed and is omitted. The responsum to Lucena in which Lamnatzeach (למנצח) is first mentioned was not located (Open Questions, no. 11). ↩
  20. מגילה כ״ט ע״א. Confirmed on Sefaria. Rabbi Yitzchak reads ואהי להם למקדש מעט (Yechezkel 11:16) of the synagogues and study halls in Babylonia, and Rabbi Elazar of the house of our master in Babylonia, i.e. Rav. The lecturer’s identification of that house with a synagogue Rav founded at Sura is his report. ↩
  21. משנה ברכות ה׳:א׳; ברכות ל״ב ע״ב; ירושלמי ברכות ה׳:א׳. Confirmed on Sefaria. The Mishnah has the early pious wait an hour before praying; the Baraisa on 32b has them wait an hour, pray, and wait an hour after, from אשרי יושבי ביתך (Tehillim 84:5) and אך צדיקים יודו לשמך (140:14); the Yerushalmi on the Mishnah in 5:1 likewise speaks of an hour after prayer. The lecturer cited the Mishnah; the summary and the working text cited the Yerushalmi. ↩
  22. רמב״ם, הלכות תפילה ד׳:ט״ז. Confirmed on Sefaria: the pious of earlier generations would wait an hour before praying and an hour after. The halakhah cites no source. ↩
  23. The legend of Rav Natronai (רב נטרונאי); the reply of Rav Hai (רב האי). As the lecturer relates it. The reply of Rav Hai was not located. In the commonly told version the legend places Rav Natronai in Germany (Mainz) and not in Spain; the lecturer said Spain (Open Questions, no. 12). ↩
  24. ברכות ט׳ ע״ב. Confirmed on Sefaria: the Gemara counts nineteen chapters up to יהיו לרצון (Tehillim 19:15) against the Sages’ eighteen, and resolves it by treating chapters 1 and 2 as one. Hence our chapter 20 is the nineteenth in the older count. The working text had the numbers reversed; the lecturer’s own wording is unclear and is rendered here as the Gemara has it. ↩
  25. תהלים כ׳:ב׳. Confirmed on Sefaria: “May God answer you in time of trouble, the name of Jacob’s God keep you safe.” ↩
  26. ירושלמי ברכות. Not located. The Yerushalmi on Berachos (ברכות) 4:4 and 5:1 on Sefaria do not cite תהלים כ׳:ב׳ in this connection. The lecturer said he found the source the night before; he cited no chapter. The phrase he quoted is as transcribed from the recording (Open Questions, no. 13). ↩
  27. משנה מכות ג׳:ט״ז. The closing Mishnah (משנה) recited by the lecturer at the end of the recording; the text was not retrieved for this edition. ↩
