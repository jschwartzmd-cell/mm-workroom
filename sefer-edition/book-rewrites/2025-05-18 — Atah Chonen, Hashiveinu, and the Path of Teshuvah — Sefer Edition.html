The First Six Petitions of Shemoneh Esrei — Da’as, Teshuvah, Selichah, Refuah, Parnasah, and Personal Ge’ulah
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Sunday Morning Series on Tefillah
Recorded 18 May 2025  ·  Sefer Edition

Sponsored by Reb Ari Buckman, לעילוי נשמת יעקב ומשה.

I. The Architecture of the Middle Twelve

Shevach, Bakashah, Hoda’ah

The Shemoneh Esrei (שמונה עשרה), as Chazal (חז"ל) established it,[1] divides into three great units: three opening berachos of shevach (שבח), twelve middle berachos of bakashah (בקשה), and three closing berachos of hoda’ah (הודאה). Within the middle twelve a further subdivision governs the entire structure and dictates the order in which every petition of the day is arranged.

Personal, Then Communal

The twelve middle berachos themselves split into two symmetrical units of six. The first six are bakashos peratiyos (בקשות פרטיות) — personal petitions, each addressing a dimension of the individual’s life that stands in need of Heavenly assistance: de’ah (דעה), teshuvah (תשובה), mechilah (מחילה), refuah (רפואה), parnasah (פרנסה), and ge’ulah peratis (גאולה פרטית). The second six are bakashos ha’klal (בקשות הכלל) — petitions on behalf of Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל) as a whole, opening with Teka B’Shofar (תקע בשופר) for the ingathering of the exiles and Hashivah Shofteinu (השיבה שופטינו) for the restoration of the courts.

The mispallel approaches the middle twelve, therefore, first as an individual, and only then as a member of the collective. He first purifies and prepares his own nefesh (נפש); only when the private bakashos are complete does he lift his eyes to the needs of the nation.

II. Da’as as the Kashrus of Avodah

The Kohen Shoteh

Why is de’ah the first of all the petitions? The Rambam (רמב"ם) in Hilchos Bi’as HaMikdash[2] rules that a kohen shoteh (כהן שוטה) — a kohen who lacks da’as (דעת) — is pasul for avodah (עבודה) in the Beis HaMikdash. The Mishnah in Bechoros that catalogues the ba’alei mumin (בעלי מומין) enumerates only physical blemishes; the Rambam’s chiddush is that absence of da’as itself constitutes a mum (מום) — a mental mum — that disqualifies him from bringing a korban (קרבן) no less than a physical blemish disqualifies its bearer.

Tefillah kNeged HaKorbanos

Tefillah (תפילה) stands kneged ha’korbanos (כנגד הקרבנות); the Shemoneh Esrei is avodah performed in the absence of the Beis HaMikdash. It follows that the same kashrus (כשרות) that the Rambam requires of the kohen approaching the mizbeach is required of the mispallel approaching the Amud. Before any other bakashah, therefore, the mispallel asks for the very faculty without which the avodah of tefillah cannot be performed at all: chaneinu me’itcha de’ah, binah, v’haskel (חננו מאתך דעה בינה והשכל). Only after this kashrus is granted may the Shemoneh Esrei’s remaining eleven petitions be uttered as genuine avodah.

III. The Unique Opening of Atah Chonen

Every Other Petition Opens with the Ask

A careful survey of the middle twelve reveals a striking anomaly. Each of the other berachos of bakashah opens directly with its request — Hashiveinu, Selach Lanu, Re’eh Ve’anyeinu, Refa’einu, Barech Aleinu, Teka B’Shofar, Hashivah Shofteinu — each begins in the register of pure petition. Alone among them, Atah Chonen (אתה חונן) opens not with a request but with shevach: Atah chonen l’adam da’as (אתה חונן לאדם דעת) — You are the One who graciously grants da’as. Only after this praise does the berachah transition into its actual bakashah for de’ah, binah, v’haskel. Some other berachos, to be sure, close with a note of shevach — ki Kel rachum v’chanun Atah (כי א-ל רחום וחנון אתה) at the seal of Selach Lanu — but no other berachah opens with shevach. Why?

The Simple Answer — A Bridge from the First Three

The straightforward reading rests on the movement between the two units. The mispallel has just completed the three opening berachos of shevach; he now enters the register of bakashah. Chazal built into the first petition a structural bridge: they opened it in the tonality of the unit just concluded — carrying the shevach of Avos, Gevuros, and Kedushah one line further into the beginning of the new unit — before transitioning into the register of petition. The soul is escorted from praise into request rather than being made to leap between them.

The Deeper Reading of Rabbi Ephraim Zalman Margolios

A far more remarkable peshat is preserved in the writings of Rabbi Ephraim Zalman Margolios, zt”l (רבי אפרים זלמן מרגליות זצ"ל),[3] the rav of Brody in Galicia in the early nineteenth century. His stature warrants an introduction. He was a rare figure — simultaneously a gadol ha’dor (גדול הדור) and a man of great wealth — a combination whose classical precedent is Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi (רבי יהודה הנשיא), of whom the Gemara records that he became wealthy through diamond trading after his marriage, at a certain point told his wife “dayeinu” (דיינו), and, against the Gemara’s own general observation that yesh lo maneh rotzeh masayim (יש לו מנה רוצה מאתיים), stopped, invested the wealth, and turned his hours to Torah — in whose zechus he became the gadol ha’dor.

The city of Brody itself was known as “the Yerushalayim of Galicia” (ירושלים דגליציה) — each great region of the European exile had its Torah-capital, as Vilna was the Yerushalayim of Lita, and Brody was Galicia’s. The Margolios family, moreover, is one of the three families singled out by tradition attributed to the Baal Shem Tov ha’Kadosh[4] as possessing unbroken yichus (יחוס) across Klal Yisrael beyond the tribes of kehunah and leviyah: Rappaport, Margolios, and Horowitz. Rabbi Ephraim Zalman authored many sefarim, each bearing the name Ephraim — Yad Ephraim (glosses on Shulchan Aruch), Mateh Ephraim (the classic sefer on the halachos of Elul, the Yamim Nora’im, and Sukkos), Rosh Ephraim, and Zera Ephraim.

The Shabbos Basis of the Berachah

Rabbi Ephraim Zalman preserves, in the name of his uncle and rebbi Rabbi Alexander (רבי אלכסנדר סענדר מרגליות) — himself a gadol ha’dor — a reading of the berachah that draws its unique opening from an unexpected quarter: the halachos of Shabbos.

The halachah, grounded in the Yerushalmi,[5] is explicit: on Shabbos Kodesh, one does not daven for personal needs. Shabbos is a day for the Ribbono Shel Olam, not for one’s private tzaros (צרות). It is precisely for this reason that the Shemoneh Esrei of Shabbos and Yom Tov omits the twelve middle bakashos peratiyos and replaces them with the single middle berachah of kedushas ha’yom (קדושת היום). Personal petition, on Shabbos, has no home in the Amidah.

Nor does the restriction end at sunset. The Rishonim teach that even after Shabbos has departed, one may not resume personal bakashos until Havdalah (הבדלה) has been recited. And Havdalah in the tefillah itself is placed inside Atah Chonen at Motzaei Shabbos, in the insertion of Atah chonantanu (אתה חוננתנו).

The structural implication is exact. On the first weekday Ma’ariv after Shabbos, the berachah of Atah Chonen must be structured so that the mispallel does not utter any bakashah peratis until Havdalah has been made. The berachah, accordingly, is composed in three stages:

  1. It opens with shevach — Atah chonen l’adam da’as — because Havdalah has not yet been said and no personal request may yet be uttered.
  2. Havdalah is then inserted — Atah chonantanu.
  3. Only then does the mispallel proceed to the actual petition — chaneinu me’itcha de’ah, binah, v’haskel.

Once Chazal had calibrated the berachah in this way for Motzaei Shabbos, they preserved its opening structure for the entire week. Every weekday recitation of Atah Chonen, on this reading, retains the imprint of Motzaei Shabbos. Every weekday, in this precise sense, is lived as a Motzaei Shabbos in miniature — shevach first, and only then bakashah.

IV. Hashiveinu — The Question of the Ba’al Teshuvah

The Question

The word hashiveinu (השיבנו) — return us — assumes a prior location to which the mispallel once belonged and to which he now asks to be restored. But what of the secular Yid, raised entirely outside a Torah environment, who never learned so much as an alef-beis, and who now, as a genuine ba’al teshuvah, wishes to enter shul and daven the Shemoneh Esrei? He has no prior Torah to which he can return. To what does his hashiveinu point?

The First Answer — The Tinok B’Mei Immo

The Gemara in Niddah[6] supplies the first and deepest answer:

תִּינוֹק בִּמְעֵי אִמּוֹ מְלַמְּדִין אוֹתוֹ כָּל הַתּוֹרָה כֻּלָּהּ; וּכְשֶׁבָּא לַאֲוִיר הָעוֹלָם, בָּא מַלְאָךְ וְסוֹטְרוֹ עַל פִּיו וּמְשַׁכְּחוֹ.
A child in his mother’s womb is taught the entire Torah; when he comes into the world, a malach strikes his mouth and he forgets.
Niddah 30b

Every Yid, without exception — the most distant from Torah, the ba’al teshuvah who never opened a Chumash, the child of the most secular home — carries the entire Torah in the depths of his neshamah (נשמה) as an inheritance from the womb. What was forgotten at birth was not erased; it was merely veiled. Hashiveinu on this reading points precisely to that inner Torah: return us to what You once taught us before we entered the world. Every Yid has been there once; every Yid has a place to which he asks to be returned.

The Second Answer — Ma’amad Har Sinai

A second, complementary answer places the point of return not in the womb of the individual but in the ma’amad of the nation. Every neshamah of Klal Yisrael stood at Har Sinai (הר סיני) and received the Torah. Hashiveinu on this collective reading is a request for Klal Yisrael as a whole to be restored to what it collectively received at the mountain. Every Yid’s hashiveinu anchors him not merely in a personal past but in the national inheritance of the Torah given to the assembled klal.

V. Torah as the Instrument of Teshuvah

The Order — Hashiveinu Before Selach Lanu

Why does hashiveinu — the request for Torah — precede selach lanu — the request for selichah? The order teaches a foundational principle in the avodah of teshuvah: the road back to the Ribbono Shel Olam begins with Torah. Selichah follows; it does not come first. Chazal arranged the berachos in the sequence they arranged them because Torah is the instrument by which the returning Yid is first restored to a state in which selichah can be sought and received.

Real Gemara as What Holds

The chiddush emerges with particular clarity from the internal experience of kiruv rechokim (קירוב רחוקים) in the modern era. Rav Noach Weinberg, zt”l,[7] founder of Aish HaTorah, sat in his later years with the entire staff of the yeshiva — rabbeim, mashgichim, program directors — and put a hard question on the table. The yeshiva had tracked the outcomes of the bachurim who passed through it. In certain years the bachurim came alight and stayed — kept Shabbos, married bnos Yisrael, built Jewish homes. In other years, with the same inspiring speeches and the same Birthright framework, the bachurim came alight and then, within months, drifted back to where they had come from. What accounted for the difference?

The internal review yielded a single, disquieting answer. The years in which the yeshiva succeeded in grounding its bachurim in real, black Gemara — Bava Kamma (בבא קמא), Shnayim Ochazin B’Talis (שניים אוחזין בטלית), the dry intricacy of dinim and their sevaros — were the successful years. Not the inspiring speeches. Not the mountaintop moments. Bachurim who learned real Gemara, and only they, remained shomrei Torah u’mitzvos long-term. Those who received only inspirational programming eventually reverted.

The mechanism need not be fully articulable to be true. What learning Torah accomplishes — any Torah, at any level — is the restoration of the Yid to the connection he had in the womb. Inspiration touches the surface of the nefesh; Torah reaches beneath. The current generation is a generation of inspiration: viral clips, two-minute videos, drashos forwarded from phone to phone. There is genuine chizuk (חיזוק) in these; the point is not to disparage them. But none of them holds the Yid in place the way sitting with a Gemara holds him, or sitting with a Tur and Shulchan Aruch, or grappling with a difficult Rambam. Hashiveinu Avinu l’Torasecha is the tefillah’s recognition that teshuvah itself is inseparable from Torah: to return is to learn.

VI. Avinu in Shemoneh Esrei

Where Avinu Appears

The address Avinu (אבינו) — our Father — appears in the middle twelve berachos only twice (or three times, depending on nusach):

  • Hashiveinu Avinu l’Torasecha (השיבנו אבינו לתורתך).
  • Selach lanu Avinu ki chatanu (סלח לנו אבינו כי חטאנו).
  • In Nusach Sefard’s Sim Shalom: Barcheinu Avinu kulanu k’echad (ברכנו אבינו כולנו כאחד).

The Principle of the Abudraham

The Abudraham,[8] in his classic commentary on the Siddur, formulates the governing principle: Avinu appears in Shemoneh Esrei precisely in those berachos whose content corresponds to a father’s chiyuv (חיוב) to his son. The Gemara in Kiddushin[9] enumerates those chiyuvim: l’molo, lifdoso, l’lamdo Torah, l’hasi’o ishah, l’lamdo umanus (למולו, לפדותו, ללמדו תורה, להשיאו אשה, ללמדו אומנות) — and per one Tanna, also l’hashito ba’mayim (להשיטו במים). Among these, l’lamdo Torah stands as foundational.

An underappreciated implication of this chiyuv confronts every father in the contemporary world. Torah has been institutionalized. A father pays tuition, entrusts his son to a rebbi, and often assumes that his chiyuv has been discharged. The assumption is halachically mistaken. The chiyuv remains the father’s; sending a son to yeshiva and to tutors does not remove it. Sitting with one’s son and learning even a single mishnah is still a father’s obligation, and no institution can perform it in his place.

Hashiveinu Avinu — The Kavvanah

The Abudraham’s principle transforms the kavvanah of hashiveinu. The mispallel, in reciting Hashiveinu Avinu l’Torasecha, is invoking a specific legal claim: Ribbono Shel Olam, I am Your son. The halachah obligates a father to teach his son Torah. Therefore — hashiveinu Avinu l’Torasecha — teach me Your Torah. The tefillah stands not on abstract yearning but on the chiyuv of a father to a son.

Selach Lanu Avinu — The Kavvanah

The Abudraham’s principle explains the appearance of Avinu in the berachah of selichah from a different angle. The only path to selichah is through rachamim (רחמים). And the rachamim of a father, as the pasuk in Tehillim teaches,[10] is the paradigm of Hashem’s mercy: ke’rachem av al banim, ken yerachem Hashem al yerei’av (כרחם אב על בנים, כן ירחם ה’ על יראיו). The mispallel awakens the rachamei ha’av by addressing the Ribbono Shel Olam as Avinu in the very berachah in which rachamim is required to unlock selichah.

Why Not in Atah Chonen

Atah Chonen does not open with Avinu. The Abudraham’s principle accounts for the absence: da’as is not, halachically, a specific fatherly chiyuv. A subtle question presses at this point: is not parnasah also part of a father’s chiyuv to his young children? Should not Barech Aleinu, then, address Avinu? The point deserves reflection; the Abudraham’s framework, as elaborated by later meforshim, admits gradations in the chiyuv-content of each berachah.

VII. Chochmah, Binah, and Da’as

The Two Nusachos

The Tur, citing the Rokeach,[11] distinguishes the two nusachos of the berachah’s petition. Nusach Ashkenaz’s formulation — chaneinu me’itcha de’ah, binah, v’haskel — refers to general chochmah for life, the seichel not to do evil, only good. Nusach Sefard’s formulation — chaneinu me’itcha chochmah, binah, v’da’as — is focused specifically on the chochmah of Torah. The two nusachos are not interchangeable; each articulates a distinct kavvanah.

The Faculties Themselves

The three terms describe a progression. Chochmah (חכמה) is original wisdom — the flash of cognitive insight that arises from within. Binah (בינה) is derivative reasoning — the capacity described by Chazal as meivin davar mi’toch davar (מבין דבר מתוך דבר), deriving one thing from another. Da’as is the highest of the three — the faculty of connection, the binding of intellectual understanding to the emotional and volitional life of the nefesh. The Tanya of Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liadi[12] develops these three as the upper sefiros of the human nefesh, from which the middos flow. The acronym Chabad — Chochmah, Binah, Da’as — names the dynasty’s defining emphasis on intellectual avodah. The full elaboration of this reading belongs to the Tanya itself and is best encountered with the sefer open.

VIII. Bechirah and Tefillah — The Rambam’s Question

The Question

A fundamental question presses on the berachah of Hashiveinu. The Gemara’s formulation — ha’kol bi’yedei Shamayim chutz mi’yiras Shamayim (הכל בידי שמים חוץ מיראת שמים) — teaches that wealth, health, and every external circumstance are decreed from Above, while whether one is a tzaddik (צדיק) or a rasha (רשע) rests in his own hands. Bechirah (בחירה) is the defining faculty that distinguishes the Yid from the animal. If teshuvah is precisely what is placed in the Yid’s own hands, why is the Yid asking the Ribbono Shel Olam to do what is his own avodah to do? Hashiveinu appears, at first glance, to be a request for the Ribbono Shel Olam to perform work that only the mispallel himself can perform.

The Rambam’s Twenty-Four Obstacles

The Rambam in Hilchos Teshuvah, in the fourth perek,[13] provides the answer. He enumerates twenty-four categories — behaviors, patterns, and circumstances — that are me’akev es ha’teshuvah (מעכב את התשובה): that obstruct the successful performance of teshuvah even after the returning Yid has resolved to undertake it. The list includes habitual patterns of chet, chataim committed through others, persistent attachment to a particular chet, benefiting from misappropriated tzedakah, certain forms of lashon ha’ra, and many others. Many of these obstacles the mispallel himself carries, often without full awareness of doing so.

The tefillah of hashiveinu, on this reading, is precise. The Yid concedes that teshuvah itself lies within his own bechirah; he does not ask the Ribbono Shel Olam to do teshuvah for him. What he asks is for the obstacles — the structural impediments the Rambam enumerates — to be removed. Ribbono Shel Olam, remove the me’akvim, and I will return. The tefillah reserves to the Yid what belongs to him and asks of the Ribbono Shel Olam only what only He can provide.

IX. Selach Lanu — Tears, Kavvanah, and the Word Mechal

The Standards of Earlier Generations

Rabbi Yonasan Eibeschutz, in his Ya’aros Devash,[14] sets the standard for the berachah of selichah in unmistakable terms: a Yid who does not cry by selichah is worthless. The bar sounds foreign to a contemporary mispallel yet was the ordinary expectation of earlier generations. The tradition preserves the account of the chazan of Brisk who, at ordinary weekday Ma’ariv — not Ne’ilah, not Yom Kippur, not even Aseres Yemei Teshuvah — would leave a puddle of tears on the floor beneath his face from the standard weekday Shemoneh Esrei. The current generation has forgotten how to cry even about the fact that it has forgotten how to cry.

The Kavvanah Requirement

One of the Rishonim renders the requirement more sharply still: a Yid who recites selichah without kavvanah is a shoteh. The characterization is not rhetorical. To stand before the Melech and enumerate one’s chataim — chatanu, avinu, pashanu — and, at the end of the recitation, to have asked for nothing at all is a form of speech that no rational human being would ever direct at a human king. The berachah of selichah is a bakashah; if the mispallel forgets to ask, he has emptied the berachah of its content.

The Word Mechal

A striking philological point sharpens the meaning of the berachah. The root mem-ches-lamed (מ-ח-ל) in the sense of to forgive does not appear in Tanach. It is a lashon of Chazal. The Chumash uses salach and kaper for forgiveness; machah in Tanach means to erase, as in machah es shemam (מחה את שמם), not to forgive.

The Gra,[15] in his commentary on the Siddur, articulates a kelal: Anshei Knesses HaGedolah, in composing the Shemoneh Esrei, used only leshon Tanach. Yet mechal in its ordinary sense appears nowhere in Tanach — a sharp kushya on our nusach.

The recovery of ancient Siddurim from the Cairo Genizah[16] resolves the kushya. The original reading in the berachah of selichah was mechei (מחה) — erase — drawn directly from the pasuk of Moshe Rabbeinu’s plea after the Chet HaEgel: mechei na avoni mi’sifrecha asher kasavta (מחה נא עוני מספרך אשר כתבת). Over centuries, in the European nusachos, the reading shifted from mechei to mechal. The received nusach is the received nusach, and one does not change it; but the original word, and the deeper kavvanah of the berachah, is erasure.

Three Levels of Forgiveness

The berachah in fact contains three graduated levels of forgiveness, arranged in ascending order:

  • Selach (סלח) — the first and outermost level, surface forgiveness. The Magen Avraham[17] teaches that at this level the roshem (רושם) of the chet remains — like a stain not fully removed by the cleaners, faded but still visible.
  • Mechal, originally mechei (מחה) — total erasure. Mechei ve’ha’aver avonoseinu ve’chatoseinu mi’neged einecha (מחה והעבר עוונותינו וחטאתינו מנגד עיניך) — not merely forgiven but wiped clean.
  • Kaper (כפר) — atonement. The chet is not only erased but covered, as on Yom HaKippurim: not merely absent but positively enclosed.

The mispallel ascends the ladder in a single berachah — asking not for a half-measure but for the full transformation of the record.

X. Refa’einu — Adding the Sick

Inserting the Choleh

The Shulchan Aruch, drawing on the Arizal,[18] rules that when a Yid knows of someone who is ill, the appropriate place to daven for the refuah is within the berachah of Refa’einu. The Beis Yosef preserves an alternative view that prefers to save such bakashos for the end of Shemoneh Esrei, out of concern that adding at the moment might risk missing Kedushah; the practical concern is minimal — the insertion adds only seconds — and the settled practice is to insert the name of the choleh in the berachah itself.

The Arizal on Spiritual Illness

The Arizal adds a striking further teaching: in the berachah of Selach Lanu, the mispallel should daven for the spiritually ill — Yidden who do not keep Shabbos, who are far from Torah, who have been raised at a distance from Yiddishkeit. The mispallel does not judge; the Ribbono Shel Olam is the Shofet. But the Arizal teaches that such Yidden are to be recognized as cholim in another register, and the request for their refuah belongs precisely in the berachah of selichah: not refa’einu for the body, but selach lanu for the neshamos that stand in need of the same rachamim the berachah invokes.

Personal Bakashos — Me’ein HaBerachah

The general halachah permits the mispallel to insert personal bakashos within any of the middle twelve berachos provided the insertion is me’ein ha’berachah (מעין הברכה) — within the theme of the berachah. Most mispallelim save personal petition for the end of Shemoneh Esrei; the insertion within the relevant berachah, however, personalizes the tefillah and connects the abstract bakashah to a specific tzarah. The berachah of Shema Koleinu in any case is general and admits any request.

XI. Ge’aleinu — A Personal Ge’ulah

Not the Ge’ulah HaShleimah

A common misreading assumes that the berachah of Ge’aleinu refers to the ge’ulah ha’shleimah with the coming of Mashiach. It does not. The ge’ulah ha’shleimah appears later in the Shemoneh Esrei — in Teka B’Shofar Gadol l’cheiruseinu (תקע בשופר גדול לחירותנו) and in V’liYerushalayim Ircha B’Rachamim Tashuv (ולירושלים עירך ברחמים תשוב). The seventh berachah speaks to something else entirely.

Ge’ulah Peratis

The Gemara in Megillah with Rashi’s peirush[19] establishes that the seventh berachah is ge’ulah peratis — a personal ge’ulah. Each Yid, whatever the state of the collective galus, is in his own personal Mitzrayim (מצרים) — the daily tzaros, difficulties, and impossibilities of his own life. After the berachos of teshuvah and selichah have restored him to a state in which ge’ulah is available to him, the berachah of Ge’aleinu asks the Ribbono Shel Olam to take him out of his personal Mitzrayim: Go’aleinu, Go’el chazak l’ma’an Shemecha (גואלנו, גואל חזק למען שמך).

Why the Seventh Berachah

The Gemara supplies a further angle on the placement: Mashiach will come in the seventh year — the year of Shemittah — and the berachah of ge’ulah is therefore placed as the seventh of the middle twelve. The number itself is a remez. A famous kushya was once posed to the Brisker Rav: the Gemara teaches that Mashiach will not come on Erev Shabbos — the sensitivities of Mashiach are such that he does not arrive amid the hectic preparations of a Jewish home on Erev Shabbos; how, then, can the Gemara also teach that Mashiach will arrive in the seventh year specifically? The Brisker Rav’s reply is preserved: When Mashiach comes, I will answer the question.

XII. The Three Sheleimos

Three of the middle berachos employ the word sheleimah (שלמה) — complete:

  • Teshuvah sheleimah (תשובה שלמה).
  • Refuah sheleimah (רפואה שלמה).
  • Ge’ulah sheleimah (גאולה שלמה).

The insistence on sheleimus in these three berachos alone is not stylistic. A teshuvah that is not sheleimah is not truly teshuvah; a refuah that is not sheleimah is not truly refuah; a ge’ulah that is not sheleimah is not truly ge’ulah. Each of these three states admits of no half-measure: a partial teshuvah leaves the sinner still a sinner; a partial refuah leaves the choleh still ill; a partial ge’ulah leaves the galus intact. The mispallel does not daven for halves. He asks for the full transformation, or he has not asked at all.

XIII. The Galus One Does Not See

Being Inside a Tzarah Without Knowing It

The berachah of Ge’aleinu reaches into a dimension that most mispallelim underestimate: the tzaros a Yid carries without recognizing them as such. The galus one does not see is often the most dangerous galus. Three historical episodes illustrate the point — each of them a case in which Yidden failed to perceive what was, in retrospect, unmistakable.

German Jewry Before the War

Before the Second World War, when yemach shemo came to power, certain segments of German Jewry read the signs correctly and left. Others — sophisticated kehillos with deep cultural confidence in the Germany that had granted them citizenship — said: It is a gezeirah; it will pass; everything will be fine. They are part of the six million kedoshim.

The Mir Diary

Rav Shimon Schwab once described having seen the diary of a bachur in Mir, dated to tarach (תר"ץ) — the year before the war broke out. Mir was in Poland. The bachur wrote: “I have business to attend to in Germany, but the political situation there makes travel impossible. Baruch Hashem — here in Poland things are secure. I will wait until matters settle in Germany before traveling.” He did not know what was coming.

The Bobover Rebbe’s Story

A third account was preserved by the Bobover Rebbe[20] and heard by Rabbi Ephrati directly from him during a nichum aveilim visit. A Galician Yid, having escaped the Polish pera’os in the earliest phase, crossed the border into Hungary — disheveled, torn, blood on his clothing — and entered a Hungarian shtibl to warn the local kehillah. He found them sitting after Shacharis, having a kiddush, drinking l’chaim, kibitzing. He cried out to them: “Yidden, tayere Yidden, men shecht Yidden!” (אידן, טייערע אידן, מען שעכט אידן) — Jews, precious Jews, they are slaughtering Jews. They laughed at him and continued drinking. Within months they were all killed.

The Prayer’s True Register

Go’aleinu, Go’el chazak is the tefillah for the galus one is inside without knowing it. The mispallel asks the Ribbono Shel Olam not only for redemption from the tzaros he perceives, but for redemption from the tzaros whose contours he cannot yet make out — the walls of the personal Mitzrayim that press against him without ever announcing themselves. The berachah asks for eyes to see what one has not yet noticed, and for the strength of the Go’el chazak to take one out even from a galus one has not yet named.

XIV. The Six Together

The six bakashos peratiyos form a single sequence, and each berachah presupposes the previous and prepares the next.

  • Receive da’as — the kashrus to perform the avodah of tefillah at all.
  • Let the obstacles to teshuvah be removed — the twenty-four me’akvim the Rambam enumerates.
  • Let the chataim themselves be forgiven, erased, and atoned — selach, mechei, kaper.
  • Let the body be healed — refuah sheleimah.
  • Let the household have parnasah.
  • And through all of these, let the Yid be redeemed from his private Mitzrayim.

The architecture is precise. It is the map of the Yid’s personal avodah, laid down by Chazal in the exact sequence in which the nefesh must be prepared. May every mispallel merit to daven these six berachos with the awareness the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah built into them, and through them to be inscribed for chaim tovim.

Mental incapacity functions as a structural mum. The pesak grounds the reading that tefillah, which stands kneged ha’korbanos, requires the same kashrus of da’as as the korban itself, so that the first bakashah of the middle twelve is necessarily the bakashah for the kashrus to daven at all. ↩

The sugya grounds the first answer to the question of hashiveinu for the ba’al teshuvah: every Yid has an inner Torah to which he can be returned. ↩

Talmid of Rav Yitzchak Hutner, zt”l, at Yeshivas Chaim Berlin and earlier of his elder brother Rav Yaakov Weinberg, zt”l, at Ner Yisrael. The internal review of Aish HaTorah’s outcomes — that the cohorts of bachurim grounded in real Gemara learning sustained their shemiras Torah u’mitzvos long-term, while cohorts receiving only inspirational programming did not — circulates in kiruv and chinuch circles as a working principle: real kevius in limud holds, where inspiration alone fades.   8. Abudraham — principle of Avinu in berachos tied to fatherly chiyuvim. — Rabbi Dovid Abudraham (Seville, mid-fourteenth century), in his classic commentary on the Siddur, develops the principle that Avinu appears in Shemoneh Esrei precisely in the berachos tied to a father’s chiyuv: Hashiveinu Avinu l’Torasecha (the chiyuv l’lamdo Torah) and Selach lanu Avinu ki chatanu (the chiyuv of fatherly rachamim). The principle accounts both for the presence of Avinu in these berachos and for its absence in the others. ↩
  9. Kiddushin 29a — father’s chiyuvim to his son, including l’lamdo Torah. — The Mishnah and sugya in Kiddushin enumerate the father’s chiyuvim to his son: l’molo, lifdoso, l’lamdo Torah, l’hasi’o ishah, l’lamdo umanus (and per the Tanna Kamma, also l’hashito ba’mayim). The chiyuv of l’lamdo Torah is foundational. The Abudraham’s reading of Hashiveinu Avinu l’Torasecha rests on this sugya: when the mispallel addresses Hashem as Avinu in connection with Torah, he is invoking precisely the fatherly chiyuv codified here. ↩
  10. Tehillim 103:13 — ke’rachem av al banim, ken yerachem Hashem al yerei’av. — Dovid HaMelech’s pasuk in Tehillim 103 establishes the dugma of fatherly rachamim as the model for Hashem’s rachamim. The pasuk is the textual ground for the Abudraham’s reading of Selach lanu Avinu ki chatanu: selichah requires rachamim; the rachamim of a father is the deepest available form; the mispallel therefore addresses Hashem as Avinu in this berachah specifically. ↩
  11. Tur Orach Chaim, siman 115, citing the Rokeach — de’ah, binah, v’haskel vs. chochmah, binah, v’da’as. — The Tur, siman 115, brings Rabbi Elazar of Worms (the Rokeach, c. 1176–1238; talmid of Rabbi Yehudah HaChassid) on the two nusachos. De’ah, binah, v’haskel in Nusach Ashkenaz’s Chaneinu refers to general chochmah for life; chochmah, binah, v’da’as in Nusach Sefard is focused specifically on chochmas haTorah. The two nusachos are not interchangeable; each reflects a distinct kavvanah. ↩
  12. Tanya (Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liadi) — Chochmah, Binah, Da’as as sefiros in Chabad. — Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liadi (the Ba’al HaTanya / Admur HaZaken, 1745–1812, founder of Chabad chassidus) develops Chochmah, Binah, Da’as as the upper three sefiros of the human nefesh — the cognitive faculties from which the middos (Chesed, Gevurah, Tiferes) flow. Chochmah: original cognitive flash. Binah: derivative reasoning and expansion. Da’as: the binding of cognitive understanding to emotional experience. The acronym Chabad names the dynasty’s defining emphasis on intellectual avodah. ↩
  13. Rambam, Hilchos Teshuvah, perek 4 — twenty-four obstacles to teshuvah. — The Rambam in the fourth perek of Hilchos Teshuvah enumerates twenty-four categories of behavior, attitude, and circumstance that me’akev es ha’teshuvah — obstruct teshuvah from succeeding. The list includes habitual chataim; chataim committed through others; persistent attachment to a particular chet; benefiting from tzedakas rabbim taken under false pretenses; certain forms of lashon ha’ra; and many more. The Rambam’s framework grounds the tefillah of hashiveinu: although teshuvah itself lies in the ba’al bechirah’s hands (ha’kol bi’yedei Shamayim chutz mi’yiras Shamayim), the structural obstacles in his way require siyua from Above to remove. ↩
  14. Ya’aros Devash (Rabbi Yonasan Eibeschutz) — tears at selichah; the Yid who does not cry is worthless. — Rabbi Yonasan Eibeschutz (c. 1690–1764; Prague, Metz, Hamburg-Altona) was one of the great darshanim of his generation. His Ya’aros Devash is a two-volume collection of drashos delivered in his shul in Prague; some individual drashos run forty-five pages or more. On Birchas Selichah he sets the standard: a Yid who can stand in Shemoneh Esrei, recite Selach lanu Avinu ki chatanu, and feel nothing has not understood what he is saying. The standard preserves the weight of weekday tefillah in earlier generations, exemplified by the chazan of Brisk who left a puddle of tears on the floor at ordinary weekday Ma’ariv. ↩
  15. Bi’ur HaGra on the Siddur — Anshei Knesses HaGedolah used only leshon Tanach; the kushya on mechal. — The Gra in his bi’ur on the Siddur develops the kelal that Anshei Knesses HaGedolah composed Shemoneh Esrei exclusively in leshon Tanach — language drawn directly from biblical sources. The principle raises a sharp kushya on mechal lanu, since the root mem-ches-lamed in the sense of forgive appears nowhere in Tanach (salach and kaper are the biblical roots for forgiveness). The kushya is resolved by the Cairo Genizah’s recovery of older Siddurim reading mechei — drawn from mechei na avoni (Shemos 32:32) — which over time shifted to mechal. ↩
  16. Cairo Genizah — recovery of ancient Siddurim preserving mechei (מחה, root mem-ches-hei, erase) as the original reading in Birchas Selichah, in place of the contemporary mechal (root mem-ches-lamed, forgive). The Genizah’s Siddurim — including fragments of the Siddur of Rav Saadya Gaon and various Eretz Yisrael-rite Siddurim — confirm that the lashon originally drew on pesukim such as mechei na avoni mi’sifrecha asher kasavta (Moshe Rabbeinu’s plea after Chet HaEgel, Shemos 32:32) and was therefore in leshon Tanach as the Gra’s kelal requires. The shift from mechei to mechal occurred over centuries in the European nusachos. Not Sefaria-indexable; preserved.
    ↩
  17. Magen Avraham on the three levels of forgiveness in Birchas Selichah: selach, mechal, kaper. — The Magen Avraham (Rabbi Avraham HaLevi Gombiner, c. 1635–1682), in his commentary on Shulchan Aruch Orach Chaim, develops the three-tier structure embedded in Birchas Selichah. Selach — surface forgiveness; the roshem of the chet remains, like a stain not fully removed. Mechal (originally mechei) — total erasure: mechei ve’ha’aver avonoseinu ve’chatoseinu mi’neged einecha. Kaper — atonement: the chet not only erased but covered, as on Yom HaKippurim. The mispallel ascends the ladder in a single berachah. ↩
  18. Arizal and Shulchan Aruch Orach Chaim, siman 119 — inserting names of the choleh in Refa’einu; davening in Selach Lanu for the spiritually ill. — The Shulchan Aruch in siman 119 (and surrounding simanim) rules on the insertion of personal bakashos within the middle twelve berachos: one may insert me’ein ha’berachah (within the theme of the berachah), specifically including names of the sick in Refa’einu. The Arizal in Sha’ar HaKavannos further develops the kabbalistic dimension and adds the teaching that in Selach Lanu, one davens for those who are spiritually ill — distant from Torah, not keeping Shabbos — recognizing them as cholim in another register, and asking for their refuah through the berachah of selichah. ↩
  19. Megillah 17b with Rashi — Ge’aleinu as ge’ulah peratis; ge’ulah as the seventh berachah. — The sugya in Megillah on the seder berachos of Shemoneh Esrei, with Rashi’s peirush, distinguishes the seventh berachah (Re’eh Ve’anyeinu... Go’el Yisrael) from the berachah of kibutz galuyos and from V’liYerushalayim. The seventh is ge’ulah peratis — redemption from one’s personal tzaros, one’s daily Mitzrayim — distinct from the messianic ge’ulah ha’shleimah that appears later. The sugya further notes that the berachah sits as the seventh because Mashiach will arrive in the seventh year (Shemittah); the position is not arbitrary. ↩
