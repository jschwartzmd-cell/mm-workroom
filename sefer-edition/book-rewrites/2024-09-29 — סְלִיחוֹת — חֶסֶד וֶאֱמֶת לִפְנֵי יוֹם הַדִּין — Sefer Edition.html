סְלִיחוֹת — חֶסֶד וֶאֱמֶת לִפְנֵי יוֹם הַדִּין

Slichos: Chesed v'Emes Before Yom HaDin
Drashah of Rabbi Ephrati — Motzaei Shabbos, Opening Night of Slichos
Delivered 29 September 2024, 1:46 AM  ·  Sefer Edition

For the soldiers of Klal Yisrael (כְּלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל) who kissed their wives and children on that first night of Slichos (סְלִיחוֹת) and turned north toward Lebanon — and for the widows, orphans, and captives of the war that followed Simchas Torah (שִׂמְחַת תּוֹרָה) of Tav-Shin-Peh-Daled (תשפ״ד).

I. The Brisker Rav’s Mashal — Three Mindsets at the Border

A Motzaei Shabbos Tradition

The Brisker Rav (הַבְּרִיסְקֶ׳ר רַב), zecher tzaddik livrachah (זֵכֶר צַדִּיק לִבְרָכָה),[1] was accustomed to deliver a brief drashah (דְּרָשָׁה) in his home minyan (מִנְיָן) every Motzaei Shabbos (מוֹצָאֵי שַׁבָּת) before the first Slichos (סְלִיחוֹת). The pizmon (פִּזְמוֹן) that inaugurates the Ashkenazic Slichos speaks of Klal Yisrael (כְּלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל) trembling before the approaching Yom HaDin (יוֹם הַדִּין) — a trembling that, taken at face value, is difficult for the modern worshipper to summon on cue. To make that trembling real, the Brisker Rav offered a mashal (מָשָׁל) drawn from the world he himself remembered.

The Three Figures at the Border

In the alte heim (אַלְטֶע הֵיים) of pre-war Europe, Jews were barred from a great many ordinary trades. Not a few turned to smuggling across borders for parnassah (פַּרְנָסָה). Picture, then, a Jew who has arranged a major deal: on a single moonless night he will smuggle costly merchandise from Poland into Hungary. He has engaged a balagulah (בַּעַל עֲגָלָה) who knows the back roads. The crossing has been set for thirty days out.

For thirty days that Jew lives with palpable fear. The stakes press on him: if he is caught, he loses everything he has invested, ends up in prison, and leaves his wife and children with no parnassah. He davens, he prepares, he plans, he checks and rechecks every detail. In the thirty days between the deal and the crossing, he becomes a different man.

The balagulah, by contrast, has less at stake. If they are caught, he loses his horse, perhaps his wagon; he sits in prison a few days, pays a knas (קְנָס), and returns to ordinary life. He prepares somewhat — but not with the merchant’s intensity.

The horse — the sus (סוּס) — has no awareness at all. It will pull the wagon across the border tonight or it will remain in the stable; either way, no preparation, no concept, no fear.

Three Levels of Slichos

The nimshal (נִמְשָׁל) is severe in its plainness. There are three ways to arrive at Yom HaDin.

The aspiration: to be like the merchant. To prepare for thirty days. The month of Elul (אֱלוּל) is built for precisely this — weeping and teshuvah (תְּשׁוּבָה), cheshbon ha’nefesh (חֶשְׁבּוֹן הַנֶּפֶשׁ) and hisorerus (הִתְעוֹרְרוּת), a full thirty-day discipline that turns a Jew into a different person by the time Rosh HaShanah (רֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה) dawns.

The minimum: to be at least like the balagulah. In an Ashkenazic year like this one, when only four days of Slichos stand before Rosh HaShanah, do not let those four days slip past. Rise early, feel the weight of the days approaching, take the seriousness of Yom HaDin to heart.

The warning: not to be the horse. To enter the courtroom of Yom HaDin with no awareness, no teshuvah, no preparation, is a tragedy of a different order altogether. The horse is pulled across the border whether or not it understands what is happening to it. A Jew is not made to be a horse.

II. The Yud-Gimmel Middos and the Question They Force

The Gemara in Rosh HaShanah

The central pizmon of Slichos — and of Aseres Yemei Teshuvah (עֲשֶׂרֶת יְמֵי תְּשׁוּבָה) as a whole — is the thirteen middos ha’rachamim (מִדּוֹת הָרַחֲמִים): Hashem, Hashem, Keil rachum v’chanun, erech apayim, v’rav chesed v’emes. Whence do these middos draw their koach (כֹּחַ)? The Gemara (גְּמָרָא) in Rosh HaShanah[2], expounding the verse Vaya’avor Hashem al panav vayikra (וַיַּעֲבֹר ה׳ עַל פָּנָיו וַיִּקְרָא)[3], records a teaching so bold that the Gemara itself pauses to say it could not be uttered were it not written in the verse:

אִלְמָלֵא מִקְרָא כָּתוּב, אִי אֶפְשָׁר לְאוֹמְרוֹ. מְלַמֵּד שֶׁנִּתְעַטֵּף הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא כִּשְׁלִיחַ צִבּוּר וְהֶרְאָה לוֹ לְמֹשֶׁה סֵדֶר תְּפִלָּה. אָמַר לוֹ: כָּל זְמַן שֶׁיִּהְיוּ יִשְׂרָאֵל חוֹטְאִים, יַעֲשׂוּ לְפָנַי כַּסֵּדֶר הַזֶּה, וַאֲנִי מוֹחֵל לָהֶם.
Were it not written in the verse, it would be impossible to say. This teaches that HaKadosh Baruch Hu wrapped Himself like a shliach tzibbur and showed Moshe the order of prayer. He said to him: “Whenever Yisrael sin, let them perform before Me this very order, and I will forgive them.”
Rosh HaShanah 17b

From this Gemara flows the universal minhag (מִנְהָג) that the shliach tzibbur (שְׁלִיחַ צִבּוּר) for Slichos dons a tallis (טַלִּית), even at night when he would otherwise not do so, enacting the image of HaKadosh Baruch Hu (הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא) Himself wrapped as a shliach tzibbur. The minhag carries a well-known halakhic complication — night is generally lav zman tzitzis (לָאו זְמַן צִיצִית), and the chazan (חַזָּן) is technically patur (פָּטוּר) — and the accepted practice is either to borrow a tallis or to wear one without a brachah (בְּרָכָה). The minhag stands nonetheless, for its whole point is not the mitzvah (מִצְוָה) of tzitzis (צִיצִית) but the enactment of the Divine image.

The Question Every Year Demands

If reciting the thirteen middos is the master key promised by the Gemara — ya’asu lefanai ka’seder ha’zeh va’ani mochel lahem — why have Klal Yisrael been reciting them year after year, for centuries beyond number, and the geulah (גְּאֻלָּה) has not come? The year just passed was no good year; the year before it, no better. The tzaros (צָרוֹת) persist. Where is the promised forgiveness?

The answer lies in a single word of the Gemara’s formulation. Ya’asu — let them perform, not merely ya’omru, let them recite. The Gemara in Sotah[4] supplies the operative principle behind that choice of verb:

מָה הוּא רַחוּם — אַף אַתָּה רַחוּם. מָה הוּא חַנּוּן — אַף אַתָּה חַנּוּן.
As He is merciful, so you must be merciful. As He is gracious, so you must be gracious.
Sotah 14a; cf. Rambam, Hilchos De’os 1:6

The thirteen middos are not a formula pronounced but a discipline enacted. To utter rachum without cultivating rachamim (רַחֲמִים), to utter chanun without practicing graciousness, is to hand up an empty vessel. The she’arei rachamim (שַׁעֲרֵי רַחֲמִים) open precisely when the recitation is matched by the avodah (עֲבוֹדָה) the words describe. The classical poskim (פּוֹסְקִים) read the Gemara’s ya’asu in a narrower liturgical register as well — that the thirteen middos must be said b’tzibbur (בְּצִבּוּר), with the proper seder (סֵדֶר) and introductory pesukim (פְּסוּקִים) — and that reading too is preserved in halachah (הֲלָכָה). But the deeper reading, the reading upon which the drashah of these days rests, is the behavioral one: walk the path of the middos, and only then does the recitation open the gates.

III. Standing as One — The Teaching of Tanna D’Bei Eliyahu

A second wellspring for Slichos appears in Tanna D’Bei Eliyahu (תַּנָּא דְּבֵי אֵלִיָּהוּ)[5], the compilation traditionally attributed to the teachings that Eliyahu HaNavi (אֵלִיָּהוּ הַנָּבִיא) transmitted in his daily study-partnership with Rav Anan. There, the frame is not the Gemara’s seder tefillah (סֵדֶר תְּפִלָּה) but a question posed by David HaMelech (דָּוִד הַמֶּלֶךְ). Ribbono shel olam (רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם), he asks — what shall Klal Yisrael do in an age when there is no Beis HaMikdash (בֵּית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ), no korbanos (קָרְבָּנוֹת), no Avodah (עֲבוֹדָה), when the people is sunk in galus (גָּלוּת) and hester panim (הֶסְתֵּר פָּנִים)? To which HaKadosh Baruch Hu answers with a specific instruction: stand before Me בַּאֲגֻדָּה אַחַת — as one bound bundle — and recite the thirteen middos.

The teaching’s force lies in its logic of substitution. In the age of the Mikdash (מִקְדָּשׁ), the korban tzibbur (קָרְבַּן צִבּוּר) was the vessel through which the many became one avodah before their Creator. In the age of exile, what takes its place is Klal Yisrael itself, bound together into a single organism — and this bundled community, uttering the middos as one, is the vessel that draws down the response. The unity does not merely accompany the tefillah; it constitutes the medium in which the tefillah operates.

The practical translation of agudah achas is not sentimental. It is tzedakah (צְדָקָה) — not only tzedakah b’mamon (צְדָקָה בְּמָמוֹן) but also tzedakah b’guf (צְדָקָה בְּגוּף): shouldering another Jew’s burden, nosei b’ol im chaveiro (נוֹשֵׂא בְּעֹל עִם חֲבֵרוֹ), lifting up a spirit that has bent under its load. The gates of rachamim open before a people whose members present themselves as a single body, not as a crowd of separate suppliants.

IV. Tzedek u’Mishpat, Chesed v’Emes

The opening line of Slichos in many kehillos (קְהִלּוֹת) is the verse from Tehillim (תְּהִלִּים):[6]

צֶדֶק וּמִשְׁפָּט מְכוֹן כִּסְאֶךָ; חֶסֶד וֶאֱמֶת יְקַדְּמוּ פָנֶיךָ.
Righteousness and justice are the foundation of Your throne; chesed and emes precede Your face.
Tehillim 89:15

The verse describes the courtroom of Yom HaDin and instructs how to approach it. Tzedek u’mishpat is the foundation of the throne: Hashem sits in strict justice and the ledger is opened. Chesed v’emes yikadmu fanecha — chesed and emes precede that face. Bring them into the courtroom before judgment is rendered.

The Chasam Sofer (חֲתַם סוֹפֵר)[7] notes a gematria (גִּימַטְרִיָּא) that gathers the point in numerical form. Chesed (חֶסֶד) is seventy-two; emes (אֱמֶת) is four hundred and forty-one; together, five hundred and thirteen. Slichos equals five hundred and fourteen — a difference of one, resolved by the standard kollel (כּוֹלֵל) of one for the word itself. The work of these days, the gematria hints, is nothing other than the work of chesed and emes.

What Is Emes?

Emes, in Chazal (חֲזַ״ל)’s reading, is Torah (תּוֹרָה). Emes k’neh v’al timkor (אֱמֶת קְנֵה וְאַל תִּמְכֹּר)[8] — buy truth and do not sell it — refers, in the classical understanding, to Torah itself, the one truth acquired that a person must never surrender. Real teshuvah begins with returning to that truth: with taking up again what the book instructs a Jew to do and what it forbids him from doing. The opening step of the parshas teshuvah (פָּרָשַׁת תְּשׁוּבָה)[9] read on the Shabbos (שַׁבָּת) preceding this Slichos is v’shamata b’kolo (וְשָׁמַעְתָּ בְּקֹלוֹ) — to hear His voice, which lives in Torah Shebiksav (תּוֹרָה שֶׁבִּכְתָב) and in Torah She’baal Peh (תּוֹרָה שֶׁבְּעַל פֶּה).

In an earlier generation, Slichos-time meant more Torah: an added shiur (שִׁעוּר), an extra shtikel Gemara (שְׁטִיקֶעל גְּמָרָא), a nightly page of mussar (מוּסָר), an added kapitel Tehillim (קַאפִּיטֶעל תְּהִלִּים).[10] We are shvachei doros (חַלָּשֵׁי הַדּוֹרוֹת), weakened generations, but a kabbalah (קַבָּלָה) on one concrete piece of emes — a single halachah observed with new precision, a single fixed seder added — is within reach of any Jew who resolves to reach for it.

What Is Chesed?

Chesed is practical action: tzedakah when it is hard, kindness when it is inconvenient, generosity when it costs.

V. Malveh Hashem Chonein Dal — A Daring Application

The Baal HaTanya’s Letter

The koach (כֹּחַ) of that practical chesed (חֶסֶד) is captured in a startling formulation from the Alter Rebbe, the Baal HaTanya (בַּעַל הַתַּנְיָא), in the famous Igros Kodesh (אִגְּרוֹת קֹדֶשׁ) appended to Sefer HaTanya. Were Jews truly to understand what tzedakah (צְדָקָה) accomplishes, the Alter Rebbe writes, they could not tear themselves away from giving it. His anchor is the verse in Mishlei (מִשְׁלֵי): Malveh Hashem chonein dal (מַלְוֵה ה׳ חוֹנֵן דָּל)[11] — one who shows favor to the poor lends to Hashem (הַשֵּׁם). Tzedakah is not a gift but a loan. And HaKadosh Baruch Hu (הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא) repays His loans.

The general rule of Chazal (חֲזַ״ל) is sechar mitzvah b’hai alma leikah (שְׂכַר מִצְוָה בְּהַאי עָלְמָא לֵיכָּא) — the reward of a mitzvah (מִצְוָה) is not paid in this world; the principal awaits olam ha’ba (עוֹלָם הַבָּא). Tzedakah is one of the classical exceptions, and the exception operates through a distinct mechanism: it is not the ordinary reward-scheme of peiros (פֵּרוֹת) and keren (קֶרֶן) familiar from the Gemara (גְּמָרָא) in Kiddushin (קִדּוּשִׁין), but the mechanism of a loan repayable in this world. This distinction is what makes the next step in the argument coherent rather than merely poetic.

The Leket Yosher’s Chiddush

The Leket Yosher (לֶקֶט יֹשֶׁר)[12], composed by Rav Yosef (Yoyzpa) Ostreicher, records the minhagim (מִנְהָגִים) and chiddushim (חִדּוּשִׁים) of his teacher, the Terumas HaDeshen (תְּרוּמַת הַדֶּשֶׁן). He builds a chiddush (חִדּוּשׁ) on the following halakhic foundation.

The Rambam (רַמְבַּ״ם)[13] rules that in a din Torah (דִּין תּוֹרָה) between a malveh (מַלְוֶה) and a loveh (לוֹוֶה), the malveh may compel the venue. If the lender insists that the case be heard at the Beis Din HaGadol (בֵּית דִּין הַגָּדוֹל) in Yerushalayim (יְרוּשָׁלַיִם), the borrower must travel there, however many days the journey requires. Even the borrower’s strongest procedural objection — his right, under ordinary circumstances, to demand a local beis din (בֵּית דִּין) or zabla (זַבְלָ״א) — yields to the lender’s prerogative. The premise standing behind this halachah (הֲלָכָה) is the verse in Mishlei: eved loveh l’ish malveh (עֶבֶד לֹוֶה לְאִישׁ מַלְוֶה)[14] — the borrower is, in a certain sense, a servant to the lender.

The Leket Yosher’s application, transmitted in his teacher’s name, follows the chain step by step. If our tzedakah renders us, kviyochol, the malveh and HaKadosh Baruch Hu the loveh, then the halakhic standing of the lender is ours. We may — kviyochol (כִּבְיָכוֹל) — insist that the din Torah against us be conducted not at the Kisei HaDin (כִּסֵּא הַדִּין) but at the Beis Din HaGadol shel Rachamim (בֵּית דִּין הַגָּדוֹל שֶׁל רַחֲמִים). By the koach of tzedakah we may relocate the entire courtroom.

The formulation is bold, and one would not construct it on one’s own. It emerges from a Rishon (רִאשׁוֹן) in his own teacher’s name, from the Rambam’s own halakhic architecture applied through the verse’s own metaphor. Its koach is not unconditional — the halakhic prerogative of the malveh operates within limits, and a malveh who is himself the defendant on other counts does not therefore acquire universal immunity — but the principle it expresses is entirely real. This is what tzedakah can do on Yom HaDin.

VI. Rachmana D’Anei Le’Aniyei Aneinan

The closing refrain of Slichos, borne on the melody familiar to every Jew who has ever risen at that hour, is:

רַחֲמָנָא דְעָנֵי לַעֲנִיֵּי, עֲנֵינָא. רַחֲמָנָא דְעָנֵי לִתְבִירֵי לִבָּא, עֲנֵינָא.
O Merciful One who answers the poor, answer us. O Merciful One who answers the broken-hearted, answer us.

An obvious difficulty presses upon the mefarshim (מְפָרְשִׁים). The refrain works as a plea if a person is genuinely poor, genuinely broken-hearted. What standing does a Jew have to invoke a formula predicated on a status he does not qualify for?

The answer draws on a category the Gemara (גְּמָרָא) in Megillah (מְגִלָּה)[15] opens in a different context: kafin v’ani v’lo yada (כָּפִין וְעָנִי וְלֹא יָדַע) — a person may be starving, may be impoverished, may be in dire need, and not even know it. The condition is paradoxical but real: a soul so dulled, so long habituated to lack, that it no longer registers the lack itself. That, in avodas Hashem (עֲבוֹדַת הַשֵּׁם), is precisely our state. We have lived so long in galus (גָּלוּת), in hester panim (הֶסְתֵּר פָּנִים), in the flat noise of olam ha’zeh (עוֹלָם הַזֶּה), that we are spiritually impoverished without feeling the impoverishment. The refrain Rachmana d’anei le’aniyei aneinan works precisely because we cannot feel it. What it asks is: Ribbono shel olam, You see what we cannot see. You know how poor we truly are, how broken-hearted we should be. Answer us as the impoverished and broken-hearted ones we in truth are.

VII. The Moment We Are In

The drashah (דְּרָשָׁה) closes upon its historical moment. Bachurim (בַּחוּרִים) in Eretz Yisrael (אֶרֶץ יִשְׂרָאֵל) were dancing that night in celebration of partial yeshuos (יְשׁוּעוֹת) — senior Hezbollah leadership had been eliminated in the days preceding. But the geulah (גְּאֻלָּה) was not complete. As Rabbi Ephrati spoke, his phone showed a text message: They are calling all the reservists. The Lebanon ground operation is starting tonight. Thousands of Jews who had been home with their families that Shabbos (שַׁבָּת) were kissing their wives and children goodbye and heading north. The matzav (מַצָּב) was kasheh me’od (קָשֶׁה מְאֹד).

“Had we known on Erev Simchas Torah Tav-Shin-Peh-Daled (עֶרֶב שִׂמְחַת תּוֹרָה תשפ״ד),” Rabbi Ephrati said, “what would happen the very next day, we would have davened differently. We would have said Slichos differently. In truth, every year we could say the same. But especially this year — on the far side of a yemei rachamim (יְמֵי רַחֲמִים) followed by such a klap (קְלַאפּ) for Klal Yisrael (כְּלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל), with the entire year that followed shaped by the war — let us set the record straight this time.”

VIII. Practical Kabbalos for the Four Days

A brief accounting of concrete kabbalos (קַבָּלוֹת) that translate the drashah (דְּרָשָׁה) into action across the four days that remain before Rosh HaShanah (רֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה).

Emes — Torah

  • Add one fixed seder (סֵדֶר) of Torah (תּוֹרָה) daily through Aseres Yemei Teshuvah (עֲשֶׂרֶת יְמֵי תְּשׁוּבָה).
  • Learn one piece of mussar (מוּסָר) each night before Slichos (סְלִיחוֹת) — Rambam Hilchos Teshuvah (רַמְבַּ״ם הִלְכוֹת תְּשׁוּבָה), Mesilas Yesharim (מְסִלַּת יְשָׁרִים), Sha’arei Teshuvah (שַׁעֲרֵי תְּשׁוּבָה).
  • Take a single halachah (הֲלָכָה) and resolve to keep it with new precision. V’shamata b’kolo (וְשָׁמַעְתָּ בְּקֹלוֹ) begins with one specific act of listening.

Chesed — Tzedakah and Rachmanus

  • Commit to a measurable daily tzedakah (צְדָקָה) amount; increase it on Erev Rosh HaShanah (עֶרֶב רֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה).
  • Chesed b’guf (חֶסֶד בְּגוּף): call someone lonely; offer real time to a discouraged chaver (חָבֵר); forgive a slight that has been festering.
  • Give to a communal fund — for hostage families, for soldiers, for widows and orphans of the war.

Tefillah and the Yud-Gimmel Middos

  • Recite Slichos with kavanah (כַּוָּנָה), b’tzibbur (בְּצִבּוּר), focusing on each middah (מִדָּה) in turn.
  • Match one middah each day with one concrete act that mirrors it — erech apayim (אֶרֶךְ אַפַּיִם) with a moment of patience, rav chesed (רַב חֶסֶד) with an unexpected kindness.

Achdus — B’Agudah Achas

  • Make peace with one person before Rosh HaShanah, if only by a single phone call.
  • Daven (דַּאוֶן) for another Jew’s specific need by name, daily.

IX. Conclusion

Slichos (סְלִיחוֹת) is not a password but a path. Chesed v’emes yikadmu fanecha (חֶסֶד וֶאֱמֶת יְקַדְּמוּ פָנֶיךָ) means: arrive at Yom HaDin (יוֹם הַדִּין) holding both Torah (תּוֹרָה) and acts of kindness, embodying — not merely reciting — the thirteen middos (מִדּוֹת). Then va’ani mochel lahem (וַאֲנִי מוֹחֵל לָהֶם) becomes the reality, both for the individual Jew and for Klal Yisrael (כְּלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל) as one bound bundle.

Not the horse, pulled across the border in unawareness. Not merely the balagulah (בַּעַל עֲגָלָה), prepared but half-invested. But the merchant — the Jew whose thirty days of preparation, compressed in a difficult year into four, are lived in the trembling awareness of what the crossing means, with chesed (חֶסֶד) and emes (אֱמֶת) in hand as he approaches the border. May we be zoche (זוֹכֶה) that HaKadosh Baruch Hu (הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא) move from Kisei HaDin (כִּסֵּא הַדִּין) to Kisei HaRachamim (כִּסֵּא הָרַחֲמִים); that He grant ksivah v’chasimah tovah (כְּתִיבָה וַחֲתִימָה טוֹבָה) to all of Klal Yisrael; that the families holding their breath in Eretz Yisrael (אֶרֶץ יִשְׂרָאֵל) see their sons and husbands return home safely; and that the geulah sheleimah (גְּאֻלָּה שְׁלֵמָה) arrive b’meheirah b’yameinu, amen (בִּמְהֵרָה בְּיָמֵינוּ אָמֵן).

  1. The Brisker Rav (R’ Yitzchak Ze’ev Soloveitchik), drashos on Motzaei Shabbos before the first Slichos; preserved in the collected Brisker drashos tradition (oral mussar transmission). — The three-tier mashal of the merchant, the balagulah, and the horse — the framework of preparation for Yom HaDin. Preserved in Brisker drashos collections and in the oral mussar tradition of Yerushalayim. The mashal’s severe reading of the third tier — that awareness itself is the difference between a Jew’s standing at the border and an animal’s — is the Brisker Rav’s own chiddush; the Rambam’s treatment of the shogeg and the tinok she’nishba (Hilchos Mamrim 3:3) preserves a distinct halakhic register in which awareness meaningfully modulates culpability. ↩
  2. Rosh HaShanah י״ז ע״ב (17b). — נִתְעַטֵּף הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא כִּשְׁלִיחַ צִבּוּר: HaKadosh Baruch Hu wrapped Himself as a shliach tzibbur and showed Moshe the order of prayer. “Whenever Yisrael sin, let them perform before Me this very order, and I will forgive them.” The textual basis for the shliach tzibbur’s tallis at Slichos, and for the operative force of the thirteen middos ha’rachamim. On the tallis-at-night question, see Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chaim 18:1–2 and the discussions in the Mishnah Berurah ad loc. ↩
  3. Shemos 34:6 — Vaya’avor Hashem al panav vayikra; the thirteen middos ha’rachamim. — The verse from which the thirteen middos are drawn: Hashem, Hashem, Keil rachum v’chanun, erech apayim, v’rav chesed v’emes… ↩
  4. Sotah י״ד ע״א (14a), on acharei Hashem Elokeichem teileichu; parallel in Sifrei, Eikev; Rambam, Hilchos De’os 1:6. — מָה הוּא רַחוּם — אַף אַתָּה רַחוּם: the classical formulation of imitatio Dei. The Rambam codifies the principle in Hilchos De’os as the framework for middos generally. It is upon this principle that ya’asu lefanai ka’seder ha’zeh is read behaviorally — the thirteen middos are enacted, not merely recited. ↩
  5. Tanna D’Bei Eliyahu Rabbah (the agudah achas teaching; siman varies by edition). — David HaMelech’s question and HaKadosh Baruch Hu’s response: in an age without Beis HaMikdash, when Klal Yisrael is sunk in galus, “stand before Me b’agudah achas” and recite the thirteen middos as a bundled tzibbur. The logic is substitutional: the korban tzibbur was the vessel by which the many became one in the Mikdash; the bundled community is that vessel in the age of exile. The traditional attribution of Tanna D’Bei Eliyahu to teachings Eliyahu HaNavi transmitted to Rav Anan (Kesuvos 106a) is the frame within which classical commentators cite the work; the precise redactional history has been treated at length in the scholarly literature. Precise location of the agudah achas passage varies by edition; the standard chapter numbering places it in the Rabbah, with parallels in the Zuta. ↩
  6. Tehillim 89:15. — צֶדֶק וּמִשְׁפָּט מְכוֹן כִּסְאֶךָ; חֶסֶד וֶאֱמֶת יְקַדְּמוּ פָנֶיךָ. The opening pasuk of Slichos in many kehillos: justice as the foundation of the throne; chesed and emes as what Klal Yisrael brings before that throne. ↩
  7. Chasam Sofer, drashos on Aseres Yemei Teshuvah. — The gematria: chesed (חסד) = 8 + 60 + 4 = 72; emes (אמת) = 1 + 40 + 400 = 441; together 513. Slichos (סליחות) = 60 + 30 + 10 + 8 + 6 + 400 = 514. The difference of one is resolved by the standard kollel of one for the word itself. Preserved in the Chasam Sofer’s collected drashos; the precise printed location varies by edition. ↩
  8. Mishlei 23:23 — Emes k’neh v’al timkor. — Chazal identify the emes one acquires and never sells as Torah itself. Real teshuvah begins with returning to that truth — Torah Shebiksav and Torah She’baal Peh. ↩
  9. Devarim 30:1–10 — parshas teshuvah. — The parshah of teshuvah read on the Shabbos preceding this Slichos. Its opening step is v’shamata b’kolo — hearing His voice. The Torah’s own architecture of teshuvah: shemiah first, corrected behavior second, return to Eretz Yisrael last. ↩
  10. Rambam, Hilchos Teshuvah, chs. 2–7; Mesilas Yesharim; Sha’arei Teshuvah. — The classical mussar framework for the additional Aseres Yemei Teshuvah learning. The Rambam’s chapters on teshuvah map the inner work; Mesilas Yesharim and Sha’arei Teshuvah structure the practical climb. The earlier generation’s Slichos-time discipline of “more learning” tracked these sefarim. ↩
  11. Mishlei 19:17 — Malveh Hashem chonein dal; Alter Rebbe, Igros Kodesh at the back of Sefer HaTanya. — The verse’s metaphor: one who shows favor to the poor lends to Hashem. The Alter Rebbe’s iggeres: were Jews to understand the koach of tzedakah, they could not tear themselves from giving. The exception to sechar mitzvah b’hai alma leikah operates here through the specific mechanism of a loan repayable in this world — distinct from the Gemara’s discussion of peiros and keren in Kiddushin 39b, which addresses reward for mitzvos generally. ↩
  12. Leket Yosher, by R’ Yosef (Yoyzpa) Ostreicher — recording the minhagim and chiddushim of the Terumas HaDeshen (R’ Yisrael Isserlein). — The Terumas HaDeshen fled the Rhineland during a period of redifus and settled in Wiener Neustadt (Austria), where he opened a yeshivah that became a central Ashkenazic institution of the fifteenth century. His talmid R’ Yosef Ostreicher preserved his minhagim and chiddushim in Leket Yosher, printed in two volumes (Yoreh De’ah and Orach Chaim, ed. Freimann). The chiddush on the malveh/loveh venue rule transposed to our din Torah with HaKadosh Baruch Hu appears in this collection; the precise volume and siman should be verified against a printed edition, as the chiddush is the intellectual centerpiece of the drashah and warrants the tightest pinning. ↩
  13. Rambam, Hilchos Sanhedrin ch. 6 (the malveh’s prerogative on venue); parallel in Hilchos Malveh v’Loveh. — In a din Torah between malveh and loveh, the malveh can compel the venue: if he insists on the Beis Din HaGadol, the loveh must travel there. Even the borrower’s ordinary right to a local beis din or to zabla yields to the lender’s prerogative. This is the structural premise the Leket Yosher applies to our tzedakah-relationship with HaKadosh Baruch Hu. The chapter and precise halachah number vary by edition; the parallel in Hilchos Malveh v’Loveh is worth consulting alongside. ↩
  14. Mishlei 22:7 — Eved loveh l’ish malveh. — “The borrower is a servant to the lender.” The textual anchor for the malveh’s elevated standing in the halakhic din Torah framework, and, in the Leket Yosher’s transposition, our standing before HaKadosh Baruch Hu by the koach of tzedakah. ↩
  15. Megillah 7b (the sugya of the ani who is unaware of his impoverishment); cf. related discussions in Bava Metzia on the categories of poverty. — כָּפִין וְעָנִי וְלֹא יָדַע: the paradoxical category of one so impoverished, so long habituated to lack, that he no longer registers the lack. Applied in the drashah to our state in galus: spiritually starving without realizing it. The closing refrain Rachmana d’anei le’aniyei aneinan works precisely because HaKadosh Baruch Hu sees the impoverishment we ourselves cannot feel. ↩
