The Light That Never Dims: Yosef HaTzadik, the Menorah in the Tunnel, and the Kiel Photograph
A guest discourse for the eve of Chanukah (חנוכה)
24 Kislev 5786 · 14 December 2025 · Sefer Edition

This short discourse, delivered as a guest at Young Israel of Woodmere on the eve of Chanukah (חנוכה), is not part of the lecturer’s usual series on Tefillah (תפילה). It takes up a single question, what success means in the language of the Torah, and answers it from the story of Yosef HaTzadik (יוסף הצדיק) in Parashas Vayigash (פרשת ויגש).

It then turns to the footage, published that week, of six hostages lighting the menorah (מנורה) in a tunnel in Gaza, and to the photograph of a menorah (מנורה) in Kiel in 1932 that one of them recalled; and it closes with what the lecturer would have us carry from both into our own homes.

✦   ✦   ✦

I. A Story of Tears

The Survivor

The lecturer opened with an encounter that has stayed with him. He sat once with an eighty-five-year-old Holocaust survivor, a group of them together in a shul, and told a story of mesiras nefesh (מסירות נפש) in the Shoah (שואה): of Jews who went up in flames על קידוש השם. He was moved to tears. The survivor took him by the hand, called him by a Yiddish diminutive of his name, and asked in Yiddish why he was crying. The lecturer answered that he had never heard such a story, and that it moved him.

The survivor answered: “I do not cry anymore.” As a child, in those years (the lecturer does not remember whether the survivor said he was ten or eight, or whether the year was 1939, 1943 or 1944), an SS officer once asked him a question. He did not speak German; he answered in Yiddish, and not as expected, and the officer slapped him. “I looked at that officer, that רשע ימח שמו, and I realized that I did not shed a tear. I could not cry.”

Then he spoke of Hallel (הלל), which he says every time he recites Hallel (הלל): כי חלצת נפשי ממות את עיני מן דמעה את רגלי מדחי[1] “I think of myself. You saved me; You pulled me out of that מות, out of the valley of death. But You also took away my ability to cry: את עיני מן דמעה. I cannot cry. I have seen so much in my life.”

Then he took the lecturer’s hand again and said, in Yiddish, אבער דו דארפסט וויינען: “You should cry.” They wept together. The lecturer felt very close to him, and says the encounter gave him a whole new understanding of what kind of people we are speaking about.

A Guest’s Apology

The lecturer then thanked those who had invited him, and apologized: he would be brief, and some in the room had heard the same message from him in his own shul that morning. He gave it again, he said, because it puts Chanukah (חנוכה) in perspective: not Chanukah (חנוכה) in the days of old, but how our homes should look now. (He asked, with a smile, why a listener who had heard it that morning was looking at him: “Look down at your feet.”)

II. The Measure of Success: Yosef HaTzadik

A Title No One Else Receives

The Torah, says the lecturer, never calls Avraham Avinu (אברהם אבינו), Yitzchak (יצחק), Yaakov (יעקב), Moshe (משה), Aharon (אהרן) or David (דוד) an ish matzliach (איש מצליח). Only Yosef (יוסף) receives the title[2]. Everyone wants success: in raising children, in the family, in a career, in oneself. Would it not be worthwhile to see how the Tanakh itself uses the word? Three times the Torah applies matzliach (מצליח) to Yosef (יוסף), and each time it says that God was with him.

Three Phases

The lecturer divides the life of Yosef (יוסף) into three phases and asks in which of them the Torah uses the word.

  1. The years of light. His youth, in the light of his father: learning with Yaakov (יעקב), the best rebbe (רבי) of all time, Torah and the secrets of the universe, in the kesones passim (כתונת פסים), beloved. A seventeen-year-old learning in the best yeshivah (ישיבה) in the world: surely this is success. Yet the Torah does not use the word of this phase.
  2. The years of power. From age thirty until his death at one hundred and ten, he is the viceroy of Egypt: he foretells the famine, he sustains the entire world, and he is the most powerful man after Paroh (פרעה) in the superpower of antiquity. Surely this is success. Yet here too the Torah does not use the word.
  3. The years of darkness. Between seventeen and thirty: the pit, the hatred of his brothers, the sale, the house of Potifar (פוטיפר), the false accusation of Eishes Potifar (אשת פוטיפר) (the lecturer calls it a blood libel), the dungeon. Throughout this stretch the Torah says that he was successful. “If that is success,” says the lecturer, “I do not want any of it.” Why does the Torah use the word then, and precisely then?

The Secret: The Name on His Lips

Before the word מצליח the verses always say ויהי ה' אתו, that God was with him. Rashi (רש״י)[3] explains the phrase by quoting a midrash (מדרש), but briefly, only one line of it; the midrash (מדרש) itself is longer. According to the midrash (מדרש), Yosef (יוסף) used to come in to serve his master, doing a slave’s work (the lecturer lists cleaning and feeding the horses and sheep), and as he came in he was whispering something. פוטיפר saw it. Egypt, the lecturer notes, is the land of sorcery and black magic, and פוטיפר grew afraid: a Jew comes in muttering, and who knows what may happen? He asked: “What are you saying? Are you doing kishuf (כשוף) on me?”

Yosef (יוסף) answered: no, שם שמים שגור על פי; and what he was saying was: “Thank You, Hashem, for putting me here. Please, God, help me find favor in my master’s eyes. Thank You for the past, thank You for the present, and I pray for the future.”

From this, the lecturer says, we have the Jewish habit of saying im yirtzeh Hashem (אם ירצה ה׳) and be’ezras Hashem (בעזרת ה׳) all day long, and “thank You, Hashem” in nearly every other sentence. Where did we get it? From Yosef HaTzadik (יוסף הצדיק), who, in the worst situation a person can be in, said: thank You, Hashem; I love You; please help me do this and that; help me find my mission in this hole. That is יוסף הצדיק and that is success.

A Lesson for Our ChildrenThe lecturer would have this told to children: one who has shem shamayim (שם שמים), who says im yirtzeh Hashem (אם ירצה ה׳), who is aware that God is with him whatever happens, and that he is here for a purpose and a mission, has success. That is why the Torah says מצליח of יוסף in precisely that chapter of his life: it is authentic, genuine Jewish success.

III. A Gift From Heaven: The Menorah in the Tunnel

The Footage

The lecturer found it hard to use the words “gift from heaven,” but said that for living, breathing Jews it is a gift that, in the week just ended, the footage was made public of six hostages reciting the blessings and lighting the menorah (מנורה) in a tunnel complex in Gaza.[4] The lecturer added a prayer for vengeance for their blood and called them “the beautiful six.” Every hostage’s story is meaningful, he said, but these six lit the menorah (מנורה) before Chanukah (חנוכה), and people are watching.

The six, he said, had been secular until then, and “God help me, I am saying the truth”: they are more frum (פרומער), more ehrlich (ערלעכער) and more Jewish than all of us put together. That they light a menorah (מנורה) even in a tunnel moves us, but he sensed something deeper. It is a mitzvah (מצוה) for every Jew to watch the clip, he said; he told his congregation that whoever had not yet watched it should do so, and let the children watch. It will enhance one’s Chanukah (חנוכה): when one lights, one will feel part of their lighting and of their fulfillment of the will of the Holy One, blessed be He.

They make the blessings, he said (the lecturer described the mitzvah as publicizing the miracle, pirsumei nisa (פרסומי ניסא)). They look very frail and thin. It was two Chanukahs (חנוכות) ago, as he understood, eighty days into their captivity. They were later shot at close range, all six, in about August 2024, eight to ten months after October 7. In the clip they smile and sing Maoz Tzur (מעוז צור) and Haneiros Halalu (הנרות הללו).

The Remark at the End

At the very end of the clip, the lecturer said, a remark is heard, which he believes is by Hersh Goldberg (Goldberg-Polin). He had listened again that night to be sure. In Hebrew the speaker says: אתם יודעים, אתם מכירים את התמונה הזאת עם הדגל הנאצי והמנורה הנגדית? שמע ישראל[5] That is the picture of a menorah (מנורה) in a window with a Nazi flag opposite it.

IV. The Story Behind the Picture: Kiel

The lecturer told the story behind the picture, and said it is remarkable that the hostage brought it up, because it connects everything. In 1932 there was a rabbi in Kiel, Germany: Rabbi Dr. Akiva Baruch Posner, a doctor of philosophy of a well-known German university and the rabbi (the lecturer says “chief rabbi”) of the city. The Nazis, whom the lecturer cursed, came to power and established their headquarters in Kiel directly across the street from his home.[6]

It was Zos Chanukah (זאת חנוכה), the last night of Chanukah (חנוכה), a Friday night, as the lecturer tells it. As the rabbi was about to light, his wife, Rebbetzin Rachel Posner, took up a camera (cameras were not common then) to photograph her husband lighting. From where she stood, behind him, the picture shows the menorah (מנורה) in the open window and, across the street, the large dark Nazi flag.

On the back, as people did in those days, she wrote the date, who was in the picture, and a sentence. The date, the lecturer thinks, was Chanukah (חנוכה) 1932 (by 1933 the rabbi had already left for Eretz Yisrael (ארץ ישראל) and survived; he saw the writing on the wall). The sentence: “Death to Judah,” so says the flag; “Judah will live forever,” so answers the light.

“Death to Judah”: that, says the lecturer, was Nazism, the declaration that the eternal flame of the Jews would be eternal no more. But the light of the menorah (מנורה), the flickering flames, shows the vibrancy of Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל), Am Yisrael chai (עם ישראל חי), Netzach Yisrael lo yeshaker (נצח ישראל לא ישקר): we are here forever, no matter what.

V. Our Connection Today

“We do not need stories from the Holocaust anymore, rabbosai,” said the lecturer, “we do not need those old legends.” (He allowed that the lighting of the menorah (מנורה) in Auschwitz, mehadrin min hamehadrin (מהדרין מן המהדרין), is something we do need.) “But we have something much closer to our hearts,” for every Jewish heart was invested in the plight of these hostages, who did not come out. By lighting the menorah (מנורה), and “I do not know which Hamas terrorist took that video, but it is a great favor for us,” they have joined us to a chain: when we light the menorah (מנורה) tomorrow night, on every night of this Chanukah (חנוכה), make the blessing להדליק נר של חנוכה, and sing Maoz Tzur (מעוז צור) and Haneiros Halalu (הנרות הללו) with our children and family around the flickering flames, “we will feel that connection, from generation to generation.” Whether the enemy is Nazi Germany or Hamas in Gaza, whoever tries to extinguish the flame, our light will never go out.

VI. How? The Secret

“Because we are a nation that is successful,” said the lecturer. And what is success? That wherever I am, God put me here and God is with me. “אם ירצה השם. Thank You, Hashem”: Yosef HaTzadik (יוסף הצדיק) in the dungeon, in the pit, always knowing and feeling that God is with me and that He has me on a mission.

Someone asked him, after he gave this message in his shul that morning: why, then, did they not get out? Why were they shot? His answer: it is a story of our national mission. When one zooms in on the individual, who can understand, and who are we to understand God’s ways? But one thing we do know: the light of our nation will never be extinguished.

To bequeath this to the next generation: success, in Jewish terminology, means im yirtzeh Hashem (אם ירצה השם) and be’ezras Hashem (בעזרת השם) on one’s lips, in a tunnel, in a pit, lighting the menorah (מנורה), reciting the blessings, and knowing that God is with you. The lecturer closed with a prayer that God be with us and that we hear of no more sorrow and see only joyous occasions, thanked the congregation of Young Israel of Woodmere for having him, and wished them a פרייליכע חנוכה.

  1. תהלים קט״ז:ח׳. Confirmed on Sefaria (English): “You have delivered me from death, my eyes from tears, my feet from stumbling.” The verse is part of Hallel (הלל). The wording is as the recording has it; the AI summary instead put in the mouth of the survivor המלאך הגאל אתי מכל רע, a phrase from בראשית מ״ח:ט״ז, a verse that is not in the recording and not in הלל. The survivor’s reading of את עיני מן דמעה as the cost of survival is his, as the lecturer relates it. ↩
  2. בראשית ל״ט:ב׳–ג׳, כ״ג. Confirmed on Sefaria: 39:2, “God was with Joseph, and he was a successful man”; 39:3, God “lent success to everything he undertook”; 39:23, God made whatever he did succeed. Strictly, the phrase איש מצליח occurs only in 39:2; in 39:3 and 39:23 it is God who מצליח. The lecturer’s “three times” counts the three uses of the root. All three fall in the chapters of slavery and prison (Open Questions, no. 3). ↩
  3. רש״י על בראשית ל״ט:ג׳; מדרש תנחומא, וישב ח׳; cf. בראשית רבה פ״ו, ה׳. Confirmed on Sefaria: Rashi on the words כי ה׳ אתו says “the name of Heaven was familiar in his mouth,” citing תנחומא וישב ח׳. The Tanchuma (תנחומא) describes פוטיפר hearing יוסף whisper as he served and asking what he was saying; יוסף replied that he was praying for favor in his master’s eyes. Bereishis Rabbah (בראשית רבה) 86:5 has יוסף whispering on entering and leaving but does not have the wording about the Name. The suspicion of sorcery and the “abracadabra” are the lecturer’s telling. His remark that Rashi quotes only one line of a longer midrash (מדרש) is borne out. ↩
  4. The footage of the hostages in the tunnel. Per press reports, the footage was broadcast on 11 December 2025 (Channel 12’s Uvda, after the IDF obtained it from Gaza). It shows six hostages, Hersh Goldberg-Polin, Eden Yerushalmi, Ori Danino, Alex Lobanov, Carmel Gat and Almog Sarusi, lighting Chanukah candles (נרות חנוכה) in a tunnel in late 2023; reports say about eighty days into captivity, as the lecturer said. They were killed in a tunnel in Rafah on 29 August 2024. These facts are from press reports, not from Sefaria or from the recording (Open Questions, no. 4). ↩
  5. Hersh Goldberg-Polin’s remark. The recording gives the lecturer’s identification (“I think, by Hersh Goldberg”); press reports on the clip name Hersh Goldberg-Polin as the speaker who mentions the picture of the chanukiah (חנוכיה) with a Nazi flag. The Hebrew wording is as transcribed from the recording, the lecturer having listened to it again the night before. ↩
  6. The Posner photograph, Kiel. Rabbi Dr. Akiva Posner, a Ph.D. of Halle-Wittenberg, was the last rabbi of the Kiel community; the family left Germany in 1933 and reached Eretz Yisrael in 1934. The photograph was taken by his wife, Rachel Posner; the inscription on the back, in translation, is as the lecturer gave it. The photograph is loaned to Yad Vashem by the Posner family estate. Yad Vashem’s page dates the photograph to Chanukah 1931 with the inscription dated 1932; other reports give 1932; the lecturer said 1932. The sources consulted do not say which night it was, and describe the building opposite as hung with Nazi flags but do not confirm that it was the Nazi headquarters (Open Questions, no. 5). The earlier text’s German wording, “5694,” and the rabbi’s dates were unsupported and are omitted. ↩

Editorial Notes  ·  Open Questions

Items unresolved at the time of writing, to be settled with the lecturer or from the sources before printing. Reports of the lecturer are recorded as his and are not asserted in the text.

  1. The survivor’s story. The setting (the lecturer says a group in a shul), the survivor’s age at the time (ten or eight) and the year (1939, 1943 or 1944) are as the lecturer gave them, hesitantly. In the recording the men who “went up in flames” are described with a word that reads as Tisha B’Av, which does not fit; it is not used here. The sentence about “the Axelrod space” in the recording is unclear and is omitted.
  2. The mitzvah of publicizing the miracle. The AI summary has the lecturer say the hostages fulfilled pirsumei nisa (פרסומי ניסא); the raw transcript has an unclear phrase at that point (“the mitzvah of rochus”) and the bilingual transcript does not have it. Retained in the text, hedged; to be confirmed with the lecturer.
  3. “Three times.” Whether the lecturer meant the three uses of מצליח (as rendered) or, as he said, three times איש מצליח; only 39:2 has the latter phrase (note 2).
  4. Eighty days. The lecturer, and press reports, say about eighty days into captivity. By calendar, Chanukah 2023 began on the evening of 7 December, about sixty-two days after October 7. To be confirmed.
  5. The Kiel photograph. The year (1931 or 1932), the night of Chanukah (חנוכה), whether it was a Friday night, and whether the building opposite was the Nazi headquarters, to be confirmed (note 6).
  6. “Tomorrow night.” The lecturer says that the menorah (מנורה) would be lit “tomorrow night.” The first night of Chanukah 5786 was the evening of 14 December 2025 itself. The date of the recording, or the lecturer’s wording, to be confirmed.
  7. The Hebrew date. Computed as 24 Kislev 5786 for 14 December 2025 (the previous discourse, 7 December, was 17 Kislev). The recording gives no Hebrew date.
  8. The last line. The recording has the lecturer closing with an unclear phrase about “reciting השם מלך”; omitted.
  9. Names of those present. The lecturer thanks Rabbi Axelrod, mentions Rabbi Hackenbroch and quotes “Rabbi Schorr” of Boro Park; the spellings and roles are unconfirmed and the names are omitted.
