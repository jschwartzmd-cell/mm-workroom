קְדֻשָּׁה — הַמִּצְוָה הַיּוֹמִית שֶׁל קִדּוּשׁ הַשֵּׁם

Kedushah — The Daily Mitzvah of “V’Nikdashti B’Soch Bnei Yisrael”
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Sunday Morning Series on Tefillah
Recorded ראש חודש ניסן תשפ"ה · 30 March 2025  ·  Sefer Edition

Sponsored לעילוי נשמת אלטר מרדכי בן שמואל ז"ל; ולרפואה שלימה פנחס בן [שמו].

Kedushah (קדושה) as a daily mitzvah de’oraisa (מצוה דאורייתא) grounded in “v’nikdashti b’soch Bnei Yisrael” (ונקדשתי בתוך בני ישראל); its rank above tefillah (תפילה) itself; the architecture of the nusach (נוסח) with the insertion of David HaMelech’s pasuk; the Rosh/Arizal dispute over “l’umasam meshabchim v’omrim”; the practices of jumping, trembling, and gazing upward; the mystical romance depicted in Pirkei Heichalos; the biography of Rabbeinu Kalonymus HaZakein from the Crusades through the burial in the mikveh; and the letter of Rav Pirkoi ben Baboi, recovered only in the Cairo Geniza, that reframes the ancient Bnei Eretz Yisrael practice of Shabbos-only Kedushah as a relic of persecution rather than a minhag to be preserved.

I. The Whole Month of Nissan as Yom Tov

Rosh Chodesh Nissan and its Extension

Rosh Chodesh Nissan (ראש חודש ניסן) is not merely one day of festive weight but the doorway into a full month bearing the sanctity of the day. The Shefa Kodesh (שפע קודש)[27] develops the doctrine that the entire month of Nissan (ניסן) — anchored by Pesach (פסח) and the redemption from Mitzrayim (מצרים) — carries the kedushah of Rosh Chodesh throughout, and this teaching stands behind the well-established practice of omitting tachanun (תחנון) the entire month and of maintaining a broadly festive demeanor. The foundation lies in the Gemara in Shevuos,[2] which derives that Rosh Chodesh is included in the Torah’s designation “mo’ed” (מועד): “Rosh Chodesh ikri mo’ed” (ראש חודש איקרי מועד) — the day is subject to the framework of Yom Tov in defined respects, and this classification is what the Shefa Kodesh extends across the whole of the month.

Gehinnom Closed for the Month

The Mordechai (מרדכי) on Maseches Pesachim[1] teaches that just as Gehinnom (גהינום) is closed on Shabbos, so too it is closed on Rosh Chodesh. This ruling has a concrete halachic footprint. At Minchah of Shabbos the tzibbur recites “Tzidkascha Tzedek” (צדקתך צדק) as a plea that the din of the neshamos waiting to re-enter Gehinnom be delayed; on a Shabbos that falls on Erev Rosh Chodesh, however, “Tzidkascha Tzedek” is omitted — the neshamos require no extension, since Gehinnom remains closed on Rosh Chodesh as well. The Mordechai’s extension is that this closure persists throughout the entire month of Nissan, and this extension grounds the broader festive treatment of the month. From the Mordechai’s own life a further weight adheres to the ruling: Rabbeinu Mordechai ben Hillel Ashkenazi, talmid of the Maharam MiRotenburg, was himself killed al Kiddush Hashem in the Rindfleisch massacres of 1298.

The Gra’s Shabbos Hat on Rosh Chodesh

A related minhag preserved in the Ma’aseh Rav: the Gra (גר"א) wore his Shabbos hat — a spodik (ספודיק) in the levush of his time and place — on Rosh Chodesh, but did not change into the remainder of his bigdei Shabbos.[28] The reasoning preserved by his talmidim: Rosh Chodesh is Yom Tov in kedushah but muttar bi’melachah (מותר במלאכה). The neshamah (נשמה) experiences the full Yom Tov; the body does not, since ordinary melachah proceeds. Kedushah is l’maalah (למעלה) — in the head, the seat of the neshamah — and so the head wears the Shabbos hat while the rest of the body does not. The Shulchan Aruch in Orach Chaim siman תי"ט records the broader minhag to wear at least some bigdei Shabbos on Rosh Chodesh, and it is a ma’alah to accustom children to this practice from a young age.

II. The Purpose of Chazaras HaShatz

The received explanation for chazaras ha’shatz (חזרת הש"ץ) in every tefillah other than Ma’ariv is well known: in earlier generations, not every member of the tzibbur was able to daven the Shemoneh Esrei on his own. There were no siddurim, many could not read, and the chazarah made it possible for the am ha’aretz (עם הארץ) to be yotzei by answering amen to each of the brachos. The category of am ha’aretz in the classical sense no longer describes the contemporary kehillah — nor, one must acknowledge, does the classical din of talmid chacham apply with its historic force. The current mispallel occupies a middle ground, and the historic function of the chazarah now depends less on the literacy gap than on the deeper reason.

Several Acharonim identify that deeper reason as something else entirely: the chazarah exists so that the tzibbur can say Kedushah. The whole institution of chazaras ha’shatz — twice every weekday, three times on Shabbos — is built around that single moment of the tzibbur’s Kedushah. This alone testifies to the centrality of the Kedushah in the halachic and hashkafic architecture of the day.

III. The Halachic Weight of Kedushah

The Din of a Late Arrival

The Gemara in Berachos[3] rules — and the Shulchan Aruch in Orach Chaim siman kuf-tes[4] codifies — that one who arrives at shul as the tzibbur is approaching Kedushah may not begin his private Shemoneh Esrei until after the chazan has said Kedushah, in order to answer “Kadosh, kadosh, kadosh” (קדוש קדוש קדוש) with the tzibbur. Even the individual’s tefillah b’tzibbur defers to the Kedushah — a striking measure of its rank within the structure of communal tefillah.

The Aruch HaShulchan’s Tension

Precisely for that reason the Aruch HaShulchan[5] raises a structural tension. If Kedushah is so central that Chazal require the late arrival to delay his private Amidah in order to catch it, what right does an ordinary mispallel — who is already davening with the tzibbur — have to draw out his own Shemoneh Esrei at such length that the chazan reaches Kedushah while the mispallel is still in his silent Amidah? The tzibbur finishes in five or six minutes; the mispallel who lingers falls behind and forfeits participation in the tzibbur’s Kedushah.

Le’halachah, the mispallel who finds himself mid-brachah when the chazan reaches Kedushah may pause silently and have in mind what the chazan says — this listening posture does not constitute hefsek. But the Aruch HaShulchan does not offer this technical mechanism as his resolution. His answer is that Chazal did not mechayev the mispallel to truncate his Shemoneh Esrei. Where the mispallel is davening at length with genuine kavvanah — “halevai!” — the tension is real but not dispositive against extended kavvanah. The mispallel who is truly present in every brachah is not required to sacrifice his Amidah for the Kedushah, though every effort should be made to align the timing.

IV. Klal Yisrael’s Shirah Above the Malachim’s

The Gemara in Chullin tzadi-alef amud bais[6] teaches a doctrine of remarkable force: the shirah (שירה) of Klal Yisrael stands at a higher madreigah than the shirah of the malachei ha’shareis (מלאכי השרת). The sugya substantiates this from two directions: the frequency of the Kedushah and the intimacy of the pronunciation of the Shem.

How often, the Gemara asks, do the malachim recite “Kadosh, kadosh, kadosh”? Several shitos are brought: once a day, once a week, once a month, once a year, once every shemittah (שמיטה), once every yovel (יובל) — and, according to one opinion, only once in their entire existence. Klal Yisrael, by contrast, recites Kedushah twice every single day at Shacharis and Minchah, without even counting the Kedushah of Yotzeir Or (יוצר אור). And in the interval before the pronunciation of the Shem: when Klal Yisrael says “Shema Yisrael”, two words later the Shem is already pronounced. The malachim require three words — “Kadosh, kadosh, kadosh” — before “Hashem Tzeva’os”. Klal Yisrael stands closer to the Ribbono Shel Olam than the malachim do. The Gemara designates the Kedushah itself as shirah: just as the malachim sing shirah, so does Klal Yisrael.

V. The Architecture of the Kedushah

The Three Pesukim

The Kedushah opens with the invitation: “Nakdish es Shimcha ba’olam, k’shem she’makdishim oso bi’shmei marom” (נקדיש את שמך בעולם, כשם שמקדישים אותו בשמי מרום) — we will sanctify Him below as He is sanctified above. The body of the Kedushah then consists of three pesukim:

  1. “Kadosh, kadosh, kadosh Hashem Tzeva’os” — from Yeshayahu ו'; the shirah of the malachim.
  2. “Baruch kevod Hashem mi’mekomo” (ברוך כבוד ה' ממקומו) — from Yechezkel ג'; the shirah of the malachim.
  3. “Yimloch Hashem l’olam, Elokayich Tziyon l’dor va’dor Halleluka” (ימלוך ה' לעולם, אלקיך ציון לדור ודור הללוי-ה) — from Tehillim קמ"ו:י'; not from the malachim.

The Avudraham’s Question

If the design of the Kedushah is to mirror the shiras ha’malachim, the third pasuk is a puzzle: why is a pasuk from Tehillim — not the shirah of the malachim — inserted between the two pesukim that are? The Avudraham[7] offers a resolution that ties the structure directly to the theme of Chullin tzadi-alef: the only figure in the whole of Jewish history who came near to a malach in his shirah was David HaMelech (דוד המלך). David HaMelech composed Sefer Tehillim, the enduring shirah of Klal Yisrael, and a pasuk of his is inserted to mark that Klal Yisrael, too, has a figure whose shirah stands beside the malachim’s. The Kedushah is not the shirah of the malachim alone but the joint shirah of the malachim and Klal Yisrael represented by their chazan, David HaMelech.

A secondary reason is offered as well. The pasuk mentions Tziyon (ציון), and there is a shitah in the Rishonim that every tefillah must mention Yerushalayim (ירושלים) and Tziyon — “im lo a’aleh es Yerushalayim al rosh simchasi” (אם לא אעלה את ירושלים על ראש שמחתי). The Kedushah of chazaras ha’shatz is one place where this requirement is satisfied; the Kedushah d’Yotzeir Or does not require it because the surrounding brachos already include it.

VI. Nusach HaKedushah — The Rosh and the Arizal

The Litvish Practice and its Halachic Consequence

The scope of what the kahal recites during the chazan’s Kedushah is the subject of a live machlokes. In Litvish batei knesios, the kahal recites only “Kadosh, kadosh, kadosh” and “Baruch kevod Hashem mi’mekomo”. They do not say the bridging phrase “L’umasam meshabchim v’omrim” (לעומתם משבחים ואומרים).

This is not merely a stylistic difference. It has a direct halachic consequence in the halachos of hefsek. The halachah permits interrupting Kri’as Shema (קריאת שמע) for Kedushah — but only for the essential pesukim, namely “Kadosh, kadosh, kadosh” and “Baruch kevod Hashem mi’mekomo”. One does not interrupt Kri’as Shema for “L’umasam...” The taxonomy is: within Pesukei D’zimra (פסוקי דזמרה) one may recite the entire nusach; within Kri’as Shema proper, only the essentials are permitted.

The Rosh’s Reasoning

The Rosh[8] supplies the reasoning by analogy to Kaddish. When does anyone say “Yisgadal v’yiskadash Shmei Rabba” (יתגדל ויתקדש שמיה רבא) together with the chazan? Nobody. The chazan says “Yisgadal”; the kahal answers amen. The Kedushah, in the Rosh’s reading, follows the same model. The chazan says “Nakdishcha” (נקדישך) — that is his invitation to the kahal to sanctify; the kahal’s role is the response, not the invitation. According to the Rosh, the kahal should not even say “Nakdishcha” along with the chazan. The only words that belong to the kahal are the two response-pesukim. The Litvish minhag rests on this shitah.

The Arizal’s Ruling

The Arizal HaKadosh (אריז"ל הקדוש),[29] however, ruled — on kabbalah grounds — that the kahal must recite the entire nusach: “Nakdishcha v’na’aritzcha”, the bridging phrases “L’umasam meshabchim v’omrim”, and the third pasuk. The reasoning is that each phrase corresponds to a specific tikkun in the upper worlds, and the kahal’s participation is essential to the structural ascent. This is the ruling that stands behind the majority Ashkenazi practice today, in which the kahal says the full nusach.

The Magen Avraham’s Harmonizing Pesak

The Magen Avraham[9] supplies the harmonizing pesak. The phrases “L’umasam meshabchim v’omrim” are, in his reading, descriptive narrative — narrating that the second group of malachim, positioned opposite the first, also responds — and not part of the Kedushah’s essential pesukim. Where the minhag is to recite them, they should be said quietly, and only after the chazan — not with him. The Litvish practice omits them; the Ashkenazi practice includes them; the Magen Avraham’s qualification governs the manner in which they are said where they are said.

VII. Kedushah D’Yotzeir Or is Not the Same Kedushah

The Gemara in Megillah[10] establishes the category of davar sh’bi’kedushah (דבר שבקדושה) — items that may not be said without a minyan of ten — and includes Kedushah in that list. It follows immediately that Kedushah proper cannot be said alone.

What then is the status of the “Kadosh, kadosh, kadosh” embedded in the Kedushah d’Yotzeir Or? An individual davening alone at home recites Yotzeir Or with “u’v’divrei kodshecha kasuv leimor: Kadosh, kadosh, kadosh...” (ובדברי קדשך כתוב לאמר: קדוש קדוש קדוש). The Rishonim answer that this is not Kedushah. It is narrative — the mispallel is describing what the malachim say, not himself performing an act of Kedushah. The actual Kedushah takes place only in chazaras ha’shatz; that is where the mitzvah of “v’nikdashti b’soch Bnei Yisrael” is discharged.

The proof is sharp. If a goy held a gun to a Jew’s head on Shabbos and demanded that he recite “Kadosh, kadosh, kadosh” as a performance of public chillul Shabbos, the Jew would be forbidden to comply. But an individual reciting the narrative “Kadosh, kadosh, kadosh” of Yotzeir Or commits no violation whatever, since in that context the words are not an act of Kedushah. The categorical distinction is intrinsic: Kedushah as davar sh’bi’kedushah exists only where the tzibbur is present to constitute the framework.

VIII. Kedushah as a Daily Mitzvah De’Oraisa

The Pasuk and the Two Kiyumim

The pasuk “v’nikdashti b’soch Bnei Yisrael” (Vayikra כ"ב:ל"ב) is the source from which the Gemara derives the well-known din of Kiddush Hashem (קידוש השם) in the classical case: where a goy demands public idolatry under threat of death, one gives one’s life. Baruch Hashem, the gun-to-the-head case does not describe the daily reality of the contemporary Jew.

The Shaarei Kedushah’s Chiddush

The Shaarei Kedushah[23] of Rabbi Chaim Vital, foremost talmid of the Arizal, develops the doctrine that the same pasuk grounds a daily mitzvah: the kiyum of Kiddush Hashem in the form of the Kedushah recited with a minyan. The Mishnah Berurah[11] codifies this lechatchilah: when the mispallel stands for Kedushah, he should have explicitly in mind the words, “I am about to fulfill the mitzvas aseih d’oraisa of ‘v’nikdashti b’soch Bnei Yisrael.’” This kavvanah is not decorative but essential to the kiyum, since the daily Kedushah is the principal non-martyrdom kiyum of the pasuk.

The chiddush that follows is of significant halachic-hashkafic weight: the Kedushah stands on a higher tier than tefillah itself, which is de’rabbanan. The mispallel who davens Shemoneh Esrei is discharging a mitzvah de’rabbanan; the mispallel who says Kedushah with the tzibbur is discharging a mitzvah de’oraisa. The Arizal HaKadosh was makpid on this kavvanah; saying Kedushah with this consciousness draws the kedushah down “b’soch Bnei Yisrael”.

IX. Regel Yeshara — Joining the Stance of the Malachim

The Terumas HaDeshen[12] rules that during Kedushah one stands with feet together — “regel yeshara” (רגל ישרה) — exactly as during Shemoneh Esrei. The reasoning is textual: the pasuk in Yechezkel א':ז' describes the malachim: “v’ragleihem regel yeshara” (ורגליהם רגל ישרה). The mispallel physically joins the malachim’s stance as he joins their shirah.

X. The Mystical Dimension — Pirkei Heichalos

The Tur’s Unusual Citation

The Tur, Rabbeinu Yaakov Baal HaTurim — who is characteristically distant from kabbalistic literature in his halachic compendium — uniquely cites Pirkei Heichalos (פרקי היכלות) in his treatment of Kedushah.[13] Pirkei Heichalos is a tannaitic-era mystical text — some twenty-odd pages dense with names of malachim and a description of the shamayim during Kedushah; only serious mekubalim ordinarily learn it. The Tur’s decision to lift its imagery into mainstream halachic awareness signals how central this dimension is to the Kedushah’s meaning.

The Scene Described

When Yidden below recite Kedushah — lifting their heels, gazing upward — the Ribbono Shel Olam, kaviyachol (כביכול), experiences an immense simchah. It is as if Klal Yisrael and the Ribbono Shel Olam lock eyes; Klal Yisrael is in love with Him in that moment, and He looks back. He grasps the Kisei HaKavod (כסא הכבוד) — specifically the part upon which the image of Yaakov Avinu is engraved (“Yaakov chakuk al Kisei HaKavod”, יעקב חקוק על כסא הכבוד)[14] — the image standing for Klal Yisrael below. The Midrash describes what follows in language of unmistakable intimacy: “u’mchabek u’menashek” (ומחבק ומנשק) — He embraces and kisses. He declares: “They are in love with Me!” — and at that moment, He awakens geulah (גאולה).

Klal Yisrael has been doing this every day for centuries and the geulah has not yet come. Yet the scene is not thereby diminished. It remains a rega (רגע) of deveikus (דביקות) between Klal Yisrael and the Ribbono Shel Olam that the poskim bring down as halachah, not merely as poetic elaboration.

XI. The Mechanics — Jumping, Trembling, Gazing

Jumping at Kadosh

The Rishonim and the Shulchan Aruch in siman קכ"ה and קכ"ח[19] describe the minhag of jumping at each “Kadosh”. Variations are recorded: some lift the toes only with heels remaining grounded; some lift the heels with toes grounded; some leave the ground entirely. The Mishnah Berurah records the full range. The Magen Avraham cautions against a complete jump in a shul where this is not the minhag of the community — lest one provoke mockery — and rules that lechatchilah the lifting of the heels suffices. The mekubalim: three lifts at the three “Kadosh”s and one at “Baruch kevod.”

The Two Reasons for Motion

Two sources ground the practice of physical motion at the Kedushah:

  • The Midrash Tanchuma[20] develops the imagery of Yeshayahu ו':ב': the malachim have six wings — two cover the face, two cover the feet, and two carry them aloft (“u’vishtayim y’ofef”, ובשתים יעופף) when they sing Kedushah. The mispallel mirrors the malachim’s wing-motion as he joins their shirah.
  • The Shibbolei HaLeket,[24] citing Rashi, brings the pasuk in Yeshayahu ו':ד': “va’yanu’u amos ha’sipim mi’kol ha’korei” (וינועו אמות הספים מקול הקורא) — even the wooden and stone thresholds of the Heichal trembled when the malachim recited Kedushah. Rashi’s kal va’chomer: if the eitzim v’avanim (עצים ואבנים) tremble, all the more so must the mispallel.

Looking Upward

Though not explicit in the body of the Shulchan Aruch, the nose’ei keilim record the practice of looking upward at “Kadosh, kadosh, kadosh.” The mekubalim hold that the eyes ought to be closed; the general minhag is that the eyes are open. Either way, the head is oriented upward — the physical posture of the mispallel enacts the theological content of the Kedushah.

XII. Rabbeinu Kalonymus HaZakein

The most consequential Rishon standing behind the Ashkenazi nusach ha’tefillah and its minhagim is Rabbeinu Kalonymus HaZakein (רבינו קלונימוס הזקן). Every ben Torah should know who he was.[25]

The First Crusade — 1096 / תתנ"ו

The First Crusade began in 1096.[15] Pope Urban II called Christians to retake Yerushalayim from the Fatimid Caliphate; it took three years for the Crusader armies to take the city in July of 1099, with the slaughter of its Muslim and Jewish populations. But the Crusader path through Europe — particularly through Germany and France — left in its wake the devastation of the kehillos ha’kedoshos known collectively by the acronym ShUM (שו"ם):[16]

  • ש — Speyer (Shpira, שפירא)
  • ו — Worms (Vermeiza, ורמייזא)
  • ם — Mainz (Magentza, מגנצא)

The Maharal MiPrague taught that every ben Torah is obligated to know this. These are the cities from which the Ashkenazi mesorah and the nusach ha’tefillah descend — the brief and devastating window of the early Rishonim from Rabbeinu Kalonymus HaZakein through Rashi.

The Burial in the Mikveh

Rabbeinu Kalonymus HaZakein was among the few survivors of the gezeiros in Worms. He fled to Mainz, where cousins took him and his son into their home. When he was niftar, however, Worms was still under siege — the community could not bring him to the beis ha’kevaros of Worms, where his ancestors lay, though they wished to bring him to kever avosav.

The Rosh in Maseches Mo’ed Katan records the ma’aseh, and the Shulchan Aruch in Yoreh De’ah siman shin-ayin-hei brings the pesak:[17] the community placed the aron in the mikveh. Several weeks later, when the siege lifted, they transported him to Worms for kevurah. From this ma’aseh the Rosh develops the halachic discussion of kevurah zemannis (קבורה זמנית) — the parameters of permitted delay in kevurah and what constitutes an acceptable temporary interment. A snippet of catastrophic Jewish history sits embedded permanently in halachah.

The Chain of Sodos HaTefillah

Rabbeinu Kalonymus HaZakein’s Torah reaches later generations through a precise chain of transmission. The hakdamos to Sefer Chassidim record the sequence:

רבינו קלונימוס הזקן → his son → רבי יהודה החסיד (author of Sefer Chassidim) → the רוקח

The sodos ha’tefillah (סודות התפילה) were transmitted father-to-son and rebbi-to-talmid. The Rokeach — Rabbi Elazar MiGarmeiza, of Worms — was the most prolific writer of these peirushei ha’tefillah. Rashi and Tosafos quote Rabbeinu Kalonymus HaZakein repeatedly, and most of the Ashkenazi nusach ha’tefillah traces ultimately to him.

XIII. The Daily Chiyuv — Rabbeinu Kalonymus vs. Rashi

Rabbeinu Kalonymus HaZakein writes a striking teshuvah: the Kedushah that a mispallel says as part of Kedushah d’Yotzeir Or in the morning does not discharge the daily chiyuv of “v’nikdashti b’soch Bnei Yisrael.” To discharge that chiyuv, one must say the Kedushah of chazaras ha’shatz — the Kedushah recited with the minyan.

Rashi, talmid of a talmid of Rabbeinu Kalonymus, dissents: “I have not found such a chiyuv in the Gemara.” Rashi regards the Kedushah as beautiful and beloved to the Ribbono Shel Olam, but not as constituting an absolute daily chiyuv.

The received pesak follows Rabbeinu Kalonymus. The Shulchan Aruch[18] rules accordingly, and the Zohar HaKadosh treats the Kedushah as a kiyum to be discharged daily. The practical implication follows immediately: if a mispallel has missed Kedushah at Shacharis, he should make every effort to catch it at Minchah, or to locate a later minyan, and should not let the day pass without Kedushah.

XIV. The Bnei Eretz Yisrael Practice — and the Cairo Geniza

The Old Minhag

In ancient Eretz Yisrael — long before the era of the Shulchan Aruch — the minhag was to recite Kedushah only on Shabbos and Yom Tov, not during the week. This is striking in light of everything said above about the daily mitzvah. The Tosafos in Sanhedrin ל"ז ע"ב[21] offers a kabbalistic reason for this ancient practice. The malachim have six wings, one for each weekday. On Shabbos they have no seventh wing on which to elevate the Kedushah; the Ribbono Shel Olam answers them, “I have a seventh wing below — Klal Yisrael.” On Shabbos, in this reading, the Kedushah is Klal Yisrael’s; during the week it belongs to the malachim.

The Cairo Geniza

The history by which the real story of the Shabbos-only Kedushah came to be known is itself remarkable. In the late 1800s, two Scottish twin sisters — Margaret Gibson and Agnes Smith Lewis — devoted themselves to Semitic studies and traveled the world hunting for ancient manuscripts for the libraries and universities of Britain. Solomon Schechter was a Jew on the faculty of Cambridge. The sisters traveled to Egypt, to the ancient quarter of Fustat in Cairo where the Rambam had once lived, and there they learned of the Ben Ezra Synagogue’s geniza — an upstairs shaimos repository hundreds of years deep. Schechter saw what they had brought back, recognized what it was, and traveled to Egypt himself. He spent months in an attic without windows or doors — sitting with the manuscripts of a thousand years of Klal Yisrael — sorting, cataloging, and arranging orderly transit to Cambridge. The bulk of the collection remains at Cambridge University Library to this day, available for scholarly consultation. The discovery transformed the modern scholarly understanding of ancient Jewish life.

Rav Pirkoi ben Baboi’s Letter

Among the manuscripts recovered from the geniza was a letter from a previously unknown Gaon: Rav Pirkoi ben Baboi (רב פרקוי בן באבוי),[26] a talmid of Rav Yehudai Gaon of the yeshiva of Sura. His letter, sharp in tone, is directed against several minhagim of Eretz Yisrael — including the Shabbos-only Kedushah.

The Yesod

Rav Pirkoi’s yesod is breathtaking and, on his own view, decisive. The only authentic, unbroken chain of mesorah from the Beis HaMikdash ran through Bavel. The yeshivos of Eretz Yisrael suffered gezeiros, redifos, and shmad (שמד) on a continuous basis from non-Jewish authorities; the minhagim that emerged there in those generations, therefore, cannot be presumed to be unbroken Torah sheba’al peh. They must be examined and, where the historical record allows, distinguished from authentic minhag.

The Historical Reason for Shabbos-Only Kedushah

According to Rav Pirkoi, the Shabbos-only Kedushah in Eretz Yisrael was not a kabbalistic minhag built on the Tosafos’ reading of the malachim’s six wings. It was a relic of persecution. At one point there had been a gezeirah (a Byzantine-era decree) that forbade tefillah be’rabbim (תפילה ברבים) in Eretz Yisrael during the week; the batei knesios were closed. Kedushah, which requires a minyan, simply could not be said. When the gezeirah lifted, the Shabbos-only practice ossified into a minhag.

Rav Pirkoi’s pesak is uncompromising: do not preserve a minhag born of shmad. Recite Kedushah daily, as in Bavel, where the mesorah ran without interruption. The kabbalistic reading of Tosafos is a beautiful theological gloss but does not, in his view, explain how the practice actually arose.

The Larger Machlokes

The Kedushah question was in fact a single facet of a far larger machlokes between the Bnei Bavel (בני בבל) and the Bnei Eretz Yisrael. In the 900s, for two full years, the two communities observed the Yamim Tovim on different days[22] — Pesach on Sunday for one and Monday for the other; Shavuos on different days as well. The dispute concerned authority over the luach after the transition from empirical Kiddush HaChodesh by witnesses to the fixed cheshbon. Bnei Eretz Yisrael claimed authority by location; Bnei Bavel claimed it by mesorah. Every recitation of the Kedushah on a Tuesday morning stands downstream of this larger dispute.

XV. Synthesis

The Kedushah is the tefillah in which Klal Yisrael stands with the malachim and declares itself — through the Gemara’s chiddush — higher than they are; embeds David HaMelech’s pasuk alongside the shirah of the malachim; jumps or trembles or looks upward, mirroring the wing-motion and the trembling thresholds; locks eyes with the Ribbono Shel Olam while He embraces the image of Yaakov engraved upon the Kisei HaKavod; and discharges a daily mitzvah de’oraisa that stands, in halachic weight, l’maalah from tefillah itself.

Behind the Kedushah stands Rabbeinu Kalonymus HaZakein’s teshuvah, transmitted from the burning of ShUM through Sefer Chassidim and the Rokeach to every Ashkenazi siddur in use today. In front of it stands Rav Pirkoi ben Baboi’s letter, unknown to the medieval poskim and recovered a thousand years later from a Cairo geniza through the work of two Scottish twins and a Cambridge Jew. May we say Kedushah tomorrow morning with all of it in mind.

XVI. Practical Summary

  • Say Kedushah daily with a minyan. This is the principal non-martyrdom kiyum of “v’nikdashti b’soch Bnei Yisrael” — a mitzvah de’oraisa that stands above tefillah itself.
  • Have explicit kavvanah. As one rises to Kedushah, articulate internally: I am about to fulfill the mitzvas aseih d’oraisa of ‘v’nikdashti.’
  • Stand regel yeshara. Feet together as in Shemoneh Esrei, joining the malachim’s stance.
  • Lift the heels at each “Kadosh” and at “Baruch kevod.” Lechatchilah, lifting the heels suffices; do not perform a full jump outside a community where all do so.
  • Say the essentials with the chazan; the bridging phrases quietly, after him. The Magen Avraham’s harmonizing pesak between Rosh and Arizal.
  • Do not interrupt Kri’as Shema for anything beyond the essentials. Only “Kadosh, kadosh, kadosh” and “Baruch kevod Hashem mi’mekomo.”
  • If Kedushah was missed at Shacharis, pursue it at Minchah. Do not let the day pass without Kedushah.

Six of the malachim’s wings correspond to the six weekdays; on Shabbos there is no seventh wing. The Ribbono Shel Olam declares: “I have a seventh wing — Klal Yisrael below”; therefore on Shabbos the Kedushah is Klal Yisrael’s, while during the week it remains the malachim’s. Rav Pirkoi ben Baboi’s letter from the geniza displaces this kabbalistic explanation with a historical-political one. ↩

Ben Meir claimed authority by location (Eretz Yisrael); Rav Saadia claimed it by mesorah from the Tannaim. For two years (922 and 923) the kehillos divided: Pesach on different days, Shavuos on different days. Rav Saadia’s authority eventually prevailed and the luach followed the Babylonian cheshbon. The dispute is the most acute historical instance of the broader Bnei Bavel / Bnei Eretz Yisrael tension within which Rav Pirkoi ben Baboi’s letter (a century earlier) belongs.

Rabbeinu Kalonymus HaZakein (Rabbi Kalonymus ben Meshullam, late tenth–early eleventh century, Italy and the Rhineland) — foundational Rishon of Yahadus Ashkenaz; survivor of the destruction of Worms in 1096 (per the chronicles); transmitter of the chain of sodos ha’tefillah recorded in the hakdamos to Sefer Chassidim: Rabbeinu Kalonymus HaZakein → his son → Rabbi Yehudah HaChassid (1140–1217, Regensburg, author of Sefer Chassidim) → the Rokeach (Rabbi Elazar MiGarmeiza, c. 1176–1238, Worms; the most prolific writer of peirushei ha’tefillah in the Chassidei Ashkenaz tradition). Most of the nusach ha’tefillah Ashkenaz traces ultimately to him.

  26. Rav Pirkoi ben Baboi (early ninth century, Bavel) — talmid of Rav Yehudai Gaon (head of the yeshiva of Sura, mid-eighth century). His polemical letter to the communities of Eretz Yisrael and Tzafon Afrika, recovered in fragments from the Cairo Geniza (the geniza of the Ben Ezra Synagogue in Fustat, brought to Cambridge in 1896 by Solomon Schechter through the work of Margaret Gibson and Agnes Smith Lewis), argues against several minhagei Eretz Yisrael — most notably the Shabbos-only Kedushah. His yesod: the only continuous, unbroken mesorah from the Beis HaMikdash ran through Bavel; the minhagim of Eretz Yisrael emerged under shmad, gezeiros, and forced closures of batei knesios, and several should be reformed accordingly. The Shabbos-only Kedushah in particular he attributes to a Byzantine-era gezeirah against weekday tefillah be’rabbim, not to authentic kabbalistic minhag. The letter was unknown to medieval poskim and is now central to scholarly understanding of Bnei Bavel / Bnei Eretz Yisrael tensions.

  27. Shefa Kodesh — kedushas chodesh Nissan as extending throughout the entire month. — Shefa Kodesh is the sefer of Rabbi Yissachar Dov of Belz (1854–1926, third Rebbe of Belz) on the Torah and mo’adim, in the mesorah of his grandfather the Sar Shalom of Belz. He develops the kabbalistic-chassidishe reading that the entire month of Nissan, anchored by Pesach and the redemption from Mitzrayim, carries the kedushah of Rosh Chodesh throughout — explaining the minhag to omit tachanun the entire month and to maintain festive demeanor. ↩
  28. Minhag HaGra — Shabbos hat (spodik) on Rosh Chodesh; cf. Shulchan Aruch Orach Chaim siman 419. — Recorded in the Ma’aseh Rav and the Minhagei HaGra: Rabbi Eliyahu of Vilna (1720–1797, the Gra) wore his Shabbos hat — a spodik in the levush of his time — on Rosh Chodesh, but did not change into the rest of his bigdei Shabbos. The reasoning preserved by his talmidim: Rosh Chodesh is Yom Tov in kedushah but muttar bi’melachah; the neshamah experiences the full Yom Tov while the body still does melachah, so the head — seat of the neshamah — wears Shabbos garb while the rest of the body does not. The Shulchan Aruch in siman 419 records the broader minhag to wear at least some bigdei Shabbos on Rosh Chodesh. ↩
  29. Arizal HaKadosh, Peri Etz Chaim / Shaar HaKavvanos — kabbalah requirement to recite the full nusach ha’Kedushah, including “L’umasam meshabchim v’omrim.” — Rabbi Yitzchak Luria (the Arizal, 1534–1572, Tzfas), as transmitted in Peri Etz Chaim and Shaar HaKavvanos by Rabbi Chaim Vital, ruled — against the Rosh’s narrow reading — that the kahal recites the entire nusach ha’Kedushah: “Nakdishcha v’na’aritzcha,” the bridging phrases (“L’umasam meshabchim v’omrim”), and the third pasuk. The basis is kabbalistic: each phrase corresponds to a specific tikkun in the upper worlds, and the kahal’s participation is essential to the structural ascent. The Ashkenazi minhag follows the Arizal; the Litvish minhag follows the Rosh. Magen Avraham’s qualification (recite quietly, after the chazan) is the harmonizing pesak. ↩
