כְּפִילַת מִלִּים בַּחֲזָנוּת

Repetition of Words in Chazanus
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Sunday Morning Series on Tefillah
Recorded 2 March 2025  ·  Sefer Edition · Revised 30 August 2026

The following chapter treats a question that lives at the meeting point of halacha, aesthetics, and the sociology of the bais hakenesses: may a shaliach tzibbur (שליח ציבור) repeat words in the fixed nusach of tefillah for the sake of the niggun (ניגון)? The mekoros read the reader through five centuries of increasingly resigned protest.

I. The Question in Its Setting

A chazan (חזן) of the old European school — trained in the tradition of Kwartin, Rosenblatt, Pinchik, and Koussevitzky — steps to the amud (עמוד) on a Shabbos morning. The nusach flows; the kehilla is stirred. But at several points in the tefillah he does what the great cantorial repertoire requires him to do: for the sake of the musical line, he repeats a word. Ha-E-l becomes Ha-E-l, Ha-E-l; mekimi becomes mekimi, mekimi. To the oilam (עולם) it is beautiful. To one man in the back it is a serious halachic problem. He waits for the end of davening and comes forward.

The question is not new. Every generation for the past five hundred years has been forced to reckon with it, because in every generation the same tension has reasserted itself: the chazan is a musician, and the fixed nusach of tefillah is a text. Musical structure often demands what textual fixity forbids. The halacha did not develop in a vacuum. It developed in and against the actual practice of chazanim (חזנים), and its mekoros preserve the sound of the argument.

Before entering the sugya (סוגיא) it is worth naming the internal check that any halachic protest of this kind requires. Is the objection kanaus l'sheim Shamayim (קנאות לשם שמים) — a jealous concern for the honor of tefillah itself — or is it, more quietly, the discomfort of a rav or a congregant whose authority has been sidestepped? The poskim ahead do not soften on the underlying issur; but the honesty of the question is itself part of the sugya, and it belongs at the entrance rather than in a footnote.

II. The Foundational Mishnah: מוֹדִים מוֹדִים

The starting point is the fifth perek of Berachos (ברכות):[1]

הָאוֹמֵר "מוֹדִים מוֹדִים" — מְשַׁתְּקִין אוֹתוֹ.
One who says “Modim Modim” — we silence him.
Mishnah Berachos 5:3

The gemara (גמרא) supplies the reason: to say “Modim” twice suggests that one is addressing two entities.[2] The concern was not theoretical. In the bais hakenesses (בית הכנסת) of the Mishnaic and Talmudic era, minim (מינים) and dualists prayed alongside Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל); a doubled word of thanksgiving could be seized upon and read as an admission of the very heresy the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah had structured the Shemoneh Esrei (שמונה עשרה) to exclude.

The same sugya in Berachos 33 extends the principle: one who repeats “Shema” is meguneh (מגונה). The gemara's milder verb marks a distinction — between the actively silenced and the merely disapproved — that the Rishonim (ראשונים) discuss at length and that need not detain the present sugya. What matters is that the Mishnah names two specific words and forbids their repetition; the classical dispute begins where the Mishnah stops.

III. The Machlokes Rishonim: How Far Does the Rule Reach?

The Ritva — A Principle, Not a List

The Ritva (ריטב"א) — Rabbeinu Yom Tov ben Avraham Asvili (c. 1260–1320), foremost talmid of the Ra'ah and the Rashba — reads the Mishnah as principle rather than as list.[3] Chazal (חז"ל) did not intend to prohibit only the two specific words named in the text. Any repetition capable of suggesting dual address falls within the issur (איסור). This is the strict reading that will later become the Beis Yosef's (בית יוסף) position.

Rabbeinu Yonah — The Kushia from Ne’ilah

Rabbeinu Yonah of Gerona (רבינו יונה, c. 1200–1263), commenting on the Rif, raises the difficulty that the Rishonim after him will have to resolve.[4] If repetition is forbidden, how does the tzibbur (ציבור) at Ne'ilah (נעילה) call out “Hashem Hu HaElokim” (ה' הוא האלקים) seven times? The machzorim (מחזורים) offer the mystical reading — that the Shechinah ascends through the seven rekiim (רקיעים) with each acclamation — but the halachic question stands on its own.

The resolution reshapes the whole sugya. The phrase itself is not new liturgical composition. It is the biblical cry of Klal Yisrael at Har HaKarmel (הר הכרמל):

וַיַּרְא כׇּל הָעָם, וַיִּפְּלוּ עַל פְּנֵיהֶם, וַיֹּאמְרוּ: ה' הוּא הָאֱלֹקִים, ה' הוּא הָאֱלֹקִים.
All the people saw, and they fell on their faces, and they said: Hashem, He is God; Hashem, He is God.
Melachim I 18:39[5]

When the entire tzibbur together reenacts Har HaKarmel, the theological framework the Mishnah was guarding against — the suggestion of dual address by a lone speaker in a mixed bais hakenesses — is off the table by construction. A communal, biblically-modeled acclamation is a different din altogether. The same logic covers the doubled pesukim that close Pesukei d'Zimra (פסוקי דזמרא) — “Kol haneshamah tehallel Yah, Halleluyah” (כל הנשמה תהלל י-ה הללוי-ה) — and the closing “Hashem yimloch l'olam va'ed” (ה' ימלוך לעולם ועד) of Shiras HaYam (שירת הים). These are structural seals, parallel in form to chazak chazak ve'nischazek, not doubled addresses.[6]

Rabbeinu Yonah's resolution supplies a distinction that later poskim depend on without always naming: repetition that is structural and communal is a different halachic category from repetition that is interpolative and individual. Everything ahead in the sugya — the Beis Yosef's prohibition, the Maharam Shik's five reasons, and the eventual heter for Hallel — is worked out within this bifurcation.

The Bach — The Halachic Counterweight

The Bach (ב"ח) — Bayis Chadash, Rabbi Yoel Sirkis (1561–1640, Cracow) — takes the opposite methodological road.[7] The Mishnah, he argues, names “Modim” and “Shema”, and no others. A derabbanan (דרבנן) is not to be extended by hekesh (היקש). Further, the underlying concern about minim exploiting a doubled word presupposes the acoustic conditions of a bais hakenesses where a lone speaker could be overheard by a hostile listener; where the entire tzibbur is davening aloud in unison, the original grounds of the gezeirah (גזירה) evaporate.

The Bach's position must be taken seriously on its own terms and not dismissed as idiosyncratic. He is a first-tier posek (פוסק), and his lenient reading is the actual halachic counterweight to the mainline strict view — not the far more controversial heter that will appear later in this chapter. The methodological question he raises is genuine: what is the halachic status of the issur beyond “Modim” and “Shema”? Is it a full issur extended by hekesh, a gezeirah whose reasoning is time-bound, or a hiddur (הידור)? The Beis Yosef's answer — that Chazal's principle is not exhausted by their examples and that the extension is itself part of the gezeirah — carries the day l'halacha (להלכה);[20] but the Bach's methodological reservation continues to hover over the sugya, and later chazanim who will one day lean on the Venetian heter often think they are leaning on the Bach. They are not; the Bach is a narrower and more disciplined position.

IV. The Four Categories of Repetition

Before proceeding to the nineteenth-century poskim, it is useful to sort the mekoros. Not every act of repetition in tefillah is the same halachic phenomenon. At least four distinct categories are visible in the sources:

  1. Theologically-loaded repetition — “Modim Modim,” “Shema Shema”: forbidden by the Mishnah; the mechanism is maris ayin (מראית עין) of dual address; the response is meshatkin oso.
  2. Mid-berachah word-repetition — the chazan who says “Ha-E-l, Ha-E-l” for the sake of the niggun: this is the operative subject of the Maharam Shik's teshuvah, and it is here that the multiple grounds of prohibition — hefsek (הפסק), bal tosif (בל תוסיף), kavod malchei ha-melachim (כבוד מלכי המלכים) — converge.
  3. Structural closing doublings — “Kol haneshamah,” “Hashem yimloch,” “Hashem Hu HaElokim” at Ne'ilah: permitted lechatchilah per Rabbeinu Yonah; the category is siyum and edus tzibburis, not repetition.
  4. Minhag nevi'im repetition inside Hallel — “Ana Hashem hoshia na,” the closing pesukim: positively enjoined by the Mishnah in Sukkah;[19] the mechanism is takanas nevi'im, obligatory rather than tolerated.

The Maharam Shik's five reasons, which follow, all address category (2). They do not touch categories (3) and (4). This sorting will become load-bearing at the end of the chapter, where the practical heter for Hallel rests precisely on the difference between (2) and (4).

V. The Maharam Shik — Five Reasons Against the Chazan's Repetition

The Setting

Rabbi Moshe Shick, the Maharam Shik (מהר"ם שיק, 1809–1879), was the foremost talmid of the Chasam Sofer (חתם סופר) and Rosh Yeshiva in Huszt (חוסט, Hungarian Huszt / modern Ukrainian Khust), where he taught eight hundred talmidim and authored a Shu"t (שו"ת) of over a thousand teshuvos. The acronym “Shik” stands for “Shmo Yisroel Kodesh” (שמו ישראל קודש). The tradition preserves the account of him as a bachur, slow to grasp limud, whose sheer hasmadah (התמדה) transformed him into the Chasam Sofer's greatest talmid; the Chasam Sofer, defending his sleeping talmid on a wagon journey, called him a living Sefer Torah. The point is not sentimental. It bears on how one should read what follows: a teshuvah written by a man whose intellectual authority derives from an entire life of quiet, disciplined hasmadah under the Chasam Sofer's eye.

The Historical Moment

The Maharam Shik's era was the nineteenth-century struggle against the Haskalah (השכלה) and Reform in central Europe. The Chasam Sofer had been the leading kanai (קנאי) of the generation; the Maharam Shik stood as his standard-bearer. Reform's characteristic liturgical innovations — the bimah moved to the front of the bais hakenesses, the organ introduced within it, choral and operatic chazanus (חזנות) with chidushim in the nusach — were the visible battlefield. Hungarian Jewish life was tri-partite in that period: the Neologs (Reform), the Orthodox, and the so-called Status Quo movement between them. In a well-known teshuvah to the Ksav Sofer (כתב סופר) — the Chasam Sofer's son — the Maharam Shik writes that even the Status Quo must be treated as fully outside the halachic community, on the principle that one cannot remain neutral where emes is being negotiated away.

Against this backdrop, the Maharam Shik's language on the chazan question is sharp. It is worth registering, however, one asymmetry that qualifies any simple reading of him as a reflexive traditionalist: on the introduction of German for the drashah he did not oppose the innovation. He himself spoke elegant German and considered that particular front unwinnable. The cherem against changing the language of the drashah was pressed most forcefully in the Eleh Divrei HaBris of 1819, in the wake of the Hamburg-Altona dispute over the Reform temple's German prayers; that cherem, and not any imaginary 1800 declaration, is what continues to shape certain kehilos' refusal of vernacular drashos to this day.[21]

The Five Reasons

In Shu"t Maharam Shik, Orach Chaim,[8] the Maharam Shik assembles five independent grounds on which a chazan is forbidden to repeat words in Shemoneh Esrei, Kedushah (קדושה), or any “matbea sh'tavu Chachamim” (מטבע שטבעו חכמים) — the fixed coinage of tefillah:

  1. Bal tosif. Tefillah is derabbanan, but Chazal's takanos are k'ein deoraisa (כעין דאורייתא). To add to the fixed nusach — even by repetition — is a form of bal tosif. Whether bare repetition of an existing word (rather than the introduction of new content) is a genuine hosafah for these purposes is itself a real lomdishe question. Rav Ovadia Yosef in Shu"t Yabia Omer[9] takes it up at length and rules narrowly: a yachid (יחיד) who lost kavvanah (כוונה) may in defined circumstances repeat “Baruch”; a shaliach tzibbur may not. The operative distinction, on reflection, is probably not bal tosif at all but the public/private divide of hefsek — which is the Maharam Shik's next ground.
  2. Public embarrassment / amad nizok. The very act of repeating a word broadcasts, in front of the tzibbur, that the first recitation lacked kavvanah. What sounds to the ear like enhancement functions in halachic reality like an admission of inadequate first attempt.
  3. Hefsek. This is the central reason, and the one to which the others are structurally subordinate. A word repeated in the middle of a berachah is an interruption, no different in halachic form from an idle utterance. The berachah's matbea is a single continuous verbal act; repetition breaks it.
  4. The issur of turning tefillah into shirah. The gemara in Sanhedrin[10] warns that when pesukim are treated as material for ordinary musical performance, the Torah personified comes before the Kisei HaKavod wrapped in sackcloth and protests: “Your children have made me into a singer's instrument.” Contemporary practice tolerates a great deal of singing in tefillah that the gemara's framework would treat with alarm; the underlying gravity remains. One is standing before the Melech, not producing a performance.
  5. Kavod malchei ha-melachim. A person addressing an earthly monarch on a matter of consequence would not stutter, would not double back, would not repeat words for aesthetic effect. Before the Melech Malchei HaMelachim the same elementary decorum applies. What is at stake in the chazan's repetition is not principally the aesthetics of the tefillah but the awareness of the One before whom the tefillah stands.

The Psak

Having assembled the five grounds, the Maharam Shik rules that one is obligated to protest a chazan who repeats words in the fixed nusach, and to remove him from the amud even in the middle of tefillah.[11] The severity of the halachic remedy invites a further question: is the removal a kenas (קנס), a communal disciplinary act aimed at the chazan's conduct; or is it a statement that the chazan's chazaras hashatz (חזרת הש"ץ) is halachically invalid, so that the tzibbur is exposed and must have a valid shatz installed to complete the tefillah? The Maharam Shik's lashon tilts toward the second: the repetition disqualifies the chazaras hashatz as a proper matbea, and the tzibbur's chiyuv is unfulfilled. The practical stakes follow accordingly — a tzibbur that lets the chazan finish has not necessarily discharged its chazaras hashatz.

VI. The Machaneh Chaim — Removing a Chazan from the Amud

The Maharam Shik's ruling is not merely theoretical. The Machaneh Chaim — Rabbi Chaim Sofer (1821–1886, Hungary) — records his father's actual practice.[12] In a Sephardic bais hakenesses, the chazan at Mussaf (מוסף) began repeating words during “Kesser” (כתר) in the middle of Kedushah. The elder Rabbi Sofer was mafsik from Kedushah mid-tefillah, addressed the chazan directly — “Get off the amud; you cannot daven for this tzibbur” — and physically removed him. The Machaneh Chaim preserves the episode as normative practice, not merely as anecdote. The psak is thus continuously attested from the early nineteenth century through its late decades: the mid-berachah repetition is forbidden, and the halachic remedy is unusually forceful.

VII. Rav Moshe Feinstein in America

Rav Moshe Feinstein zt"l (רב משה פיינשטיין, 1895–1986) took up the same question, in a very different setting, in Igros Moshe (אגרות משה).[13] The great chazanim of the pre- and post-war generations — Kwartin, Rosenblatt, Pinchik, Koussevitzky — had populated American shuls, and the cantorial repertoire that had crystallized in Europe over the preceding century required, as its musical building block, precisely the mid-word repetition that the Beis Yosef, the Maharam Shik, and the Machaneh Chaim had ruled out.

The chazanim's argument to Rav Moshe was the argument every chazan makes: without repetition, the niggun cannot be performed. Rav Moshe's answer was that this is not the cheshbon of Shulchan Aruch. The Beis Yosef rules clearly against repetition; the Bach is not the position l'halacha; the musical requirements of a particular repertoire cannot override a psak. He does not, in the body of the teshuvah, engage the Venetian heter that will occupy the next chapter of the present sugya; he paskens with the Beis Yosef as if the heter were absent. The strategic significance of this silence is itself worth noting: for Rav Moshe, the Venetian heter did not rise to the level of a position that a mainstream posek was obligated to refute. It is treated as beneath the argument.

VIII. Why the Chazanim Persist

One reads through Beis Yosef, Maharam Shik, Machaneh Chaim, and Igros Moshe and notices the same phenomenon: the poskim are screaming, and the chazanim, by and large, are not listening. L'halacha meforeshes (להלכה מפורשת) — why?

The answer, when the chazanim's seforim and their traditions of practice are examined honestly, comes down to a single teshuvah. There is one voice, almost solitary in the corpus of poskim, which permits repetition for the sake of the niggun. That voice belongs to a gaon olam of early-seventeenth-century Venice whose biography and whose halachic method deserve to be seen clearly before his ruling is weighed. Without knowing who he was, one cannot know what the chazanim are actually leaning on.

IX. Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Modena

The Ilui

Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Modena (רבי יהודה אריה ממודינא, c. 1571–1648, ה'ת"ח) was a gaon olam in early seventeenth-century Venice.[14] Born in Venice, the family originated in Modena in northern Italy near Milan. He learned under Rabbi Shmuel Yehudah Katzenellenbogen, son of the Maharam of Padua. In his autobiographical memoir Chayei Yehudah (חיי יהודה) he records that at two and a half he stood up in shul and recited an entire Haftorah by heart; the memoir is the source, and what is known is what the memoir attests, no more and no less.[22]

The Chochmos Chitzoniyos Track

He immersed himself in chochmos chitzoniyos (חכמות חיצוניות) — music, drama, poetry — to a degree without near-parallel among the Rishonim and Acharonim. He was a playwright of substantial reputation in the Italian literary world of his time. The Chida (חיד"א) in Shem HaGedolim,[15] writing a century and a half later, brings him as a chashuv talmid chacham with his seforim and pesakim intact; the Chida's inclusion places him within the standard canon of Acharonim despite what would follow.

The Tragic Hisma’chrus

By his own account he was, from adolescence, in the grip of a gambling addiction. Already at thirteen he composed a dialogue-sefer against gambling, structured as a debate between Eldad (the gambler) and Meidad (the friend attempting to pull him out), the names borrowed from the seventy zekeinim in Parshas Beha'aloscha.[16] In effect a thirteen-year-old writing to himself. The work is historically referenced as “Sefer Eldad u’Meidad” and preserved under variant titles.

The autobiographical passages of Chayei Yehudah are of unusual candor for an Acharon. He records that on Shavuos (שבועות) he davened in bais hakenesses, fasting and exerting himself in avodah; a ruach shtus (רוח שטות) then entered him, his mind clouded, and his feet carried him to the gambling house, where he lost everything he had. The severity of the entry, and the fact that he preserved it in writing, are themselves halachic data.

The Family Cost

He records the destruction of his family in the same voice. Three children: one died in youth, one was killed under circumstances his memoir does not fully explain (traditions surrounding gambling debts have been suggested), one fled to Brazil in the sixteenth-century Portuguese-Jewish diaspora and returned only after his father's death. His wife's mental health failed under the strain. In one passage he writes: “What have I done to my family? My wife is ill, I have no relationship with my children, I am destroying my life.” And in the same period he was writing teshuvos and instructing talmidim.

The Darshan

He was a darshan of extraordinary power, drawing large audiences — and, notably, non-Jewish auditors who came to hear his oratory. His talmidim included some of the leading figures of the Venetian and broader Italian-Jewish world of the mid-seventeenth century; whether Baruch Spinoza, whose formal teachers in Amsterdam were Saul Levi Morteira and Manasseh ben Israel, had any direct or indirect intellectual link to Modena's Venetian circle is a matter for the historical literature and not for the present halachic argument.

X. Shu"t Zikkenei Yehudah — The Heter and Its Method

Repetition for the Sake of the Niggun

In Shu"t Zikkenei Yehudah, Klal 1,[17] Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh takes up the very question the present chapter has been building toward. His argument, in summary, is musicological: he has studied music, he understands the internal structure of niggunim, and he holds that certain niggunim cannot be sung without the repetition of words. Because the repetition is imposed by the musical form rather than introduced as an interpolation, he does not see it as a halachic problem. His own example is his chazan's repeated “Kesser Kesser” in Kedushah, which he defends enthusiastically. He does not engage the Rishonim's framework in detail; the teshuvah reads as if the sources he would need to overcome have simply been set aside.

Christian Melodies in the Bais Hakenesses

Later in the same teshuvah[18] he extends the argument further. It is permissible, he writes, to take non-Jewish liturgical melodies and incorporate them into the chazanus of the bais hakenesses. He records his own practice: before Yamim Nora'im (ימים נוראים) he would go, lehavdil (להבדיל), to church in Venice to hear which melodies were being composed for their liturgy; he had a strong ear, he memorized them, and on Rosh Hashanah the chazanim of his circle would sing them. The Bach has a separate discussion of the general question of adopting non-Jewish melodies for tefillah, from a different angle, and the topic has its own literature.

XI. The Weight of the Heter

Here the chapter must proceed with care. The halacha does not, as a general rule, invalidate a psak on account of the posek's personal failings. The Chida's inclusion of Modena in Shem HaGedolim reflects precisely this discipline: a psak is weighed on its own mekoros and its own reasoning, not on the biography of the man who signed it. To argue simply that Modena's heter should be disregarded because Modena was a ba'al aveirah would be to trade the halachic argument for a rhetorical one.

What can nevertheless be said, on strictly halachic grounds, is this. The heter is a daas yachid (דעת יחיד) against the overwhelming mainline of Rishonim and Acharonim, from the Ritva and the Beis Yosef forward. Its methodological ground is a musicological sevara (סברא) — that the internal structure of the niggun imposes the repetition — rather than an engagement with the Mishnah's and the gemara's frameworks on their own terms. A musical sevara is not, in the Beis Yosef's categories, a halachic counterweight to a psak; it is a consideration that a posek may (or may not) weigh, but that does not, on its own, displace an established ruling. Rav Moshe's silent handling of the heter reflects this reading: he does not refute it because there is nothing halachically to refute.

Where the biography does bear on the argument is precisely at the point of internal disclosure. The author of the heter was himself deeply invested, by his own testimony, in the musical world whose interests the heter serves. Modena's Chayei Yehudah preserves in his own hand not only his gambling but his continued attraction to the concerts of the shloshes ha-shavuos (שלושת השבועות) which he acknowledges are forbidden. This is not offered as a substitute for the halachic case; it is offered as a caution that the chazanim who rest their practice on this single heter should know what they are resting on. The heter's isolation from the mainline sources, its methodological method, and the interest-alignment of its author are three independent reasons for weighing it with restraint. They do not, taken together, produce a psak; they explain why the Beis Yosef's psak holds.

XII. The Aruch HaShulchan — The Realist Coda

The Aruch HaShulchan (ערוך השלחן) — Rabbi Yechiel Michel Epstein (1829–1908, Novardok) — captures the situation with characteristic realism.[23] In Hilchos Shabbos, addressing the chazan's tuning fork, he calls it a kli l’genai (כלי לגנאי) but concludes that one cannot protest against the chazanim: the kehilla wants its chazan, is uninterested in the fine points of hilchos Shabbos, and machlokes (מחלוקת) must not be provoked.

Then, in parentheses in the same siman, he adds a second grief with the same chazanim: they repeat words. Anyone with yiras shamayim, he writes, suffers when he hears it. But — “einan shomim b'kol divrei chachamim” (אינן שומעים בקול דברי חכמים): they do not listen; the oilam will not accept the rebuke from the rav. He then adds, faintly, that “perhaps in truth there is no real issur” — invoking, almost inaudibly, the Venetian heter — and concludes with the meta-klal: once a practice has spread widely among the tzibbur, one does not raise a great commotion against it.

This is the most painful single passage in the sugya. The Aruch HaShulchan's invocation of the Venetian heter is not made because he himself trusts it; it is made because the oilam's practice has already made the argument for him, and he is describing rather than endorsing. The meta-klal then reveals its own gravitational limit. If entrenchment of practice is itself the criterion of halachic tolerance, the psak authority has effectively been transferred from the poskim to the chazanim — the very inversion the Maharam Shik had been fighting one generation earlier. The Aruch HaShulchan is not celebrating this transfer. He is describing what has already occurred, and letting the parentheses register the pain of it.

XIII. L'Halacha L'Ma'aseh — The Heter for Hallel

The chapter closes with the one place where the heter for repetition holds on its own terms, without leaning on Modena. On Rosh Chodesh (ראש חודש), a chazan asked to repeat words in Hallel (הלל) can be granted a heter, on two grounds that operate independently and reinforce one another:

  1. Hallel is a self-contained liturgical unit, with its own berachah before and after. On Rosh Chodesh the tzibbur says chatzi Hallel (חצי הלל) rather than Hallel HaShalem; the hefsek concern that dominates the Maharam Shik's treatment of Shemoneh Esrei repetition operates with reduced force here.
  2. More fundamentally: repetition is built into Hallel itself. “Ana Hashem hoshia na, Ana Hashem hatzlicha na” is doubled; the closing pesukim are doubled. This is minhag nevi'im, taught in the Mishnah in Sukkah.[19]

The second ground is the load-bearing one. Once a tefillah is by its own construction a doubled-pesukim form, the theological concern of “two entities” is off the table by definition — the same category that Rabbeinu Yonah invoked at Ne'ilah. A chazan's further repetition of words within Hallel, at least within the doubled pesukim themselves and their immediate structural neighborhood, is not the same halachic act as his repetition inside a Shemoneh Esrei berachah. The hefsek ground (i.e., ground 1 above) then supplies the additional cushion that permits a wider grant in practice.

For Hallel on Rosh Chodesh, then, the heter stands, grounded in a Mishnah in Sukkah and a chatzi-Hallel structure. For Kedushah, chazaras hashatz, and the balance of Shemoneh Esrei, the psak of the Beis Yosef, the Maharam Shik, the Machaneh Chaim, and Rav Moshe is unchanged. The same chazan, in the same davening, receives a heter in one tefillah and no heter in another — because each tefillah is governed by a different set of mekoros, and the halacha is fine-grained enough to hold both rulings at once.

XIV. Closing

What the sugya shows, in the long arc from the Mishnah to the Aruch HaShulchan, is that a fixed nusach of tefillah is not the private property of the chazan. It is the inherited coinage of Chazal, and every generation of poskim has understood that it must be defended against the pressures that would deform it — whether those pressures come from a Venetian musicological argument, a nineteenth-century Reform aesthetic, or the twentieth-century golden age of American chazanus. The defense has not, on the whole, prevailed with the oilam. That does not change the psak.

The Rabbi's role, when a chazan steps to the amud with a Rosenblatt or a Koussevitzky in his memory, is neither to celebrate the beauty of the niggun nor to declare war on the chazan. It is to know the sugya well enough to draw the line at the right place: chas v'shalom in Shemoneh Esrei, in Kedushah, in chazaras hashatz; a specific, sourced heter in Hallel; and, all along, the internal question of whether the protest is kanaus l'sheim Shamayim or a defense of one's own authority. On the honesty of that internal question, more of the sugya depends than any of the mekoros quite say.

Each note runs short citation first (for quick lookup), then an expanded elaboration.

  1. Mishnah Berachos 5:3 — “Ha’omer ‘Modim Modim’ — meshatkin oso.” — The opening Mishnah of the fifth perek of Berachos. The gemara at Berachos 33b explains the silencing as a guard against the appearance of addressing two entities — an active concern in the bais hakenesses of the Mishnaic era, where minim and dualists prayed alongside Klal Yisrael. ↩
  2. Berachos 33b — “Ha-koreh Kriyas Shema, ve’kofelah — harei zeh meguneh.” — The gemara extends the principle of Mishnah 5:3 to one who repeats “Shema”, but uses the milder meguneh rather than meshatkin oso. The precise distinction is debated by the Rishonim and is set aside in the present chapter. ↩
  3. Chidushei HaRitva on Berachos 33b (and parallel in Sukkah). — Rabbeinu Yom Tov ben Avraham Asvili (c. 1260–1320, Spain), foremost talmid of the Ra’ah and the Rashba, holds that Chazal never intended to restrict the issur to the two specific words named in the Mishnah. Any repetition that could suggest dual address is included. This is the strict reading that the Beis Yosef subsequently adopts. ↩
  4. Rabbeinu Yonah on the Rif, Berachos ch. 5 — the kushia from “Hashem Hu HaElokim.” — Rabbeinu Yonah of Gerona (c. 1200–1263) raises the difficulty: if repetition is forbidden, why does the tzibbur at Ne’ilah say “Hashem Hu HaElokim” seven times? His resolution — that a communal, biblically-modeled repetition is a categorically different din from a personal interpolation — supplies the framework within which the entire subsequent sugya is worked out. ↩
  5. Melachim I 18:39. — The biblical source for the doubled declaration. After the fire descends at Har HaKarmel and consumes Eliyahu HaNavi’s korban, the entire nation falls on its face and proclaims Hashem’s exclusivity twice. The tefillah at Ne’ilah echoes this collective acclamation. ↩
  6. Tur and Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chaim siman 51 (laws of Pesukei d’Zimra); Magen Avraham and Mishnah Berurah on the closing pesukim of Shiras HaYam. — The doubled closing pesukim — “Kol haneshamah tehallel Yah, Halleluyah” and “Hashem yimloch l’olam va’ed” — function as structural seals, not as separate addresses. The poskim treat them as concluding markers, parallel in form to chazak chazak ve’nischazek at the end of a chumash. ↩
  7. Bach (Bayis Chadash) on Tur, Orach Chaim siman 61 — lenient view. — Rabbi Yoel Sirkis (1561–1640, Cracow), the Bach, argues that the Mishnah’s prohibition is limited to the specific words named in it — “Modim” and “Shema” — and is reluctant to extend a derabbanan by hekesh. He further argues that when the tzibbur is davening aloud the concern about a min exploiting the repetition evaporates. The Bach stands famously at odds with the Beis Yosef across many sugyos; this is one of his characteristic counter-readings. ↩
  8. Shu"t Maharam Shik, Orach Chaim, siman on chazanus (variously cited in the printed editions). — Rabbi Moshe Shik (1809–1879, Hungary), foremost talmid of the Chasam Sofer and Rosh Yeshiva in Huszt, wrote a Shu"t of over a thousand teshuvos. The teshuvah on the practice of nineteenth-century chazanim assembles five independent grounds for prohibition: bal tosif; public embarrassment / amad nizok; hefsek; the issur of shirah in tefillah; and kavod malchei ha-melachim. The siman is cited variously in different oral mesorahs of this sugya; the reader should consult a printed Maharam Shik, Orach Chaim to fix the exact siman in the edition at hand. ↩
  9. Shu"t Yabia Omer (Rav Ovadia Yosef), Orach Chaim — extended treatment of repetition and bal tosif. — Rav Ovadia Yosef zt"l (1920–2013) takes up the question whether the bare repetition of an existing word counts as an hosafah for purposes of bal tosif, or only the introduction of new material. He surveys the Rishonim and rules narrowly: a private yachid who lost kavvanah may in defined circumstances repeat, but a shaliach tzibbur may not. The teshuvah appears in the chalakim on Orach Chaim. ↩
  10. Sanhedrin 101a — “Ha-koreh pasuk shel Shir HaShirim ve’oseh oso k’min zemer… ha-Torah chogeres sak… ve’omeres lifnei ha-Kadosh Baruch Hu: Ribbono shel Olam, asaani banecha ki-kinor she’menagnin bo leitzim.” — The gemara’s warning against treating pesukim as material for ordinary musical performance. The Torah personified comes before the Kisei HaKavod wrapped in sackcloth and protests that her words have been turned into a singer’s instrument. The Maharam Shik uses this as the fourth of his five grounds. ↩
  11. Maharam Shik, ibid. — psak requiring removal of the chazan from the amud. — Concluding the same teshuvah, the Maharam Shik rules that one is obligated to protest a chazan who repeats words and to remove him from the amud, even mid-tefillah. Whether the removal is a kenas or a statement that the chazaras hashatz is halachically invalid is discussed in the body of the present chapter (siman V); the Maharam Shik’s lashon tilts toward the invalidity reading. ↩
  12. Shu"t Machaneh Chaim (Rabbi Chaim Sofer) — incident at a Sephardic bais hakenesses at “Kesser.” — Rabbi Chaim Sofer (1821–1886, Hungary) records his father’s actual practice. The chazan in a Sephardic bais hakenesses began repeating words during “Kesser” in Kedushas Mussaf; the elder Rabbi Sofer interrupted Kedushah, instructed the chazan to step down from the amud, and physically expelled him. The episode furnishes the psak from the mid-nineteenth century and matches the Maharam Shik’s ruling. ↩
  13. Shu"t Igros Moshe (Rav Moshe Feinstein), Orach Chaim — prohibition of word-repetition by chazanim. — Rav Moshe Feinstein zt"l (1895–1986) addressed the question as the great American chazanim of the early twentieth century — Kwartin, Rosenblatt, Pinchik, Koussevitzky — populated American shuls. He rules sharply with the Beis Yosef against the Bach: musical convenience cannot override a psak of the Shulchan Aruch. The teshuvah appears in Orach Chaim, Chelek II; the reader should consult the printed Igros Moshe to fix the exact siman. Rav Moshe does not engage the Venetian heter of Zikkenei Yehudah directly; the silence is itself argument. ↩
  14. Biographical data on Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Modena — drawn primarily from his own memoir Chayei Yehudah. — The autobiographical Chayei Yehudah is the principal source for the biographical material in the present chapter, including the childhood account, the gambling addiction, the Shavuos episode, and the destruction of the family. The memoir circulated in manuscript from the seventeenth century and was published in modern critical editions in the twentieth century, most notably in the edition of Daniel Carpi (Tel Aviv, 1985), which serves as the standard scholarly reference for citation purposes. ↩
  15. Shem HaGedolim (Chida), entry on Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Modena. — Rabbi Chaim Yosef David Azulai (1724–1806), the Chida, brings Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh as a chashuv talmid chacham with his seforim and pesakim. The Chida’s inclusion places him within the standard canon of Acharonim and stands as the historical warrant for the general principle discussed in siman XI: a psak is weighed on its own merits and not on the biography of its author. That the heter is nevertheless weighed with restraint in the present chapter is not a violation of this principle but its application on independent halachic grounds. ↩
  16. The anti-gambling dialogue at age thirteen — historically referenced as “Sefer Eldad u’Meidad,” also under variant titles such as Sur me’Ra. — The dialogue between Eldad (the gambler) and Meidad (his moral counterweight), the names borrowed from the seventy zekeinim in Bemidbar 11:26. The sefer has been printed under variant titles in different editions; the standard scholarly literature on Modena preserves the textual history. ↩
  17. Shu"t Zikkenei Yehudah, Klal 1 (Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Modena) — heter for word-repetition for the sake of the niggun. — Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh of Modena (c. 1571–1648, Venice) treats the question musicologically in his Shu"t Zikkenei Yehudah: a trained ear understands that the structure of certain niggunim requires the repetition of words, and there is therefore no issur. He gives his own chazan’s “Kesser Kesser” as illustration. The teshuvah stands nearly alone among the poskim on the lenient side; the chazanim of subsequent centuries have leaned on it almost exclusively. ↩
  18. Shu"t Zikkenei Yehudah, ibid. — incorporation of non-Jewish melodies into chazanus. — Continuing in the same teshuvah, Rabbi Yehudah Aryeh defends taking liturgical melodies heard in non-Jewish religious settings and adapting them for the bais hakenesses, citing his own pre-Yamim Nora’im attendance at Venetian church services. The passage is the central historical text invoked when later poskim debate the use of non-Jewish-origin melodies in tefillah; the Bach has a separate discussion from a different angle. ↩
  19. Mishnah Sukkah 3:11 (daf 38a; cf. Sukkah 53) — “Makom she-nahagu likhpol — yikhpol.” — The Mishnah on the minhagei Hallel records that doubled pesukim in Hallel — “Ana Hashem hoshia na, Ana Hashem hatzlicha na,” and the closing pesukim — are minhag nevi’im. Because the structure of Hallel contains its own internal repetition lechatchilah, poskim permit further repetition for the niggun, particularly in chatzi Hallel on Rosh Chodesh where the hefsek concern is reduced. ↩
  20. Beis Yosef and Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chaim siman 61 — main halachic position against the Bach. — Rabbi Yosef Karo (1488–1575, Tzfas), in the Beis Yosef on the Tur and in his own Shulchan Aruch, rules with the Ritva: the issur of word-repetition is not limited to “Modim” and “Shema.” This is the position adopted by the overwhelming majority of later poskim — Maharam Shik, Machaneh Chaim, Rav Moshe — against the Bach’s narrow reading and against the Venetian musicological heter. ↩
  21. Eleh Divrei HaBris (Altona / Hamburg, 1819) — the rabbinic response to the Hamburg Reform Temple. — The best-known nineteenth-century cherem-collection against Reform liturgical innovations, including the change of the drashah language, the introduction of vernacular tefillah, and organ accompaniment. Assembled by the rabbinical courts of Hamburg-Altona-Wandsbeck in the wake of the establishment of the Hamburg Reform Temple. This is the actual documentary anchor for the tradition of resistance to vernacular drashos that persists in some kehilos to the present. Popular oral traditions often re-date and misattribute it; the reader should consult the printed Eleh Divrei HaBris. ↩
  22. The Haftorah-at-two-and-a-half account — Chayei Yehudah, autobiographical prologue. — The account of Modena as a small child reciting an entire Haftorah in shul is preserved in his own memoir Chayei Yehudah. As a self-reported childhood memory it is a memoir claim and not an independently verifiable historical datum; it is nevertheless a striking piece of testimony and consonant with the reputation for prodigious ability that his career subsequently bore out. The standard modern edition is Daniel Carpi’s (Tel Aviv, 1985). ↩
  23. Aruch HaShulchan, Orach Chaim siman 338 (Hilchos Shabbos) — chazan’s tuning fork; parenthetical lament about word-repetition. — Rabbi Yechiel Michel Epstein (1829–1908, Novardok), the Aruch HaShulchan, treats the chazan’s tuning fork as kli l’genai on Shabbos but rules that one should not protest its use, since the kehilla is invested in its chazan. In a parenthetical aside in the same siman he extends the same realism to word-repetition: those with yiras shamayim suffer when they hear it, but “einan shomim b’kol divrei chachamim,” and he concludes with a faint invocation of the Venetian heter and the meta-klal that one does not raise a great commotion against an entrenched practice. ↩
