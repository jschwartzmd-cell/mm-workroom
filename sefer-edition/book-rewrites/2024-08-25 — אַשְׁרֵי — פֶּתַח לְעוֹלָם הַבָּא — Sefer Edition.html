אַשְׁרֵי — פֶּתַח לְעוֹלָם הַבָּא

Ashrei: A Gateway to Olam HaBa
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Sunday Morning Series on Tefillah
Recorded 25 August 2024  ·  Sefer Edition · Revised 23 August 2026

Sponsored by Michael Shema, l’iluy nishmas his wife’s grandmother, Frumid bas Shlomo, on her yahrtzeit.
Delivered on the yahrtzeit of Rabbeinu Chaim HaLevi Soloveitchik — Reb Chaim Brisker — who reshaped the very landscape of Torah havanah and whose method of prayer, no less than his method of learning, was the fruit of a mind that refused to say a word it did not understand.

I. The Gemara’s Promise

The Talmud in Berachos[1] issues a promise whose plain reading is astonishing:

כָּל הָאוֹמֵר תְּהִלָּה לְדָוִד בְּכָל יוֹם שָׁלֹשׁ פְּעָמִים — מֻבְטָח לוֹ שֶׁהוּא בֶּן עוֹלָם הַבָּא.
Whoever recites Tehillah l’David three times each day is assured that he is a ben olam ha’ba.
Berachos 4b

The sugya identifies two features of this single kapitel that together generate the promise. The first is its aleph-beis acrostic structure, one pasuk per letter (with the nun deliberately omitted, of which more below). The second is the presence of the verse פּוֹתֵחַ אֶת יָדֶךָ וּמַשְׂבִּיעַ לְכָל חַי רָצוֹן[2] — the foundational declaration that HaKadosh Baruch Hu sustains every living creature, moment by moment, from His hand. Other chapters of Tehillim contain one or the other of these features; only Ashrei joins both. The confluence is what earns the promise.

Cheilek and Ben Olam HaBa

The formulation demands care. The Mishnah in Perek Cheilek[3] teaches that every Jew possesses a cheilek in Olam HaBa. If the entire nation already stands on that ground, in what does the Gemara’s promise consist? The Rishonim resolve the tension by drawing a distinction between two levels of standing. The basic cheilek is the inheritance of every Jew from birth, but it is not indestructible; aveiros can erode it, and grave enough transgression can forfeit it entirely. The status of ben olam ha’ba is the higher tier: a portion secured against erosion, a station in Olam HaBa that the ordinary attritions of this world cannot dislodge.

The mechanism by which Ashrei in particular confers this security is not arbitrary. The pasuk at the heart of the Gemara’s promise — Posei’ach es yadecha — is nothing other than the pure statement of emunah b’hashgachah: that every mouthful of bread, every breath of air, every unit of sustenance drawn by any living thing, is being handed to it by the Creator in the present instant. This is the root midah that all aveirah presupposes the absence of. To sin is to act as though one’s life belonged to oneself; to internalize Posei’ach es yadecha, three times each day, is to reorient the nefesh around the daily truth that it does not. The armor is not magical; it is dispositional. A soul steeped in hashgachah from the moment it opens its eyes has already answered, in advance, the interior claim on which sin depends.

Kavvanah Is Constitutive

Precisely because this is the mechanism, the promise does not attach to rote recitation. The Radak and Rabbeinu Bachye on Tehillim 145[4] both emphasize that the Gemara’s guarantee turns on kavvanah — on actually hearing the words as one says them. Absent kavvanah, the ritual is discharged but the reorientation does not occur, and the armor is not formed.

The Mishnah Berurah[5] rules accordingly, and with unusual severity. If one realizes in the middle of Ashrei that he said Posei’ach es yadecha without kavvanah, he must return to that verse and complete the mizmor with proper intent. Even if the recognition comes only after davening — walking out of shul and only then registering that the recitation was hollow — the ruling stands: return to Posei’ach es yadecha, and complete the mizmor from there.

The severity is illuminating. Missing kavvanah in the ordinary run of Pesukei d’Zimra does not trigger any chazarah; the return here is unusual. It suggests that Posei’ach es yadecha is not merely a pasuk of praise but a discrete kiyum embedded within Ashrei — effectively a daily kabbalas ol malchus shamayim pitched at the level of hashgachah, standing to the ordinary emunah of Shema as the specific rests upon the general. Its integrity as a kiyum is what the Mishnah Berurah’s ruling is guarding.

To assist in this kavvanah, many Sephardim physically open their hands upon reciting Posei’ach es yadecha, forming a kli kibbul for the shefa that the verse describes.

II. Why Three Times Each Day

Rashi on the Gemara[6] explains the count of three: k’neged shalosh tefillos, one recitation corresponding to each of the day’s three prayers. This immediately raises a puzzle from settled practice. In the received nusach, Ashrei is said twice in Shacharis — once in Pesukei d’Zimra, once before U’va l’Tziyon — and once in Minchah, but never in Maariv. If the count is one per tefillah, why the surplus in Shacharis and the omission in Maariv?

The Rishonim answer from the historical circumstance of Maariv itself.[7] In the era of Chazal, Tefillas Maariv was reshus — optional — and many did not daven it at all. Shuls stood typically outside the city walls, travel after dark was unsafe, and the practical result was that a Jew who counted on the Maariv-Ashrei for his third recitation would often fail to attain it. To ensure that no one would forfeit the merit, Chazal moved the third recitation forward into Shacharis. In the fullness of time the community accepted Maariv as chovah, but the earlier arrangement of the Ashreis had by then settled into minhag Yisrael and was preserved.

Whether Three Is the Actual Requirement

Not every authority reads the Gemara’s count strictly. Rabbi Akiva Eiger in his Gilyon HaShas[8] preserves an alternative girsa of the sugya that speaks of a single daily recitation, and cites the Rosh as aligning with that reading. Rav Amram Gaon takes a middle position: three are required not because three are strictly necessary, but as a margin ensuring that at least one is reached with true kavvanah. The nafka minah is not trivial. If three are a margin, a Jew confident of hitting one with kavvanah has already satisfied the underlying obligation; if three are strictly required, each carries independent weight. Practical halachah follows the plain reading of the Gemara and the Zohar HaKadosh: three recitations. The operative rule is that not one is to be skipped — even one who cannot reach Minchah with a minyan is to daven Ashrei alone rather than let the third go.

III. The Structure of the Kapitel

The Two Prepended Pesukim

The chapter formally titled in Tehillim is Tehillah l’David, which opens with Aromimcha Elokai HaMelech. The siddur, however, prepends two verses drawn from elsewhere. Their placement is neither accidental nor merely aesthetic.

The first prepended verse — אַשְׁרֵי יוֹשְׁבֵי בֵיתֶךָ, עוֹד יְהַלְלוּךָ סֶּלָה, from Tehillim 84:5[9] — teaches, in miniature, the structural theory of Pesukei d’Zimra itself. The Gemara in Berachos[10] records that the Chassidim HaRishonim would shohin sha’ah achas — wait a full hour before davening — to compose their minds for the encounter. The entire institution of Pesukei d’Zimra is Chazal’s adaptation of that practice for Klal Yisrael at large: a structured span of preparation preceding the Shemoneh Esrei. It is therefore precisely fitting that the central mizmor of that section should open with the pasuk that teaches preparation. Ashrei yoshvei veisecha comes first: happy are those who settle themselves in Your house. Od y’hallelucha selah follows: only then the praise. The verse enacts what the whole section is for.

The second prepended verse — אַשְׁרֵי הָעָם שֶׁכָּכָה לּוֹ, אַשְׁרֵי הָעָם שֶׁה' אֱלֹקָיו, the closing pasuk of Tehillim 144 — carries a further remez. The verse contains the word Ashrei twice; joined to Ashrei yoshvei, it yields three occurrences of Ashrei at the opening of the recitation — a subtle allusion to the three daily recitations that the Gemara’s promise concerns.

The Maharshal[11] stands alone among the Acharonim in holding that Ashrei ha’am she’kachah lo should be omitted, with the recitation beginning directly from Ashrei yoshvei into Tehillah l’David. No printed siddur follows him; his position is preserved as an alternative nusach in the historical record, without practical consequence for the received order.

The Missing Nun and the Samech That Answers It

The Gemara teaches that David HaMelech, by ruach ha’kodesh, omitted the letter nun from the acrostic of Tehillah l’David. The reason lies in the letter’s association with nefilah — falling — and specifically with the verse in Amos:[12]

נָפְלָה לֹא־תוֹסִיף קוּם בְּתוּלַת יִשְׂרָאֵל.
She has fallen and shall rise no more — the maiden of Israel.
Amos 5:2

David HaMelech did not wish that any Jew reciting Ashrei should be met with that grim image of unrecoverable fall. The mefarshim observe that the verse itself admits a softer punctuation — naflah, lo sosif kum can be read across the pause — but the optical impression of permanent collapse remained, and David HaMelech refused to make it part of the daily tefillah of his people.

What lifts this from grammatical curiosity into theology is what stands in the nun’s place. The very next letter, the samech, does not evade nefilah; it addresses it:[13]

סוֹמֵךְ ה' לְכָל הַנֹּפְלִים, וְזוֹקֵף לְכָל הַכְּפוּפִים.
Hashem upholds all who fall, and raises all who are bowed down.
Tehillim 145:14

The theme that the nun would not name is now named — but reframed. Falls happen; and Hashem catches. The absence has become a statement. The acrostic’s omission and its neighbor’s answer, taken together, teach the shape of Jewish life: nefilah is real, and it is never final.

The Concluding Pasuk: A Petition for Olam HaBa

After Tehillah l’David closes on the letter tav, the received text appends a further verse:[14]

וַאֲנַחְנוּ נְבָרֵךְ יָ-הּ מֵעַתָּה וְעַד עוֹלָם הַלְלוּיָ-הּ.
And we shall bless Hashem from now and forever — Halleluyah.
Tehillim 115:18

The Rishonim read this appendage as a petition of remarkable delicacy. Having just recited the tefillah that earns one ben olam ha’ba, the worshiper immediately turns to HaKadosh Baruch Hu and asks that the praise should not cease: let us continue to bless You from now and forever — even in Olam HaBa. The desire itself is part of the merit. A soul that loves the act of davening so deeply that it asks to daven forever has already begun to be at home in the World to Come. Every morning, in this closing verse, we are saying not only that we want Olam HaBa but that we want to keep praising in it.

This is the reverse-side of a recurring theme in Jewish prayer: that we thank HaKadosh Baruch Hu not only for what He gives but for the capacity to thank Him at all. Not every human being is granted the interior lucidity to recognize his Creator and to speak to Him. The zechus of standing in tefillah is itself a gift; the closing verse of Ashrei is, in part, that recognition, offered in advance for eternity.

IV. The Trailing Halleluyah: Rav Amram Gaon’s Chain

The closing word of the appended verse — Halleluyah — is not incidental. Rav Amram Gaon, in his siddur, explains its function. Each of the six chapters of Halleluyos that follow Ashrei in Pesukei d’Zimra both opens and closes with Halleluyah. By ending Ashrei with the same word, Chazal forged a shalsheles — a chain — that joins Ashrei seamlessly to the sequence of Halleluyos that carry Pesukei d’Zimra to its climax.

A Chasidishe Practice

Certain Chasidic communities — among them Satmar and Vizhnitz — preserve a practice that makes the shalsheles audible. At the end of Ashrei, after v’ad olam, they recite Halleluyah, Halleluyah — the closing Halleluyah of Ashrei joined immediately, without pause, to the opening Halleluyah of the following kapitel. The Vizhnitz siddur prints the two words together on the page to signal the joining.

The Objection of Rav Shlomo Kluger

Rav Shlomo Kluger, in his peirush on Orach Chaim,[15] objects to the chained recitation on nigleh grounds. There is a klal, he writes, that any pasuk Moshe Rabbeinu did not say, we do not say — that is, we do not manufacture new pesukim by stitching fragments together. V’anachnu nevareich Kah me’atah v’ad olam closes one pasuk; Halleluyah, Hallelu Kel b’kodsho opens another. Recited back-to-back, Rav Shlomo Kluger objects, the two Halleluyahs compose a phrase that appears nowhere in Tanach.

The klal is not absolute, and its scope is worth naming. Every kri’as haTorah break, every piyyut composed from scriptural fragments, every yehi ratzon stitched from partial verses, is a case of joining Scripture without generating a formal pasuk. The strong reading of Rav Shlomo Kluger’s klal restricts it to the composition of a full pasuk-shaped unit that never existed in the text; the softer reading extends it to any liturgical stitching that resembles a pasuk on the ear. The Chasidic practice presupposes the strong reading; Rav Shlomo Kluger’s objection presupposes the soft one.

The Kabbalists, following the Arizal, preserve the chained recitation on sod grounds: the shalsheles is a tikkun whose necessity does not depend on the nigleh question. Communities follow their mesorah. What is worth carrying away is that a small liturgical detail — whether one pause before beginning Hallelu Kel b’kodsho — sits at the intersection of a serious nigleh principle and a serious nistar tradition, both of which the poskim weighed.

V. Repeating Verses to Mark Endings

The Tur’s Sephardic Minhag

The Tur in Orach Chaim[16] records a striking minhag from the Sephardic communities of his era: at the end of every discrete section of tefillah, the final pasuk was recited twice. The repetition served as a structural marker — the auditory equivalent of a section break — announcing to worshiper and congregation alike that a distinct unit had concluded.

The Sephardic communities of the Tur’s time no longer preserve the practice universally, but two vestiges of it survive even in Nusach Ashkenaz. The first is the doubling of Kol ha’neshamah tehallel Kah at the very end of Pesukei d’Zimra, marking the close of that section. (The core of Pesukei d’Zimra is the six chapters of Halleluyos that end the Tehillim; Vayivareich David and Shiras HaYam, though they stand between Baruch She’amar and Yishtabach, are not formally part of it.) The second is the doubling of Hashem yimloch l’olam va’ed at the end of Shiras HaYam, marking the close of the shirah.

VI. The Shiras HaYam Question

The repetition of Hashem yimloch l’olam va’ed at the end of Shiras HaYam generates a striking stirah between the siddur and the Sefer Torah.

The Contradiction

If the repetition marks the close of the shirah, then the next verse — כִּי בָא סוּס פַּרְעֹה בְּרִכְבּוֹ וּבְפָרָשָׁיו בַּיָּם[17] — is not part of the shirah at all; it is a narrative epilogue, explaining why the shirah was sung. This is precisely how the Ramban[18] reads it: Ki va sus Paroh stands outside the shirah, a bridge back into the flow of narrative.

In the Sefer Torah, however, the picture is otherwise. The shirah is written in the unique brick-laying format known as ariach al gabei l’veinah, and Ki va sus Paroh is written in that same format — visually contained within the shirah. The Rambam in Hilchos Sefer Torah, citing Maseches Sofrim,[19] codifies this arrangement. The result is that the siddur and the Sefer Torah reflect two distinct mesoros: the siddur follows the Ramban’s reading and closes the shirah before Ki va sus Paroh; the Sefer Torah follows the Rambam’s and includes it within.

The Story of the Maharil Diskin

This stirah stands at the center of one of the most sobering episodes in the annals of Brisk. The story is preserved in the biography HaSaraf MiBrisk[20] — a work whose accuracy is attested by the review of the Brisker Rav himself.

When Rav Yehoshua Leib Diskin — the Maharil Diskin — assumed the rabbinate of Brisk in the middle 1870s, he was not a newcomer to the rabbinate; he had already served in Lomza, Mezritch, and Shklov, and stood among the foremost talmidei chachamim of the generation. Yet Brisk was Brisk, and the elder scholars of the city did not all welcome his appointment. Among them was a particularly senior talmid chacham who resolved to test the new Rav.

He came before him with the very stirah just laid out and demanded resolution: how could the siddur say one thing and the Sefer Torah another? The Maharil Diskin explained calmly — the machlokes of the Ramban and the Rambam, the siddur following the one, the Sefer Torah the other. The scholar was not satisfied. On the strength of his own kasha, he resolved to bring the sifrei Torah of Brisk into alignment with the siddur: he would go from shul to shul, direct the sofrim to alter the formatting of Ki va sus Paroh, and correct what he now regarded as an error in every Sefer Torah in the city.

The Maharil Diskin was aghast. To alter a mesorah that all of Klal Yisrael had held for generations, on the strength of one man’s kasha — the very thought was appalling. Yet the scholar was undeterred, partly by conviction and partly by a determination to demonstrate the new Rav’s powerlessness. He began his rounds. The sofrim, intimidated by his seniority, did not refuse. The Maharil Diskin pursued him from shul to shul, pleading.

Erev Shabbos at the Mikveh

That very week, on Erev Shabbos, the man went down to the mikveh. He did not come up. He drowned in the water. HaSaraf MiBrisk preserves the account, and the reading of the event that circulated at the time and has been transmitted since is a reading of midah k’neged midah. The man had sought to alter the very verse that describes the water in which the Egyptians drowned. The water — the mikveh’s waters, mundane and daily — overcame him.

The lesson has never been softened. To tamper with the received mesorah, even one letter, even with a kasha that stands up on its own svara, is a grave matter. The siddur we daven from and the Sefer Torah we read from were transmitted to us as they are; the seeming contradiction between them is not an error to correct but the trace of an ancient machlokes, held and lived by Klal Yisrael across the centuries. There is a line, of course, between legitimate scholarly correction of a genuine scribal error — the labor of the Minchas Shai, for instance, comparing manuscripts to establish the accurate text — and the intrusion the story condemns. The line runs through the difference between correcting toward the received mesorah and correcting away from it in the name of one’s own reasoning. The scholar of Brisk crossed it, and the story preserves what the tradition made of the crossing.

VII. Closing

Ashrei stands at the heart of Pesukei d’Zimra: a kapitel whose acrostic form was designed to be memorized, framed by prepended pesukim that teach the very theory of preparation, whose missing nun becomes a theological statement when the samech that follows names the nefilah the nun would not, and whose closing verse quietly petitions that this same praise continue in Olam HaBa. Around it cluster the finer structural questions of tefillah — the shalsheles of Halleluyahs, the vestigial repetitions of the Sephardic minhag, the contested pasuk at the boundary of the shirah.

Behind all of this stands the lesson of the Maharil Diskin. Every word of the tefillah, every formatting decision of the siddur and the sefer Torah, sits inside a fabric of mesorah that is approached with reverence. We understand what we can; we bear with what we cannot; we do not repair. This is the disposition that Reb Chaim Brisker, on whose yahrtzeit the shiur was delivered, brought to every word of the Gemara and to every word of tefillah: a refusal to say what he did not understand, joined to a still deeper refusal to alter what he had received. The Ashrei-sayer who, three times each day, hears the words Posei’ach es yadecha and lets them reshape his nefesh, is walking that same road — the daily labor of understanding, the daily surrender to the received, and the promised end that the Gemara announces at the outset: muvtach lo she’hu ben Olam HaBa.

The Kabbalists, following the Arizal, preserve the chained recitation on sod grounds. ↩
  16. Tur, Orach Chaim 51. — The Sephardic minhag of repeating the closing pasuk of each discrete section of tefillah as a structural marker. Two vestiges survive in Nusach Ashkenaz: the doubled Kol ha’neshamah tehallel Kah closing Pesukei d’Zimra, and the doubled Hashem yimloch l’olam va’ed closing Shiras HaYam. ↩
  17. Shemos 15:19 — כִּי בָא סוּס פַּרְעֹה בְּרִכְבּוֹ וּבְפָרָשָׁיו בַּיָּם. — The verse at the boundary of Shiras HaYam: narrative epilogue (per Ramban) or part of the shirah (per Rambam / Maseches Sofrim). The text on which the machlokes hinges, and which underlies the stirah between siddur and Sefer Torah. ↩
  18. Ramban on Shemos 15:19. — Reads Ki va sus Paroh as a narrative epilogue: after the shirah concluded, the Torah returns to explain why the shirah was sung. The basis for the siddur’s repetition of Hashem yimloch l’olam va’ed as the formal close of the shirah. ↩
  19. Rambam, Hilchos Sefer Torah 8:4; Maseches Sofrim 12:10. — Codifies that Ki va sus Paroh is written in the brick-laying format (ariach al gabei l’veinah) of the shirah, and is therefore part of the shirah proper. The stirah: the siddur follows the Ramban’s reading; the Sefer Torah follows the Rambam’s. Two valid mesoros co-existing in a single daily avodah. ↩
  20. HaSaraf MiBrisk — biographical account of R’ Yehoshua Leib Diskin (the Maharil Diskin). — The story of the senior talmid chacham who attempted, on the strength of his own kasha, to alter the sifrei Torah of Brisk, and of his death midah k’neged midah in the mikveh, is preserved in this biography. R’ Yehoshua Leib Diskin (1818–1898) served in Lomza, Mezritch, and Shklov before assuming the Brisker rabbinate in the middle 1870s; he was already an established Rav of the previous generation when the events of the story unfolded, which sharpens rather than softens the reading of the elder Brisker scholar’s challenge as an act directed against the local authority of a Rav newly arrived from elsewhere. Precise chapter and page vary by edition. ↩
