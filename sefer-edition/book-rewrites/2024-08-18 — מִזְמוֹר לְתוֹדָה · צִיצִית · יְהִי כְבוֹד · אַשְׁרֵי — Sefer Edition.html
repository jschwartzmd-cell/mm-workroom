Mizmor L’Todah, Holding the Tzitzis, Yehi Ch’vod, and Ashrei
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Sunday Morning Series on Tefillah
Recorded 18 August 2024  ·  Sefer Edition · Revised 23 August 2026

L’iluy nishmas Esther bas Ra’, on her yahrtzeit — sponsored by her son Boruch Mandel. May this discussion of the korban todah that will never be nullified, and of the daily tefillah in which hakaras hatov ascends before the Throne of Glory, stand as an iluy for her neshamah.

I. Mizmor L’Todah — The Song of the Eternal Korban

The Mechaber’s Uncharacteristic Citation

The Shulchan Aruch (שֻׁלְחָן עָרוּךְ)[1] rules that Mizmor L’Todah (מִזְמוֹר לְתוֹדָה) is to be recited b’zimrah, b’ne’imah, u’v’niggun (בְּזִמְרָה בִּנְעִימָה וּבְנִיגּוּן) — with song, sweet melody, and tune. The Mechaber (מְחַבֵּר) departs here from his customary style and cites the underlying reason from a midrash (מִדְרָשׁ):[2] in the age to come, all tefillos (תְּפִלּוֹת) and all korbanos (קָרְבָּנוֹת) will be nullified — except for the korban todah (קָרְבַּן תּוֹדָה), which alone will endure.

The statement is jarring on its face. Klal Yisrael (כְּלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל) prays daily for the return of the avodah (עֲבוֹדָה) and the restoration of the korbanos; what can it mean that they will be nullified? The Rishonim (רִאשׁוֹנִים) resolve the difficulty: the midrash is speaking of chatas (חַטָּאת) and asham (אָשָׁם) — the sin-offerings. In the era of Mashiach (מָשִׁיחַ), when the yetzer for sin will be uprooted, the offerings that atone for sin will become unnecessary. The korban todah, however, is anchored not in transgression but in hakaras hatov (הַכָּרַת הַטּוֹב); and gratitude is eternal.

The reading is not without its complications. The Rambam (רַמְבַּ״ם)’s treatment of the messianic era in Hilchos Melachim (הִלְכוֹת מְלָכִים)[3] envisions the full restoration of the korban (קָרְבָּן) order in the Beis HaMikdash (בֵּית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ), and some Acharonim (אַחֲרוֹנִים) accordingly narrow the midrash’s scope even further — to a preliminary stage of the redemption, or to a homiletic register regarding sin-offerings in particular. Whichever resolution one adopts, the central point stands firm: the song of todah (תּוֹדָה) outlives every occasion of its offering, because the impulse it embodies is the one impulse that no future perfection removes. It is for this reason that Mizmor L’Todah alone, among the mizmorim (מִזְמוֹרִים) of Pesukei d’Zimra (פְּסוּקֵי דְּזִמְרָא), receives the Mechaber’s direction to be sung.

The Attribution to Moshe Rabbeinu

A tradition preserved in Siddur Rashi (סִדּוּר רַשִׁ״י)[4] attributes the composition of Mizmor L’Todah to Moshe Rabbeinu (מֹשֶׁה רַבֵּנוּ). Not every kapitel (קַאפִּיטְל) of Tehillim (תְּהִלִּים) is of Davidic authorship; the Gemara (גְּמָרָא) in Bava Basra (בָּבָא בַּתְרָא) lists ten elders whose compositions David HaMelech (דָּוִד הַמֶּלֶךְ) gathered and incorporated into the sefer (סֵפֶר), and Moshe is among them. On Rashi (רַשִׁ״י)’s reading, Mizmor L’Todah was Moshe’s composition for the korban todah as brought in the Mishkan (מִשְׁכָּן), later set into Tehillim by David HaMelech.

The attribution is not universally received. The Ibn Ezra (אִבְּן עֶזְרָא) and the Radak (רָדָ״ק), working from features internal to the mizmor (מִזְמוֹר), tend to read Mizmor L’Todah as Davidic in origin, composed for the todah offerings brought in the shadow of the Mishkan David (מִשְׁכַּן דָּוִד) and in anticipation of the Beis HaMikdash. The Siddur Rashi’s tradition is not thereby refuted: on the traditional reading, ancient compositions were often preserved through generations of oral transmission before David HaMelech gave them their fixed place. What the divergence does establish is that the question of authorship is itself a machlokes (מַחֲלוֹקֶת), and that one who accepts the Mosaic attribution should recognize that the mizmor he says each morning may carry a voice older than Malchus Beis David (מַלְכוּת בֵּית דָּוִד).

Placement in Pesukei d’Zimra

In Nusach Ashkenaz (נֻסַּח אַשְׁכְּנַז), the recitation of Hodu (הוֹדוּ) concludes with the personal declaration “Ashirah la’Hashem ki gamal alai” (אָשִׁירָה לַה׳ כִּי גָמַל עָלָי) and passes directly into Mizmor L’Todah — a personal thanksgiving flowing naturally into the formal song of gratitude. In Nusach Sefard (נֻסַּח סְפָרַד) and Nusach Ari (נֻסַּח אֲרִ״י), the surrounding order differs, but the internal logic remains: Mizmor L’Todah stands as the fixed point around which the surrounding tefillos are arranged.

Standing and Sitting

Among the siddurim (סִדּוּרִים), a machlokes is preserved as to whether one stands or sits for Mizmor L’Todah. Many record minhag ha’olam (מִנְהַג הָעוֹלָם) to stand, with the qualification that local custom governs. In practical terms, those who follow Nusach Sefard and stand for Baruch She’amar (בָּרוּךְ שֶׁאָמַר) typically remain standing through Mizmor L’Todah. Nusach Ari reflects its own framework, and certain sources within it direct that one be seated notwithstanding the surrounding minhag (מִנְהָג).

II. Holding the Tzitzis at Baruch She’amar — A Universal Minhag Under Question

The Minhag as Practiced

Throughout Klal Yisrael (כְּלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל), when one reaches Baruch She’amar (בָּרוּךְ שֶׁאָמַר), one takes the two front tzitzis (צִיצִית) of the tallis gadol (טַלִּית גָּדוֹל) in hand, holds them through the brachah (בְּרָכָה), kisses them at “malchus u’memshalah,” (מַלְכוּת וּמֶמְשָׁלָה) and lays them down; at Kriyas Shema (קְרִיאַת שְׁמַע), one gathers all four. The minhag (מִנְהָג) is learned in childhood and observed for a lifetime, usually without any account of its rationale ever being given.

The Magen Avraham and the Kabbalistic Root

The Magen Avraham (מָגֵן אַבְרָהָם)[5] records the minhag and cites its source as ha’kesavim (הַכְּתָבִים) — the term by which the Acharonim (אַחֲרוֹנִים) customarily refer, when unattributed, to the Kisvei HaArizal (כִּתְבֵי הָאֲרִ״י זַ״ל) as transmitted by Rav Chaim Vital (רַב חַיִּים וִיטַאל).[6] The framework of the minhag is thus kabbalistic in origin: an unfolding of oros (אוֹרוֹת) and partzufim (פַּרְצוּפִים) in which the encompassing light draws forward in stages, corresponding to the successive gatherings of the tzitzis. The minhag as practiced by the great mass of Yidden (יִידִּן) follows the Magen Avraham’s codification without engaging the underlying sod (סוֹד).

Rimzei Elul — A Nineteenth-Century Search for Reasons

An obscure nineteenth-century work called Rimzei Elul (רִמְזֵי אֱלוּל)[7] preserves an instructive testimony. Its author records that he canvassed the gedolim (גְּדוֹלִים), tzaddikim (צַדִּיקִים), and rabbonim (רַבָּנִים) of his generation for a satisfying reason for the tzitzis-holding at Baruch She’amar, and that none could supply one. He proceeds to construct his own remazim (רְמָזִים), openly noting that they are his own composition rather than received mesorah (מָסוֹרָה). That a widely observed minhag could, within living memory of its universal adoption, be opaque to its own most learned practitioners is itself a datum worth noting: it establishes that the halakhic question the minhag raises is not the projection of a modern skepticism but an ancient perplexity.

The Aderes’s Open Dissent

Among the greatest of the late-nineteenth-century Litvishe gedolim, Rav Eliyahu Dovid Rabinovitz-Teumim — the Aderes (אַדֶּרֶת)[8] — goes further than perplexity. He writes plainly: “I have no idea what kissing and holding the front tzitzis has to do with Baruch She’amar, and therefore I do not do it.” The Aderes served successively as Rav of the town of Mir and of Ponevezh, was called late in life to Yerushalayim (יְרוּשָׁלַיִם) by Rav Shmuel Salant to assist in leading the growing kehillah (קְהִלָּה), and lived his final years in an apartment directly opposite Har HaBayis (הַר הַבַּיִת), where every morning he would rise, recite Modeh Ani (מוֹדֶה אֲנִי), look out at the Makom HaMikdash (מְקוֹם הַמִּקְדָּשׁ), and weep over the galus haShechinah (גָּלוּת הַשְּׁכִינָה). His autobiography records that he felt the daily anguish shortening his life; he was niftar (נִפְטַר) four years after his arrival, predeceasing Rav Shmuel Salant. Of the more than one hundred and twenty sefarim (סְפָרִים) he composed, only two were published in his lifetime; manuscripts continue to be edited and released to this day.[9]

The Vilna Gaon and the Concern of Hefsek

The Aderes further reports that the Vilna Gaon (הַגְּרָ״א מִוִּילְנָא) also refrained from handling the tzitzis — neither at Baruch She’amar nor at Kriyas Shema.[10] The Gaon’s halakhic ground is structural. Baruch She’amar opens a brachah-frame that closes only at Yishtabach (יִשְׁתַּבַּח); the entire span of Pesukei d’Zimra (פְּסוּקֵי דְּזִמְרָא) is a single liturgical unit bracketed by that opening brachah and its concluding counterpart. Handling and kissing the tzitzis in the midst of the opening brachah constitutes, on the Gaon’s reading, a hefsek (הֶפְסֵק) — an interruption between the brachah and the mitzvah (מִצְוָה) of the tefillah (תְּפִלָּה) that it introduces. The same principle governs his position on kissing the tefillin (תְּפִלִּין) at Shema (שְׁמַע) and kissing the mezuzah (מְזוּזָה) in passing — practices concerning which the Geonim (גְּאוֹנִים) had already raised the pointed question: “Why does one run to kiss the tefillin at Shema, when no one runs to kiss the mezuzah when it is mentioned?” Communities that follow the Gaon refrain accordingly.

How the Two Positions Cohere: Tzorech HaBrachah versus Tzorech HaTefillah

The apparent stand-off between the Magen Avraham and the Gaon is, on closer inspection, a coherent expression of a single halakhic axis rather than a bare disagreement. The Mishnah Berurah (מִשְׁנָה בְּרוּרָה)[11] discusses at length which interruptions are permitted within the Baruch She’amar–Yishtabach frame. Responding amen (אָמֵן) to a brachah, joining in kedushah (קְדֻשָּׁה), barchu (בָּרְכוּ), and kaddish (קַדִּישׁ) — all of these are permitted as tzorech ha’tefillah (צֹרֶךְ הַתְּפִלָּה), needs of the tefillah itself that do not rupture the frame but participate in it. The question dividing the Magen Avraham and the Gaon is precisely whether the tzitzis-handling qualifies as such a tzorech (צֹרֶךְ). The Magen Avraham, following the Arizal (אֲרִ״י זַ״ל), treats the handling as tzorech ha’brachah (צֹרֶךְ הַבְּרָכָה): the very content of Baruch She’amar — a brachah in praise of the King who wraps Himself in light — is enhanced by the physical grasping of the garment that draws down the light upon the mispallel (מִתְפַּלֵּל). The Gaon holds the opposite: the handling belongs to no essential tzorech of the brachah, and its introduction fractures the frame. Both positions rest on the same underlying architecture; they differ on the classification of a single act within it.

How the Minhag Spread — The Kitzur Shulchan Aruch

The universality of the minhag in mainstream Ashkenazic kehillos (קְהִלּוֹת) owes more to a single sefer (סֵפֶר) than to any other cause. Rav Shlomo Ganzfried, Rav of Ungvar and author of the Kitzur Shulchan Aruch (קִצּוּר שֻׁלְחָן עָרוּךְ),[12] codified the practice in the mid-nineteenth century. Before the ascent of the Mishnah Berurah, the Kitzur was the most widely used halakhic work in Klal Yisrael: short, lucid, and accessible, it served as the daily halakhic sefer in shuls (שׁוּלְן), and as the central halakhic text taught to children in cheder (חֵדֶר). Through its reach, the tzitzis-handling at Baruch She’amar passed from a Kabbalistic minhag into a universal norm. (Rav Ganzfried also composed Kesef HaSofer (כֶּסֶף הַסּוֹפֵר)[13] on hilchos stam (הִלְכוֹת סְתָ״ם), sealed by the Chasam Sofer (חֲתַם סוֹפֵר)’s declaration that no sofer (סוֹפֵר) who had not learned that work would be relied upon for mezuzos (מְזוּזוֹת) or tefillin.)

Or Makif: The Kabbalistic Frame

Within the sod-layer of tefillah, the mekubalim (מְקֻבָּלִים) distinguish two forms of spiritual light around a person: or pnimi (אוֹר פְּנִימִי), the interior light that fills the vessels of the soul, and or makif (אוֹר מַקִּיף), the encompassing light that surrounds them. Only a few mitzvos (מִצְווֹת) envelop the whole body — sukkah (סֻכָּה), mikveh (מִקְוֶה), the tallis gadol when worn properly, and, on some views, yishuv Eretz Yisrael (יִשּׁוּב אֶרֶץ יִשְׂרָאֵל). The tallis (טַלִּית) uniquely embodies the or makif, and the mekubalim describe the encompassing light as unfolding in stages: at Baruch She’amar, the light “faces forward,” and one accordingly takes the two front tzitzis; by Kriyas Shema, the light has fully enveloped the mispallel, and one gathers all four.

A Pshat-Based Origin

The author of Rimzei Elul proposes, alongside his remazim, a pshat (פְּשָׁט)-based origin that carries an intuitive force even for one not steeped in Kabbalah (קַבָּלָה).

In the era of the Tannaim (תַּנָּאִים), the daily davening (דַּאוונען) opened at Baruch She’amar: the preliminary sections of korbanos (קָרְבָּנוֹת), of Hodu (הוֹדוּ), of Mizmor Shir Chanukas HaBayis (מִזְמוֹר שִׁיר חֲנֻכַּת הַבַּיִת), of Hashem Melech (ה׳ מֶלֶךְ), had not yet entered the liturgy. The Mishnah (מִשְׁנָה) at the opening of Berachos (בְּרָכוֹת) defines the zman (זְמַן) for Kriyas Shema as mi’sheyakir bein techeles l’lavan (מִשֶּׁיַּכִּיר בֵּין תְּכֵלֶת לְלָבָן) — the moment at which one can distinguish the blue thread of techeles (תְּכֵלֶת) in the tzitzis from the white. There were no clocks, no printed zmanim (זְמַנִּים), no gabbai (גַּבַּאי) to announce the moment. As Yidden began Baruch She’amar in the predawn light, they would physically take out their front tzitzis and check — is there sufficient light to distinguish the threads? Has the zman of Kriyas Shema arrived?

The minhag we practice today, on this reading, is the living memory of that ancient practical check. Clocks and printed zmanim have long since displaced the need; the gesture endures. Two features fall neatly out of the reconstruction: it accounts for the timing (why Baruch She’amar specifically, before Shema), and it accounts for the choice of the two front tzitzis (which alone are within convenient view for a check). The pshat explains the holding; the kissing, an act of chibbas ha’mitzvah (חִבַּת הַמִּצְוָה), is more naturally rooted in the kabbalistic frame and in the general instinct to honor a mitzvah as it passes through one’s hands. The two accounts complement rather than contradict one another.

Ten Knots and Ten Ma’amaros

The mekubalim add a further remez (רֶמֶז). When one takes the two front tzitzis together, the visible knots total ten — five on each strand. The ten correspond to the ten berachos (בְּרָכוֹת) that compose Baruch She’amar, which themselves correspond to the Asarah Ma’amaros (עֲשָׂרָה מַאֲמָרוֹת) by which HaKadosh Baruch Hu (הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא) called the world into being. The gesture at the opening of Pesukei d’Zimra is thus a small enactment of the creation the brachah celebrates.

Contemporary Practice

For the great mass of Klal Yisrael, the Magen Avraham’s codification, reinforced by the Kitzur, remains normative. Certain communities within the orbit of the talmidei haGra (תַּלְמִידֵי הַגְּרָ״א) — and individual Yidden who wish to align with the Gaon or with the Aderes — refrain from the handling, or perform it only in an abbreviated form. The two paths are both anchored in received mesorah; a ba’al teshuvah (בַּעַל תְּשׁוּבָה) or a person inheriting an unclear family minhag should consult his own rav (רַב). What the sugya (סוּגְיָא) teaches at a deeper level is that a universally practiced minhag is not the same as a settled halakhic question; the community’s acceptance of a practice does not obviate the seriousness of the concerns of those who abstained.

III. Yehi Ch’vod — The Twenty-One Names

A Composite of Ancient Pedigree

After Baruch She’amar (בָּרוּךְ שֶׁאָמַר), the siddur (סִדּוּר) presents the composition known by its opening words, Yehi Ch’vod Hashem l’olam (יְהִי כְבוֹד ה׳ לְעוֹלָם). It is not a single kapitel (קַאפִּיטְל); it is a deliberate arrangement of pesukim (פְּסוּקִים) drawn from Tehillim (תְּהִלִּים), Mishlei (מִשְׁלֵי), Divrei HaYamim (דִּבְרֵי הַיָּמִים), and possibly Iyov (אִיּוֹב) — a collage assembled by the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah (אַנְשֵׁי כְּנֶסֶת הַגְּדוֹלָה) or by early liturgists to introduce the Ashrei (אַשְׁרֵי) that follows.

The antiquity of the tefillah (תְּפִלָּה) is well attested. It appears in Maseches Sofrim (מַסֶּכֶת סוֹפְרִים)[14] among the masechtos ketanos (מַסֶּכְתּוֹת קְטַנּוֹת) appended to the Talmud, in the Siddur of Rav Amram Gaon (רַב עַמְרָם גָּאוֹן), in the Siddur of Rav Saadia Gaon (רַב סְעַדְיָה גָּאוֹן), and in the Seder HaTefillos (סֵדֶר הַתְּפִלּוֹת) of the Rambam (רַמְבַּ״ם). It is among the oldest fixtures of the daily liturgy.

Twenty-One Names, Twenty-One Pesukim

The Rishonim (רִאשׁוֹנִים) observe that Yehi Ch’vod (יְהִי כְבוֹד) contains twenty-one mentions of the Name of Hashem (הַשֵּׁם), corresponding precisely to the twenty-one pesukim that compose the Ashrei immediately following. The parallel is not coincidental: Yehi Ch’vod is constructed as an introduction to Ashrei, its twenty-one invocations of the Divine Name preparing the tongue and the mind for the twenty-one pesukim of praise that follow.

The Levush’s Counting Difficulty

A careful count of the explicit Names in Yehi Ch’vod, however, yields only nineteen. The Levush (לְבוּשׁ)[15] raises the difficulty and resolves it through two identifications. The first is the Name Kah (י־ה), which the Rema (רְמָ״א)[16] counts as a full Divine Name min ha’Torah (מִן הַתּוֹרָה) — possessed of the full sanctity that forbids its erasure. Counted as such, it constitutes the twentieth Name. The twenty-first is more remarkable still. In the pasuk (פָּסוּק) “Yismechu ha’shamayim v’sageil ha’aretz,” (יִשְׂמְחוּ הַשָּׁמַיִם וְתָגֵל הָאָרֶץ) the closing letters of shamayim (שָׁמַיִם) and the opening letters of v’sageil (וְתָגֵל) form, when read across the word break, the Sheim Havayah (שֵׁם הֲוָיָ״ה) embedded between the words. The Levush counts it toward the total.

The identification carries a broader lesson. Yehi Ch’vod is teaching that the Sheim Kadosh (שֵׁם קָדוֹשׁ) can be present in a text without being visible on its surface — encoded in the seam between one word and the next. It is a formal warrant, within the daily tefillah, for the entire tradition of tzeirufei Shemos (צֵרוּפֵי שֵׁמוֹת) upon which so much of the sod (סוֹד)-layer of tefillah rests. The mechanic is small; the principle it embodies is not.

IV. Ashrei — Acrostic, Memory, and the Nun That Was Omitted

The Gemara’s Promise

The Gemara (גְּמָרָא) in Berachos (בְּרָכוֹת)[17] declares a remarkable promise: “Kol ha’omer Tehillah l’David b’chol yom shalosh pe’amim — muvtach lo she’hu ben olam ha’ba.” (כָּל הָאוֹמֵר תְּהִלָּה לְדָוִד בְּכָל יוֹם שָׁלֹשׁ פְּעָמִים מֻבְטָח לוֹ שֶׁהוּא בֶּן הָעוֹלָם הַבָּא) Whoever recites Ashrei (אַשְׁרֵי) three times daily is guaranteed a portion in the world to come. Such language is rare in the Gemara; the guarantee attached to a single daily practice makes Ashrei among the most theologically weighted of all liturgical texts.

The Gemara anchors the promise in two features that Ashrei combines and that no other kapitel (קַאפִּיטְל) combines. The first is its aleph-beis (אָלֶף־בֵּית) acrostic form, with a pasuk (פָּסוּק) for each letter (the nun (נוּ״ן) excepted, on which more below). The second is the pasuk “Posei’ach es yadecha u’masbi’a l’chol chai ratzon” (פּוֹתֵחַ אֶת יָדֶךָ וּמַשְׂבִּיעַ לְכָל חַי רָצוֹן) — the declaration that HaKadosh Baruch Hu (הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא) opens His hand and satisfies the desire of every living being. Other kapitlach (קַאפִּיטְלַךְ) have one or the other; Ashrei alone has both. On the classical reading, this dual concentration — the acrostic’s comprehensiveness in praise and the pasuk’s acknowledgement of universal Divine hashgachah (הַשְׁגָּחָה) — is what earns the olam ha’ba (עוֹלָם הַבָּא) promise.

The Missing Nun

An acrostic kapitel in which one letter of the aleph-beis is deliberately absent is not a copyist’s lapse. The Gemara itself addresses the omission.[18] Why is there no nun-pasuk? Because the nun would introduce the pasuk from Amos (עָמוֹס): “Naflah lo tosif kum betulas Yisrael” (נָפְלָה לֹא תוֹסִיף קוּם בְּתוּלַת יִשְׂרָאֵל) — the maiden of Israel has fallen; she shall rise no more. David HaMelech (דָּוִד הַמֶּלֶךְ), whose ruach hakodesh (רוּחַ הַקֹּדֶשׁ) foresaw the churban (חֻרְבָּן) and the galus (גָּלוּת), refused to write into his kapitel a pasuk that could be heard as sealing the fall of Klal Yisrael (כְּלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל). In its place, the very next pasuk of Ashrei — the samech (סָמֶ״ךְ)-pasuk — carries the answer: “Somech Hashem l’chol ha’noflim v’zokef l’chol ha’kefufim,” (סוֹמֵךְ ה׳ לְכָל הַנֹּפְלִים וְזוֹקֵף לְכָל הַכְּפוּפִים) Hashem supports all who fall and raises up all who are bent. The acrostic’s omission is not a gap; it is a theological gesture. The nun was suppressed so that the samech could answer it.

The Sefer HaEshkol on Acrostic and Memory

The Sefer HaEshkol (סֵפֶר הָאֶשְׁכּוֹל), of Rav Avraham ben Yitzchak Av Beis Din (אַב בֵּית דִּין) of Provence — the second of three Rishonim (רִאשׁוֹנִים) called Ravad (רָאבַ״ד), not to be confused with the Ravad of Posquières, the ba’al hasagos (בַּעַל הַשָּׂגוֹת) on the Rambam (רַמְבַּ״ם)[19] — offers a further chiddush (חִדּוּשׁ) on the acrostic structure. On this reading, David HaMelech chose the aleph-beis form for Ashrei for a practical reason as well as a poetic one: to render the kapitel memorizable. In an era before printed sefarim (סְפָרִים), when every Yid (יִיד) needed to know Ashrei by heart in order to fulfill the daily obligation, the acrostic served as a mnemonic scaffold, giving the tongue a fixed sequence to trace.

The Eshkol (אֶשְׁכּוֹל) extends the principle to Megillas Eichah (מְגִלַּת אֵיכָה). Yirmiyahu HaNavi (יִרְמְיָהוּ הַנָּבִיא), composing his lament for the churban, chose the aleph-beis structure for the same reason: to enable the entire Klal Yisrael throughout the long galus to know Eichah (אֵיכָה) by heart. As Ashrei needed to be committed to memory so that daily tefillah (תְּפִלָּה) could be performed, Eichah needed to be committed to memory so that the reasons for the galus — and the summons to teshuvah (תְּשׁוּבָה) it contained — would never be lost. The four chapters of Eichah employ the aleph-beis, and chapter three triples it, precisely because the mnemonic function was central to the composition.

The tension with the nun-omission is only apparent. A mnemonic structure and a deliberate gap coexist: the mnemonic carries the tongue through the sequence, and the gap — noticed precisely because the mnemonic makes it noticeable — teaches. The two together give Ashrei its shape.

Memorization as Avodah — The Gaon’s Mastery

The centrality of memory in the older avodas Hashem (עֲבוֹדַת ה׳) can be measured by a single testimony. Rav Zalman of Volozhin (רַב זַלְמַן מִוָּאלוֹזִ׳ין), talmid (תַּלְמִיד) of the Vilna Gaon (הַגְּרָ״א מִוִּילְנָא),[20] is reported to have said of his rebbe (רֶבִּי): “My rebbe knew the entire Torah the way we know Ashrei.” The Gaon could recite the whole of the Torah forward and backward with the fluency with which the ordinary Yid says Ashrei. Try saying Ashrei backward without breaking, and one grasps the order of magnitude.

V. Sefer HaEshkol and the Vetting of Mesorah

The Halberstadt Manuscript

The Eshkol (אֶשְׁכּוֹל)’s chiddush (חִדּוּשׁ) on memorization is precious — and the sefer (סֵפֶר) that carries it is at the center of one of the most consequential authenticity controversies in the halakhic literature of the last two centuries.

In the mid-nineteenth century, a respected rav (רַב) of Halberstadt, in Germany, named Rav Tzvi Binyamin Auerbach, announced that he had located a long-lost manuscript of the original Sefer HaEshkol (סֵפֶר הָאֶשְׁכּוֹל). He published the manuscript in three volumes, adding to the previously known core roughly three-quarters more material that had never appeared in any prior edition. When pressed for the manuscript’s provenance, Rav Auerbach explained that he had copied it from the personal papers of a Jew in his town. Asked to produce the original, he replied that the owner had since been niftar (נִפְטָר); the man’s will had directed that his writings be buried with him, and the family had, by a tragic error, buried this manuscript with the rest. There was no surviving original to consult.

Professor Albeck’s Textual Demonstration

Professor Shalom Albeck — father of Chanoch Albeck, the great editor of the Mishnah (מִשְׁנָה)[21] — undertook a systematic examination of the Halberstadt edition on textual grounds and demonstrated that large portions of the expanded text could not be authentic to Rav Avraham ben Yitzchak of Provence. The method paralleled the Yaavetz (יַעֲבֵ״ץ)’s earlier linguistic critiques of portions of the Zohar (זֹהַר): anachronistic terminology, Hebrew registers inconsistent with twelfth-century Provence, references to authorities post-dating the Ravad (רָאבַ״ד)’s lifetime.

The result is uncomfortable but must be stated plainly. There is an authentic core Eshkol, cited already by the Beis Yosef (בֵּית יוֹסֵף) and the Tur (טוּר) and passed down through the standard chain of Rishonim (רִאשׁוֹנִים); and there is the expanded Halberstadt material, substantial portions of which are almost certainly later composition. The Mishnah Berurah (מִשְׁנָה בְּרוּרָה) quotes from the fuller edition, and any student of the sefer today must approach the material with the awareness that a given passage may or may not derive from the twelfth-century author.

Where the Memorization Chiddush Stands

The chiddush on the mnemonic function of the aleph-beis (אָלֶף־בֵּית) in Ashrei (אַשְׁרֵי) and in Eichah (אֵיכָה) deserves, in candor, the same scrutiny. Whether it appears in the authentic core cited by the Beis Yosef or in the Halberstadt expansion is not always transparent from the popular quotations. The chiddush’s intrinsic force does not depend on its attribution: as a reading of David HaMelech’s and Yirmiyahu’s compositional choices, it stands or falls on its own merits, and it stands. But the honest reader should hold the attribution with a light hand, and should think of the insight as anchored in an Ashkenazic-Provencal reception of Ashrei’s structure, even where the precise voice remains uncertain.

The Yerushalmi on Kodshim and the Broader Phenomenon

The Eshkol is not the singular case. The most striking parallel in modern times is the spurious Yerushalmi on Kodshim (יְרוּשַׁלְמִי עַל קָדָשִׁים)[22] — a fabricated masechta (מַסֶּכְתָּא) composed in Yerushalmi (יְרוּשַׁלְמִי) style on a seder (סֵדֶר) for which no Yerushalmi exists. Its author, a forger of remarkable Talmudic capacity, traveled the great European batei midrash (בָּתֵּי מִדְרָשׁ) presenting his “manuscript” and convinced much of the olam (עוֹלָם). Among the few gedolim (גְּדוֹלִים) who saw through the forgery on stylistic and internal-logical grounds were the Maharsham (מַהַרְשַׁ״ם) and the Rogatchover Gaon (רוֹגַאטְשׁוֹבֶר גָּאוֹן), whose extraordinary sensitivity to Talmudic register and structural logic allowed them to detect what more diffuse readers missed.

The moral of both cases is the same. Even a forger must possess deep talmid chacham (תַּלְמִיד חָכָם) capacity to persuade other talmidei chachamim (תַּלְמִידֵי חֲכָמִים); the fact that a “new” text convinces trained readers is not itself proof of its authenticity. Conversely, the response of the tradition is not to reject old texts because they might be forged, but to test them — against the citation record of the earlier Rishonim, against internal linguistic markers, against the accumulated testimony of the mesorah (מָסוֹרָה). What emerges from the vetting is not credulity and not skepticism, but a disciplined trust: the trust of a community that knows how to distinguish what it has received from what it has been offered.

VI. A Practical Halacha: Coming Late to Davening

A practical halacha (הֲלָכָה) relevant to the entire Baruch She’amar (בָּרוּךְ שֶׁאָמַר)–Yishtabach (יִשְׁתַּבַּח) frame deserves its own place. Where possible, one should arrive on time and recite Pesukei d’Zimra (פְּסוּקֵי דְּזִמְרָא) in full. Where one has arrived late and must align with the tzibbur (צִבּוּר) for Kriyas Shema (קְרִיאַת שְׁמַע) and Shemoneh Esrei (שְׁמוֹנֶה עֶשְׂרֵה), a machlokes (מַחֲלוֹקֶת) among the Rishonim (רִאשׁוֹנִים) governs which portions of Pesukei d’Zimra are essential. Rashi (רַשִׁ״י) holds that the core of Pesukei d’Zimra is the sequence of Halleluyos (הַלְלוּיָה) that closes the section. The Geonim (גְּאוֹנִים) hold that the core is the three fixed points: Baruch She’amar, Ashrei (אַשְׁרֵי), and Yishtabach.[23] The normative psak (פְּסָק) follows the Geonim: in a time-pressed situation, one recites Baruch She’amar — Ashrei — Yishtabach and joins the tzibbur, completing the remaining mizmorim (מִזְמוֹרִים) afterward, without the surrounding brachos (בְּרָכוֹת). The centrality of Ashrei to this abbreviated form of Pesukei d’Zimra is itself a further testimony to the Gemara (גְּמָרָא)’s valuation of the kapitel (קַאפִּיטְל).

VII. Synthesis — The Vetted Mesorah

The layered tefillah (תְּפִלָּה) from Baruch She’amar (בָּרוּךְ שֶׁאָמַר) through Ashrei (אַשְׁרֵי) is a tissue of ancient composition, medieval codification, and Kabbalistic elaboration. A brachah (בְּרָכָה) of remarkable antiquity opens the frame; a mizmor (מִזְמוֹר) whose korban (קָרְבָּן) alone will endure into the y’mos ha’Mashiach (יְמוֹת הַמָּשִׁיחַ) is sung within it; a minhag (מִנְהָג) of tzitzis (צִיצִית)-handling, whose pshat (פְּשָׁט) origin lies in the pre-dawn examination of the threads and whose kabbalistic frame lies in the unfolding of the or makif (אוֹר מַקִּיף), is enacted; an introductory tefillah with twenty-one Names of Hashem (הַשֵּׁם) is recited to prepare the twenty-one pesukim (פְּסוּקִים) of the Ashrei that follows; and the Ashrei itself, promising a portion in olam ha’ba (עוֹלָם הַבָּא), is structured with a deliberate acrostic and a deliberate gap.

Woven through the sugya (סוּגְיָא) is the tradition’s own critical apparatus. The dissents of the Aderes (אַדֶּרֶת) and the Vilna Gaon (הַגְּרָ״א מִוִּילְנָא) from a universal minhag; the Levush (לְבוּשׁ)’s careful arithmetic exposing an embedded Name in the seam between two words; the recognition that portions of a beloved Rishon (רִאשׁוֹן)’s work are almost certainly forged; the exposure of a spurious Yerushalmi (יְרוּשַׁלְמִי) by the extraordinary sensitivity of the Maharsham (מַהַרְשַׁ״ם) and the Rogatchover (רוֹגַאטְשׁוֹבֶר) — all of these operate within the mesorah (מָסוֹרָה), not against it. Mesorah is not credulity; it is vetted faithfulness, the tradition’s own capacity to test what it receives.

The korban todah (קָרְבַּן תּוֹדָה) of the tzaddeikes (צַדֶּקֶת) for whose yahrtzeit (יָארְצַייט) this shiur (שִׁעוּר) was dedicated stands, then, as the true bracket of the whole. All korbanos (קָרְבָּנוֹת) will one day be nullified; hakaras hatov (הַכָּרַת הַטּוֹב) alone will endure. The tefillah of a son on behalf of a mother’s neshamah (נְשָׁמָה) is itself, in the register of the age of exile, a korban todah — a song of gratitude that no future perfection will ever set aside.

Source-type note (forgery): The spurious Yerushalmi on Kodshim — a fabricated masechta in Yerushalmi style on a seder for which no genuine Yerushalmi exists, produced in the late nineteenth / early twentieth century by a forger (Shlomo Yehudah Friedlander) of remarkable Talmudic capacity. Most of the olam was initially convinced; the Maharsham and the Rogatchover Gaon were among those who exposed it on stylistic and internal-logical grounds. Cited here as a parallel case to the Halberstadt Eshkol: forgery in the halakhic literature requires talmid chacham capacity, and mesorah requires vetted faithfulness rather than credulity.
