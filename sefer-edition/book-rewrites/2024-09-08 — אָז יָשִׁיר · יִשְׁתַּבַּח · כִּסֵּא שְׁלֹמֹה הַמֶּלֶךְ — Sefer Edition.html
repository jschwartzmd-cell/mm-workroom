Az Yashir, Yishtabach, and the Throne of Shlomo HaMelech
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Sunday Morning Series on Tefillah
Recorded 8 September 2024  ·  Sefer Edition · Revised 23 August 2026

L’ilui nishmas the Skulener Rebbe zt”l, niftar the evening this teaching was delivered, whose Asher Yatzar of five and six minutes at the wall of his bungalow is itself a commentary on all that follows; and l’ilui nishmas Rav Avraham Loizban zt”l, a great marbitz Torah of his generation, niftar the same week.

I. Two Images of Tefillah

The shiur that follows was delivered on the morning after the Skulener Rebbe was niftar, and it is not possible to enter it without first pausing at his image. The Rebbe was revered across the boundaries that ordinarily divide Klal Yisrael — Litvish, Chassidish, misnagdish, and Sephardic communities alike returned to his person a devotion that transcended their internal disputes. He was among three towering figures of Boro Park niftar in close succession — Rav Moshe Wolfson, the Kossover Rebbe, and now the Skulener Rebbe himself — a triple bereavement whose weight the neighborhood is still absorbing.

Two images from his life bear directly on the theme of the shiur. The first: at a gathering some months earlier for bachurim of Mir Yerushalayim, the Rebbe was brought in attached to oxygen, barely able to muster speech. Skulen is affiliated with Satmar, and when a drashah began in Yiddish, the Rebbe interrupted in a whisper: Speak lashon ha’kodesh so the bachurim understand. He then rose, danced with them, and sent them off with chizzuk. The second: the first-time visitor to the Rebbe’s bungalow would witness him emerge from the beis ha’kisei, turn to the wall, and recite Asher Yatzar for five or six minutes. Asher Yatzar was real to him in a way it is real for very few.

These are not sentimental details. They are the substance of what this shiur is about: tefillah as literal, as weighed, as refused-to-be-rote. When the analysis below traces how the fifteen praises of Yishtabach came to be arranged as they are, or why the Zohar forbids interruption between them, the underlying question is always the same — what would it mean to say these words the way the Skulener Rebbe said Asher Yatzar? May all that follows be l’iluy nishmasam.

II. The Placement of Az Yashir — Halachic Problem and Geonic Practice

A Structural Difficulty

The section of tefillah known as Pesukei d’Zimra (פְּסוּקֵי דְְזִמְרָא) is bracketed by two brachos: Baruch She’amar at the beginning and Yishtabach at the end. Between them the body of the section consists overwhelmingly of chapters from Tehillim. Into this frame there was introduced, at some point in the development of the siddur, the recitation of Az Yashir — the Song at the Sea from Parshas Beshalach. On its face this creates a hefsek: a passage drawn from Chumash is inserted between two brachos whose intervening body is otherwise Ketuvim. The difficulty was felt from the Geonic period forward and is the seed from which nearly every other issue treated in this shiur grows.

The Geonic Reticence

The siddur of Rav Amram Gaon[1] records that the great yeshivos of Sura and Pumbedisa did not recite Az Yashir daily. He adds, with characteristic breadth, that one should not protest those communities that do recite it — the practice is permissible even if not the Babylonian standard. The Rambam,[2] writing in Hilchos Tefillah, offers the sharpest halachic resolution: one wishing to recite Az Yashir should do so after Yishtabach, thereby avoiding the hefsek altogether. The Rambam’s ruling is not a marginal preference; it is the position that best preserves the integrity of the brachah-frame that Tosafos, as will emerge below, identifies as a single unit. That no community today follows the Rambam’s placement, and that the universal practice is to recite Az Yashir before Yishtabach despite his ruling, is itself an early instance of a pattern that will recur throughout this shiur: Kabbalistic reordering displacing the halachic ordering that would otherwise have prevailed.

Ha’azinu as an Alternative

The Rambam also preserves the notice that some communities of his day recited Ha’azinu daily in place of Az Yashir. Both passages are structured as shirah; both share the compositional stichography that marks them apart from ordinary narrative. Some communities even substitute Ha’azinu for Az Yashir on Tisha B’Av, when the tone of triumph proper to Shiras HaYam is felt to be out of place. The variability is instructive: what mattered to the framers of the siddur was that some passage of shirah close Pesukei d’Zimra, not any single one.

III. The Italian Provenance — R’ Moshe ben Kalonymos

The clearest source for the daily Az Yashir in the Ashkenazic minhag is preserved in the Rokeach of R’ Elazar of Worms,[3] who cites a k’sav yad attributing the institution of the practice to Rabbi Moshe ben Kalonymos — an Italian chacham of the Geonic period. This same R’ Moshe ben Kalonymos is the author of two piyutim that every Jewish home still sings each week: Kol Mekadesh Shevi’i, sung at the daytime Shabbos seudah, and Menuchah v’Simchah, sung on Friday night. The paytan whose fingerprint sits on the weekly zemiros of Klal Yisrael is the same figure who brought the daily Az Yashir to Ashkenaz.

In Italy, the recitation of Az Yashir in this position had been the practice from the era of the Churban. The Italian Jewish community traces its origin to those who were exiled from Yerushalayim in the aftermath of Bayis Sheni; the Minhag Roma — still preserved at the Roman synagogue, with its own distinctive variations — carries within it mesoros from that early period. R’ Moshe ben Kalonymos traveled from Italy northward into Germany, where he encountered the Babylonian Geonic practice of omitting the daily Az Yashir, and he introduced the Italian custom into Ashkenaz. From there it spread across the Ashkenazic world and, in time, well beyond.

The Machzor Vitry of R’ Simchah ben Shmuel of Vitry, a talmid and bar plugta of Rashi,[4] records a complementary testimony. He received a teshuvah from the Roman kehillah affirming that their minhag to recite Az Yashir before Yishtabach traced back to the post-Churban exile. Two independent lines of evidence — the Rokeach’s k’sav yad and the Roman teshuvah preserved by the Machzor Vitry — converge on the same historical picture: an Italian minhag of ancient provenance, imported through R’ Moshe ben Kalonymos, that reshaped the Ashkenazic siddur in a way that eventually became universal.

IV. The Aruch HaShulchan’s Kashya and the Zohar’s Resolution

The Order of Sources Inverted

The Aruch HaShulchan[5] raises a piercing kashya on the placement of Az Yashir. There is a klal derived from the Gemara in Rosh Hashanah[6] concerning the structure of Malchuyos, Zichronos, and Shofros: when reciting a series of pesukim together, they are arranged in the order Torah → Nevi’im → Ketuvim. In Pesukei d’Zimra, however, the Halleluyos — drawn from Ketuvim — are recited first, and only afterward Az Yashir, which is from Torah. The canonical ordering is inverted.

The Aruch HaShulchan writes with unusual candor: “I had no resolution until I found that the Zohar HaKadosh asked the same question.”

The Zohar’s Reframing

The Zohar HaKadosh[7] answers by reframing the structural question. Under the standard halachic ordering, Az Yashir should indeed precede the Halleluyos. But al pi sod, Az Yashir is bound not to what precedes it but to what follows: it is linked to Yotzer Or, the first of the Birchos Kriyas Shema. It serves as the spiritual gesher from Pesukei d’Zimra into that next major unit of tefillah. On this reading, Az Yashir belongs precisely at the end of the section, immediately before the transition it inaugurates. After the Arizal, this reasoning became universal practice — superseding both the Rambam’s post-Yishtabach placement and the source-order that the Aruch HaShulchan’s kashya would otherwise have demanded. It is the first of the pieces at the end of Pesukei d’Zimra in which the halachic ordering yields, without protest, to the Kabbalistic reordering; it will not be the last.

V. The Three Fifteens

A structural symmetry at the seam between Az Yashir and Yishtabach is not, on close inspection, coincidental. It runs across three registers:

  • The Mechilta[8] identifies fifteen shevachim, fifteen distinct praises, within the body of Shiras HaYam itself.
  • Yishtabach contains fifteen praises: shir, shevach, hallel, zimrah, oz, memshalah, netzach, gedulah, gevurah, tehillah, tiferes, kedushah, malchus, brachos, hoda’os.
  • The genealogy at the close of Megillas Rus,[9] traced through David and extended one generation to Shlomo, gives exactly fifteen generations from Avraham to Shlomo HaMelech: Avraham, Yitzchak, Yaakov, Yehudah, Peretz, Chetzron, Ram, Aminadav, Nachshon, Salmon, Boaz, Oved, Yishai, David, Shlomo.

The alignment is the compositional scaffolding of the entire seam: fifteen shevachim at the sea flow into fifteen praises at the close of Pesukei d’Zimra, which mirror the fifteen generations from the first of the Avos to the king in whose mouth — as will emerge below — the tradition places the composition of Yishtabach. The tefillah is not accidentally patterned. Its numerical spine ties the moment of national deliverance to the reign of Shlomo, and it does so through a passage recited every morning of the Jewish year.

The Magen Avraham preserves a minhag — not codified by the Mishnah Berurah as obligatory but valued by those who can execute it — to recite the fifteen praises of Yishtabach b’neshimah achas, in a single breath. The Zohar, as will be seen, treats interruption between them as a matter of the greatest spiritual weight.

VI. The Eighteen Names and the Added Pesukim

The Rishonim observe that the Sheim Havayah occurs in the broader Shiras HaYam narrative — including the introductory verses and the concluding verses that frame the song proper — a total of eighteen times. Eighteen times four letters yields seventy-two: the Sheim known in Kabbalah as the Sheim Ayin-Beis, among the most central of the divine Names in the Lurianic system.

A practical difficulty arose. When Az Yashir is recited in the daily siddur, several occurrences of the Name that would be counted in the broader narrative fall outside the passages included in the liturgy. To complete the count to eighteen, additional pesukim were appended at the end of Az Yashir before Yishtabach — the verses beginning V’sarav chasum lemor and continuing through the closing of the unit. Their function was numerological: to restore, within the abbreviated liturgical text, the eighteen-Name structure of the fuller narrative.

The Vilna Gaon, in Ma’aseh Rav,[10] declined to recite these added verses. He held them to be a mistaken interpolation — originally belonging to the Malchuyos of the Yamim Noraim tefillah, from which they had migrated by textual error into the daily nusach. Some printed siddurim, honoring the dispute, bracket these pesukim in parentheses. The mekubalim, who regard the eighteen-Name completion as spiritually significant, retain them; most communities recite them; the Gaon and those who follow his ruling do not. The unresolved question — on which the entire dispute turns — is the precise inventory of Names in Shiras HaYam and its frame, and which specific verses are needed to bring the daily nusach to the count of eighteen. That count is not laid out in the standard sources with the precision one might wish; the arithmetic that would settle the matter for a general reader has, so far as the poskim preserve it, always been assumed rather than shown.

VII. Yishtabach — Whose Bracha Is It?

The Pri Chadash’s Question

The Pri Chadash[11] raised, in an earlier context, a fundamental kashya against reciting brachos with Sheim u’Malchus that are not preserved as fixed texts in the words of Chazal. The question presses with equal force on Yishtabach as on Baruch She’amar: neither appears as a fixed brachah-text in the Bavli or in our extant Yerushalmi.

The Yerushalmi that Resolved It

The resolution turns on a passage of Yerushalmi that is not in our extant editions but is cited by multiple Rishonim. The Yerushalmi, discussing Parshas Shoftim’s account of those who return from the battlefield on account of a small aveirah, defines the category with a striking example: even one who spoke between Yishtabach and Yotzer Or is to be turned back from war, lest his aveirah cause Klal Yisrael to lose. The passage is decisive. If Chazal had a fixed issur against speaking between Yishtabach and Yotzer Or, then Yishtabach must have existed as a fixed brachah in their day. Our girsa of the Yerushalmi may lack the passage, but the mesorah of its citation carries the weight of the ruling.

The Magen Avraham’s Leniency and Its Limits

The Magen Avraham[12] rules that given the post-Talmudic textual status of Baruch She’amar and Yishtabach, certain leniencies apply to them that do not apply to standard brachos whose text is fixed in the Bavli. The paradigm case is that one may answer amen to another’s brachah in the middle of these brachos — a leniency one would not extend to ordinary brachos. The leniency, however, is narrowly bounded. It is confined to the specific responses that halachah in any case privileges: amen, Kedushah, Barchu, and their formal counterparts. It does not extend to speech generally, and it does not soften the core stringency against hefsek between Yishtabach and Yotzer Or, which remains in full force. The Mishnah Berurah’s severe ruling — that one who spoke there must repeat from the beginning — stands beside the Magen Avraham’s leniency, not against it.

The Attributions of Authorship

The Rishonim preserve several accounts of who actually composed the language of Yishtabach:

  • R’ Shlomo of Worms, the Rokeach’s predecessor, and others: the Rabbanan Saberoyim, the generation between the close of the Talmud and the emergence of the Geonim.
  • The Rambam and the Rif: tikkenu chachamim — instituted by Chazal proper.
  • The Meiri: attributes the composition to the Geonim.
  • The Rokeach, preserving an older Ashkenazic mesorah: Shlomo HaMelech composed the body of the brachah, in a setting to which we now turn.

These attributions are not necessarily as mutually exclusive as they appear. The tradition brought below need not be read against tikkenu chachamim: the language of praise may have originated with Shlomo, and Chazal may later have fixed its liturgical form as a brachah. The halachic weight of Yishtabach reflects this hybridity: the leniencies of the Magen Avraham honor the post-Talmudic fixation of the text; the severe stringencies against hefsek honor the antiquity of the underlying words.

VIII. Yishtabach and the Throne of Shlomo

The Rokeach’s Mesorah

The Rokeach preserves a tradition that Shlomo HaMelech composed the opening of Yishtabach at a specific moment in his reign — a moment for which the underlying midrashic background must first be sketched.

The Throne in Tanach

The pesukim in Melachim Aleph[13] describe Shlomo’s throne briefly but with the marks of a wonder the text can only partly contain:

וַיַּעַש׳ הַמֶּלֶך כִּסֵּא שֵׁן גָּדוֹל וַיְצַפֵּהוּ זָהָב מוּפָזװַװ שֵׁשׁ מַעֲלוֹת לַכִּסֵּא וּשְׁנֵים אֲרָיוֹת עֹמְדִים אֵצֶל הַיָּדוֹת וּשְׁנֵים עָשָׂר אֲרָיִים עֹמְדִים שָׁם עַל שֵׁשׁ הַמַּעֲלוֹת.
The king made a great throne of ivory and overlaid it with the finest gold… six steps to the throne… two lions standing beside the armrests, and twelve lions standing on the six steps.
Melachim Aleph 10:18–20

The passage concludes:

וְלֹא נַעֲשָׂה כֵן לְכָל מַמְלָכוֹת.
And nothing like it was made for any kingdom.

The Targum Sheni’s Expansion

The Targum Sheni on Megillas Esther[14] — a midrashic work in targumic Aramaic — expands the terse Tanachic description over many pages into a wonder of mechanical sophistication. As Shlomo ascended each of the six steps, golden lions positioned themselves to escort him; as he sat, golden eagles rose into the air and circled overhead; crafted animals on the steps emitted sounds in coordinated sequence; and when witnesses stood to testify in the king’s presence, the lions and other animals roared, terrifying false witnesses into confessing before they had ever spoken. The midrashic language is compatible with a system of gears, springs, and hidden levers — ordinary mechanical craft driven to an extraordinary refinement — and this reading is far more consonant with the ancient technological record than any speculation about lost powers.

One halachic clarification is needed. The roaring lions could not have functioned as beis din. Halachic testimony requires shenei eidim ne’emanim — two trustworthy witnesses formally received — not psychological devices that extract confession by intimidation. The throne’s mechanism pushed false witnesses to abandon their falsehood by other means; once they had confessed and stood down, the actual halachic process proceeded in its normal form. The mechanism served as a filter on candor, not as a substitute for the beis din itself.

The Throne’s Journey

Tanach and Midrash together trace the throne’s passage through several non-Jewish hands after its removal from Yerushalayim. Shishak, king of Mitzrayim,[15] plundered the treasures of the city in the days of Rechavam ben Shlomo; some midrashic traditions place the throne among his spoils. Pharaoh Necho[16] appears in Tanach with the epithet נכה — “smitten,” “lame” — which the Midrash reads as commemoration of his failed attempt to sit upon Shlomo’s throne: the lions attacked him and he was left limping, Pharaoh ha’Necheh, Pharaoh the Lame. And the Targum Sheni records that Achashverosh sought the throne as well; his obsession with sitting upon it figures in the midrashic background of Megillas Esther. Centuries later, Charlemagne commissioned a replica for his palace at Aachen, attempting to evoke the same image of regal sanctity; and Napoleon, on his tour of Charlemagne’s palace after his conquests, is reported to have stood upon the steps of the replica but to have declined to sit. Even Napoleon, it was said, did not have the audacity.

Yishtabach as Shlomo’s Hodaah

The midrashic account preserved by the Rokeach: when Shlomo’s throne was completed and he ascended it for the first time, all of Klal Yisrael gathered to celebrate. Their instinct was to praise the king himself — for the mechanical wonder, for the splendor, for the malchus that the throne represented. Shlomo refused. Not to me, he said, is the praise. To HaKadosh Baruch Hu alone. And he opened his mouth and recited the words that became Yishtabach:

יִשְׁתַבַּח שִׁמְךָ לָעַד מַלְכֵּנוּ, הָאֵל הַמֶּלֶך הַגָּדוֹל וְהַקָּדוֹשׁ בַּשָּׁמַיִם וּבָּאָרֶץ.
May Your Name be praised forever, our King, the great and holy God and King in the heavens and on the earth.

The fifteen praises that follow were, in this account, Shlomo’s redirection of the tzibbur’s instinct of admiration upward — from the king before them to the King above them. Each morning, Klal Yisrael recites the tefillah Shlomo composed at the precise moment he refused to absorb honor that belonged to HaKadosh Baruch Hu.

IX. The Avraham within the Chasimah

Rav Chaim Vital[17] preserves — uncharacteristically not in the name of the Arizal, but on a separate line of received tradition — a striking teaching about the closing chasimah of Yishtabach. While the body of the brachah is attributed to Shlomo HaMelech, the chasimah itself is attributed to Avraham Avinu. The composite tefillah is thereby understood as the joining of the first of the Avos to the king of the fifteenth generation: fifteen generations framing fifteen praises, with the earliest voice sealing the latest.

The chasimah reads as follows in the standard nusach:

בָּרוּך אַתָּה ה׳, אֵל מֶלֶך גָּדוֹל בַּתִּשְׁבָּחוֹת, אֵל הַהוֹדָאוֹת, אֲדוֹן הַנִּפְלָאוֹת, הַבּוֹחֵר בְּשִׁירֵי זִמְרָה, מֶלֶך אֵל חֵי הָעוֹלָמִים.
Blessed are You, Hashem, God, King great in praises, God of thanksgivings, Master of wonders, Who chooses the songs of praise, King, God, Life of the worlds.
Chasimas Yishtabach, standard Ashkenazic nusach

The tradition preserved by Rav Chaim Vital identifies within these closing epithets the letters that spell אברהם: the א from El Melech, the ב from b’tishbachos, the ר from an appropriate word within the sequence, the ה from HaBocher, and the מ from Melech. Different mekubalim distribute the letters somewhat differently across the epithets, but the received tradition is uniform in identifying the name of Avraham Avinu as embedded within the chasimah. Whether one takes the tradition as a literal report of Avrahamic authorship, an oral mesorah of primeval language later fixed by Chazal, or a purely sod-level identification of the spiritual root of the closing words, the effect on davening is the same. The Jew who recites the chasimah is, in that instant, tracing the name of the first of the Avos across the seal of a tefillah composed by the king of his fifteenth generation.

X. Tosafos and the Brachah HaSemuchah L’Chaverta

Tosafos[18] raises the structural question that every attentive reader of the siddur eventually notices. Every extended brachah in the halachic register opens with Baruch and closes with Baruch. Yishtabach closes with the standard Baruch Atah Hashem. But at its opening — Yishtabach Shimcha — there is no Baruch at all. Why?

Tosafos’ answer: Yishtabach is a brachah ha’semuchah l’chaverta, a brachah immediately following another brachah. The Baruch at the opening of Baruch She’amar functions as the opening Baruch for the entire bracketed unit. From Baruch She’amar through the closing Baruch of Yishtabach, one is inside a single extended brachah-structure; everything in between — the Halleluyos, Az Yashir, the whole body of Pesukei d’Zimra — constitutes the substance of that one brachah.

The practical nafka mina is severe. Speaking between Baruch She’amar and Yishtabach is not hefsek between brachos; it is hefsek within a single brachah. This is why the Mishnah Berurah rules that one who spoke in this stretch must repeat from Baruch She’amar, all of Pesukei d’Zimra, and Yishtabach from the beginning. It is also why the Rambam’s post-Yishtabach placement of Az Yashir, dismissed today as a curiosity, is halachically the most careful: it alone preserves the integrity of the single-brachah unit that Tosafos’ teretz identifies. That the universal minhag overrides this careful ordering on Zohar-based grounds is the sharpest instance in Pesukei d’Zimra of Kabbalistic authority reshaping halachic structure. It is a pattern the reader has now seen three times in the same seam of tefillah.

XI. The Zohar on Interrupting the Fifteen Praises

The Zohar HaKadosh[19] describes the fifteen praises of Yishtabach as praises of exceeding holiness, to be recited b’retzef — in an unbroken flow. Interruption between them is treated with vivid Kabbalistic rebuke. From beneath the wings of the keruvim, the Zohar teaches, a flame emerges and announces by name the person who interrupted: Such-and-such has cut himself off between the praises of the holy King. The person is described as nifsak — severed — from the spiritual source into which the fifteen praises are ordinarily channeled. The Mishnah Berurah, more soberly, echoes the same principle in halachic register: one is to be particularly careful not to mafsik within these specific fifteen praises. The minhag preserved by the Magen Avraham to recite them b’neshimah achas is the practical response to the same underlying teaching.

The Zohar further draws a connection between Yishtabach and Shir HaShirim asher li’Shlomo. The link supports the attribution of the tefillah to Shlomo HaMelech and closes the circle with the throne narrative preserved by the Rokeach.

XII. Inserting Shir HaMaalos on Yamim Noraim

If hefsek between Yishtabach and Yotzer Or is treated with such severity, how do the Nusach Sefard and mekubalim communities insert Shir HaMaalos in this position on Yom Kippur and Hoshana Rabbah? The Vilna Gaon and many Litvish kehillos do not follow the practice, precisely on the strength of the hefsek concern. The mekubalim and communities that do insert it rely on a halachic distinction: the issur against hefsek applies to devarim beteilim, idle speech, whereas divrei tefillah whose insertion is authorized by an established tzibbur-level minhag — Kaddish, Barchu, Shir HaMaalos in its designated place — are not classified as hefsek.

The distinction is genuine but bears its own weight. It is not that any divrei tefillah may be inserted; that would empty the hefsek stringency of content. The permissive category is narrowly bounded to fixed communal additions of established provenance. An individual’s spontaneous tefillah, however sincere, does not fall under it. The mekubalim’s authorization of Shir HaMaalos in this position rests specifically on their claim that its insertion carries the weight of Lurianic tzibbur-level authority. Communities that decline to follow the practice do so because they weigh the underlying hefsek concern more heavily than the received Lurianic minhag. The tefillah preserves both readings; the reader who follows one minhag does so knowing that the other stands on principled halachic ground.

XIII. Posture as Structural Marker

The minhagim of standing and sitting through this section of tefillah are not, on close attention, arbitrary; they trace the same structural boundaries the analysis above has drawn. Most communities stand from Baruch She’amar — or, in variant practice, from Vayivarech David or Baruch Hashem L’olam — through the end of Yishtabach, and remain standing through Barchu. Some mekubalim preserve a specific minhag not to stand during Az Yashir itself, reflecting a distinct spiritual reading of the shirah. With the transition into Yotzer Or, one sits: Birchos Kriyas Shema and Shema are tefillah b’meyushav, prayer of the seated posture, and standing is generally discouraged there unless one lacks a seat. Shemoneh Esrei, of course, alone requires the standing of the amidah proper.

The pattern makes visible in the body what Tosafos identified in the text. The standing posture bounds the single-brachah unit from Baruch She’amar to Yishtabach; the seating that follows marks the beginning of the next unit; the standing of Shemoneh Esrei marks the final approach. Posture is the physical trace of the halachic structure the brachah-frame imposes.

XIV. Synthesis and Closing

The seam between Az Yashir and Yishtabach — a matter of a few minutes in the daily davening, easily passed over — is the sedimented deposit of four converging currents of mesorah. The halachic current, running from the Geonim through the Rambam, treats the placement with caution, protecting the framing brachos against hefsek. The historical current, running through R’ Moshe ben Kalonymos from the post-Churban Roman kehillah, imports an ancient Italian minhag into Ashkenaz and eventually into the universal practice of Klal Yisrael. The Kabbalistic current, running through the Zohar and the Arizal, reframes Az Yashir as a spiritual gesher and displaces the halachic ordering that would otherwise have prevailed. And the compositional current binds all of it together through the numerical scaffolding of fifteens — fifteen shevachim in Shiras HaYam, fifteen praises in Yishtabach, fifteen generations from Avraham to Shlomo — with the name of the first of the Avos traced as an acrostic across the seal of the tefillah that the fifteenth generation composed.

What the Jew recites each morning is not a single composition but two thousand years of layered mesorah compressed into a single arc. The halachah protects its integrity; the Kabbalah reveals its inner architecture; the midrashic tradition preserves the moments when great tzaddikim — Shlomo refusing praise at his throne, Avraham implicit at the seal — first formulated the words. The tefillah is our nation’s history compressed into a daily act.

The Skulener Rebbe, at the wall of his bungalow, said Asher Yatzar for five and six minutes because Asher Yatzar was real to him. What was true of Asher Yatzar in his mouth is potentially true of every word of Yishtabach in ours. The fifteen praises are not a series to be checked off. They are the words in which Shlomo HaMelech, refusing honor at the height of his reign, turned the eyes of Klal Yisrael upward — and in which, at the closing, the name of the first of the Avos is traced across the seal. To recite them b’retzef, in an unbroken flow, as the Zohar asks, is to receive that redirection anew each morning. It is a small thing that can, in the mouth of one who takes it seriously, transform the day into which one enters. May the tefillos of our generation ascend on the strength of those who, like the Rebbe, showed us what real tefillah looks like.

מראה מקומות  ·  Mareh Mekomos

Each note runs short citation first (for quick lookup), then an expanded elaboration. Footnotes run sequentially with the body text.

  1. Siddur Rav Amram Gaon, order of Pesukei d’Zimra. — Records that the yeshivos of Sura and Pumbedisa did not recite Az Yashir daily. Rav Amram notes — significantly — that one should not protest those communities that do recite it, and the practice is permissible even if not the Babylonian standard. The baseline against which the Italian and Ashkenazic minhag stands in contrast. ↩
  2. Rambam, Hilchos Tefillah, ch. 7 (Seder HaTefillah). — The Rambam’s halachic placement: Az Yashir, if recited daily, belongs after Yishtabach, avoiding hefsek within the brachah-frame of Pesukei d’Zimra. He also records that some communities of his day substituted Ha’azinu for Az Yashir. No community today follows the post-Yishtabach placement; the universal practice is to recite Az Yashir before Yishtabach, on the strength of the Zohar and the Arizal. ↩
  3. Rokeach (R’ Elazar of Worms), siman 320 and parallels — citing R’ Moshe ben Kalonymos and the Italian minhag. — Preserves the k’sav yad attributing the institution of daily Az Yashir in Ashkenaz to R’ Moshe ben Kalonymos, who imported the practice from Italy. Italy’s mesorah traces back to those exiled from Yerushalayim after the Churban Bayis Sheni, preserving the Minhag Roma. R’ Moshe ben Kalonymos is also the paytan of Kol Mekadesh Shevi’i and Menuchah v’Simchah. ↩
  4. Machzor Vitry (R’ Simchah ben Shmuel of Vitry), siman 7 and following. — A talmid and bar plugta of Rashi. His machzor preserves the complementary testimony: the Roman kehillah sent him a written teshuvah affirming that their minhag to recite Az Yashir before Yishtabach traced back to the post-Churban exile to Italy. Independent corroboration of the Italian provenance documented by the Rokeach. ↩
  5. Aruch HaShulchan, Orach Chaim 51. — The kashya on the inverted source-order in Pesukei d’Zimra: Halleluyos (Ketuvim) recited before Az Yashir (Torah), opposite the canonical Torah → Nevi’im → Ketuvim rule from Rosh Hashanah 32a. The Aruch HaShulchan’s candid resolution: “I had no resolution until I found that the Zohar HaKadosh asked the same question.” ↩
  6. Rosh Hashanah ל״ב ע״א (32a). — The structural klal for Malchuyos, Zichronos, and Shofros: when reciting a series of pesukim together, the order runs Torah → Nevi’im → Ketuvim. The source of the Aruch HaShulchan’s challenge to the placement of Az Yashir after the Halleluyos. ↩
  7. Zohar, Parshas Beshalach; cf. parallels in Terumah (referenced through the Aruch HaShulchan, Orach Chaim 51). — The Zohar’s reframing: al pi sod, Az Yashir is bound to Yotzer Or, serving as the spiritual gesher from Pesukei d’Zimra into Birchos Kriyas Shema. Its placement at the end of the section, immediately before that transition, follows from this pairing. After the Arizal, this became universal practice, superseding the Rambam’s post-Yishtabach placement and the source-order the Aruch HaShulchan’s kashya would otherwise have demanded. ↩
  8. Mechilta d’Rabbi Yishmael, Parshas Beshalach, on Shemos ch. 15. — Identifies fifteen shevachim, fifteen distinct praises, within the body of Shiras HaYam. The structural anchor for the three-way symmetry of fifteens: fifteen shevachim in Az Yashir, fifteen praises in Yishtabach, fifteen generations from Avraham to Shlomo. ↩
  9. Megillas Rus 4:18–22 (closing yichus). — The yichus traced from Peretz to David, extended with the Avos prefixed and Shlomo appended, yields fifteen generations: Avraham, Yitzchak, Yaakov, Yehudah, Peretz, Chetzron, Ram, Aminadav, Nachshon, Salmon, Boaz, Oved, Yishai, David, Shlomo. The numerical scaffolding behind the fifteen praises of Yishtabach. ↩
  10. Vilna Gaon, Ma’aseh Rav, siman 41 and parallels. — The Gaon’s omission of the added pesukim at the end of Az Yashir (V’sarav chasum lemor and continuing). He held these to be a mistaken interpolation from the Malchuyos of the Yamim Noraim, which migrated into the daily nusach by textual error. Some printed siddurim bracket these pesukim in parentheses to honor the dispute. Mekubalim, who regard the eighteen-Name completion as spiritually significant, retain them; the majority of communities recite them. ↩
  11. Pri Chadash, Orach Chaim 51 (R’ Chizkiyahu di Silva). — The kashya against reciting brachos with Sheim u’Malchus not preserved as fixed texts in the words of Chazal. Applied to both Baruch She’amar and Yishtabach; resolved by the cited Yerushalmi that establishes the issur hefsek between Yishtabach and Yotzer Or as Talmudic in origin, even though the passage does not appear in our extant Yerushalmi. ↩
  12. Magen Avraham, Orach Chaim 51. — Given the post-Talmudic textual status of Baruch She’amar and Yishtabach, the Magen Avraham permits leniencies that do not apply to standard brachos whose text is fixed in the Bavli — paradigmatically, answering amen and its formal counterparts (Kedushah, Barchu) in the middle of these brachos. The leniency is narrowly bounded to those specific responses and does not soften the core stringency against hefsek between Yishtabach and Yotzer Or. Also records the minhag to recite the fifteen praises of Yishtabach b’neshimah achas, in a single breath, where one is able. ↩
  13. Melachim Aleph 10:18–20; parallel in Divrei HaYamim Beis 9:17–19. — The Tanachic description of Shlomo’s throne: ivory overlaid with the finest gold, six steps, two lions beside the armrests, twelve lions on the steps. The passage concludes וְלֹא נַעֲשָׂה כֵן לְכָל מַמְלָכוֹת — “and nothing like it was made for any kingdom.” ↩
  14. Targum Sheni on Megillas Esther 1:2. — A midrashic work in targumic Aramaic, expanding briefly-recorded narratives of Esther. The throne description spans many pages: golden lions positioning themselves to escort Shlomo upward the six steps, golden eagles rising and circling, animals emitting coordinated sounds, lions roaring at false witnesses to induce truthful testimony. Achashverosh’s obsession with sitting on the throne figures in the midrashic background of Esther. ↩
  15. Melachim Aleph 14:25–26. — Shishak king of Mitzrayim plundered the treasures of Yerushalayim in the days of Rechavam ben Shlomo. Some midrashic traditions hold that Shlomo’s throne was among the spoils transported to Egypt, before later non-Jewish sovereigns sought to reclaim it in turn. ↩
  16. Melachim Beis 23:29 and following. — The Tanachic account of Pharaoh Necho. The Midrash reads his epithet נכה (“smitten,” “lame”) as commemoration of his attempt to sit upon Shlomo’s throne: the lions attacked him and he was left injured — Pharaoh ha’Necheh, Pharaoh the Lame. ↩
  17. R’ Chaim Vital — preserved separately from his transmission of the Arizal’s teachings; cited in later Kabbalistic anthologies. — Records the tradition that while Shlomo HaMelech composed the body of Yishtabach, the closing chasimah was composed by Avraham Avinu — the name אברהם is embedded within the closing epithets. Rav Chaim Vital cites this not in the name of the Arizal but on a separate line of received tradition, which is itself notable; the standard channels of Lurianic transmission are not the source. Different mekubalim distribute the letters somewhat differently across the epithets of the chasimah; the received tradition is uniform in identifying the acrostic as present. ↩
  18. Tosafos, Berachos מ״ו ע״א (46a), s.v. kol habrachos kulan. — The structural teretz: Yishtabach opens without Baruch because it is a brachah ha’semuchah l’chaverta, a continuation of Baruch She’amar. The Baruch at the opening of Baruch She’amar functions as the opening for the entire bracketed unit. Practical consequence: speaking between the two is hefsek within a single brachah, not merely between brachos, and the Mishnah Berurah accordingly rules to repeat from the beginning. ↩
  19. Zohar, Terumah (on the fifteen praises of Yishtabach). — The fifteen praises of Yishtabach as praises of exceeding holiness, to be recited b’retzef, in an unbroken flow. Vivid Kabbalistic rebuke for interrupting: a flame emerges from beneath the wings of the keruvim and announces by name the one who interrupted, described as nifsak from the spiritual source. The Mishnah Berurah echoes the principle more soberly as a caution against hefsek within these specific fifteen. The Zohar also draws a connection to Shir HaShirim asher li’Shlomo, supporting the attribution of Yishtabach to Shlomo HaMelech. ↩
