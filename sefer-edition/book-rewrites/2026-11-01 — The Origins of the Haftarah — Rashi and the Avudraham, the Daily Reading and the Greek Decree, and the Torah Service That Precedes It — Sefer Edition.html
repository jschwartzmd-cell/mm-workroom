מְקוֹר הַהַפְטָרָה — רש״י וְהָאַבּוּדַרְהַם, קְרִיאָה יוֹמִית וּגְזֵרַת יָוָן, וְסֵדֶר הוֹצָאַת סֵפֶר תּוֹרָה

The Origins of the Haftarah: Rashi and the Avudraham, the Daily Reading and the Greek Decree, and the Torah Service That Precedes It
Studies in the Shabbos Morning Tefillah (תפילה) — From שיר של יום and אתה הראת to the Reading of the Prophets
21 Cheshvan 5787  ·  Sefer Edition

This discourse was delivered in honor of the yahrzeit (יארצייט) of מרים בת חיים יצחק, the mother of a friend of the shul, who sponsored the morning’s study and breakfast. The lecturer asked that her soul have an aliyah (עליה) and that it bring salvation to all Israel.[1]

Why do we read the haftarah (הפטרה)? The Rishonim give two answers that could hardly be further apart. According to Rashi, as recorded in the Sefer HaPardes (ספר הפרדס), a chapter of the Prophets was once read every day by every Jew after prayer, before he went to work, and it survived only on Shabbos when poverty (רבתה העניות) ended the daily practice. According to the Avudraham (אבודרהם), it began as an emergency measure when a foreign decree forbade the reading of the Torah itself: the Sages set at least twenty-one verses of the Prophets in its place, so that the people should not forget the Torah reading, and the substitute outlived the decree. This discourse sets the two accounts side by side, with the later story of how the haftarah came to match the portion and the day, and with the dating of the decree to Antiochus by Elijah Levita. As a setting it first takes up, briefly, the service that leads to the reading: the song of the day (שיר של יום), the taking out of the Torah, the verses of Masechet Soferim (מסכת סופרים) that the Or Zarua restored, אתה הראת, and the Mi Sheberach (מי שברך) after each aliyah.

I. The Setting: The Song of the Day and the Taking Out of the Torah

The discourse takes up the Shabbos morning service at the point where Shacharis is complete. One item remains before the Torah is taken out: the Shir Shel Yom (שיר של יום), the psalm of the day. On Shabbos the congregation says היום יום שבת קודש, שבו היו הלוים אומרים בבית המקדש, “Today is the holy Shabbos, on which the Levites used to say in the Temple,” and then Psalm 92, מזמור שיר ליום השבת.[2] Nusach Sefard (נוסח ספרד), which is the lecturer’s own, says the psalm after Shacharis, although it has already said Psalm 92 among the Verses of Praise (Pesukei DeZimrah, פסוקי דזמרה). Traditional Nusach Ashkenaz (נוסח אשכנז) says it after Musaf.

Why After Musaf?

The lecturer calls the Ashkenazic placement a peleh (פלא), a wonder. The song of the day belongs to the daily offering: while the Kohanim poured the libations that accompany every tamid (תמיד) offering, the Levites stood on the platform and sang, and Shacharis corresponds to the morning tamid. There is no reason to wait for Musaf. On the contrary, on Rosh Chodesh, Shabbos and Yom Tov the Levites sang a special song over the Musaf offering, so that after Musaf a different song was due. This, in the lecturer’s understanding of the Magen Avraham, is why that authority finds the Ashkenazic custom to make no sense and holds the Sefardic placement to be the sound one. The Magen Avraham’s printed wording is, however, more guarded: it describes a custom of saying the song both after Shacharis and after Musaf, and the lecturer’s summary is to be read with that in mind.[3]

A second difficulty, which the lecturer says the Aruch HaShulchan also raises, is that the song of the Musaf offering is not said at all today. Why should the congregation say the song that went with the morning tamid and omit the song that went with the Musaf? The lecturer’s reply is that Masechet Soferim, at the end of the volume of Avodah Zarah, sets out the songs of each Musaf (Rosh Chodesh, Shabbos, Yom Tov, Shavuos, Sukkos and Pesach).[4] How this explains the present custom is not worked out. A member of the congregation suggested that the songs are named for the day, so that a Musaf song would not be a song of the day; the lecturer answered that the Musaf song could still be added.

The same logic, he adds, ought to require a song after Minchah, since the evening tamid also had its libation. The Magen Avraham answers that the evening tamid was sometimes offered so late that the libation fell after sunset, and that the Levites did not sing by night; since the song might therefore not have been said, none is said at Minchah.[5] Some congregations, it is reported, have moved the song of the day, and Anim Zemiros, to after Shacharis for a quite different reason: the disorder after Musaf, when worshippers talk and fold their talleisim before the prayers are over. “It is not after davening yet!”[6]

Who Opens the Ark

In the lecturer’s shul the honor of taking out the Torah is sold on Simchas Torah for the whole year, a custom, he says, that not every shul has. The buyer will sometimes yield it for a given Shabbos to a groom (chasan, חתן) or to a man whose wife is expecting a child. The custom has a source in the Or Zarua (אור זרוע), the work of Rabbi Yitzchak ben Moshe, the rabbi of Vienna in the thirteenth century and one of the Baalei HaTosafos, a book that records a great many halachos. He writes that it is a great merit (zechus, זכות) to buy the taking out of the Torah, and an honor to the Torah (כבוד התורה).[7]

For the man whose wife is in her ninth month, the lecturer cites the Chida, who records it as an ancient custom (minhag vasikin, מנהג ותיקין). The idea is symbolic: she needs the opening of the womb, so he opens the ark, and the opening of the ark is an auspicious time (eis ratzon, עת רצון) for prayer.[8] When the lecturer sees a young man go up for this honor, he stands beside the ark and says to him: “Don’t just open it and take out the Torah. Daven! Daven that you should have children, and that the child should be a ben Torah.” For a girl, he says, he is not sure what to ask; a member of the congregation suggested that she marry a talmid chacham, and he agreed. As to why a groom receives the same honor, he put the question to the room: perhaps the same auspicious moment, perhaps a preparation for the mitzvah (הכשר מצוה). “It could be” (קען זיין), he says, and moves on.

II. The Verses Said as the Torah Is Taken Out: Soferim and the Or Zarua

During the week, as the Torah is taken out, the congregation says אל ארך אפים, or a few verses such as אין כמוך, and then ויהי בנסוע הארן. On Shabbos it begins with אתה הראת and says verses that differ from the weekday ones. The lecturer, who says he likes to ask what stands behind every line of the siddur, asks where they come from.

Masechet Soferim and the Customs of Eretz Yisrael

Among the minor tractates printed after Avodah Zarah stands Masechet Soferim (מסכת סופרים), the tractate of the scribes. It contains much that is found in neither Talmud, and the lecturer says that scholars still debate when it was written, by whom, and how it spread. From his reading of a book on the subject, he takes it to be chiefly a record of the ancient customs of Eretz Yisrael, which stood in a great dispute with the customs of Bavel.[9] In Eretz Yisrael, it seems, a long series of verses was said when the Torah was taken out of the ark, one verse by the chazzan and then by the congregation, some repeated twice. To do all of it on Shabbos and Yom Tov would add an hour to the service. בריך שמיה is absent, and comes, the lecturer says, from the Zohar. Our practice is a much abbreviated form: some of our verses are in Soferim and some were added, and ויהי בנסוע הארן, which we say each time, is not there at all. “It is a new thing.”

The Or Zarua and the Return of the Verses

What we say today, the lecturer holds, comes from the Or Zarua. The author’s book is known by a title that he explains by a story from its introduction. Writing a get (גט) for a man named Akiva, he had to decide whether the name ends in an alef (as in the Bavli and the Mishnah) or in a heh (as, the lecturer says, in the Yerushalmi and the Tosefta). A get with a single letter wrong is invalid, and the case is that of a married woman. He tells that he was shown from Heaven in a dream a verse:

אוֹר זָרֻעַ לַצַּדִּיק וּלְיִשְׁרֵי־לֵב שִׂמְחָה
Light is sown for the righteous, and joy for the upright in heart.
Tehillim 97:11 (the last letter of each word marked)

The final letters of the six words read ר-ע-ק-י-ב-ה, which the lecturer takes as רבי עקיבה; and the last letter is a heh. “So he said, thank you, Heaven, this is how I will write the get.” He loved the revelation so much, the lecturer says, that he named his book Or Zarua to remind himself. The story explains the name of the book and not the custom of the verses; it is told here to introduce its author. (The objection that “the Torah is not in Heaven,” לא בשמים היא, was raised in the room, and the lecturer called it a good one.) How the matter is decided in practice, and the related disputes over names such as Pinchas in gittin, are beyond the scope of the discourse.[10][11]

The Or Zarua writes that he travelled widely in Ashkenaz and Poland, in the thirteenth century, and that in the region he calls Medinas Reinus (the Rhineland) nothing was said when the Torah was taken out: after Shacharis they went to the ark, took out the scroll, carried it to the bimah and read. He urged that the custom follow that of Eretz Yisrael in Soferim, and he gave it in an abbreviated form, only several verses. Those are the verses we say. All of them are verses of Tanach, from Dovid HaMelech in Tehillim and elsewhere.[12]

The lecturer gives a second account, which credits a different person. The Shibbolei HaLeket (שבלי הלקט), which the Beis Yosef quotes very often in the laws of prayer, was written, as the lecturer recalls, by the son of Rav Avraham the doctor, a leading authority and head of a yeshivah in Rome, of the family of the Anavim. In this account it was Rav Avraham who saw that in Rome, as elsewhere, nothing was said when the Torah was taken out, and who urged that the custom of Soferim be brought back; “from that point on in history,” the lecturer says, all Israel says these verses. The two accounts are not reconciled in the discourse. The lecturer’s own statement, that our practice “comes from the Or Zarua,” is taken here as the main account, and Rav Avraham’s as a variant.[13]

The Four Families Exiled to Rome

The lecturer tells, as a legend recorded by the Rishonim, that when Titus destroyed the Second Temple he exiled many Jews, and among them four very prominent families from the nobility of Jerusalem: the Adomim (האדומים), the Tapuchim (התפוחים), the Anavim (הענוים), and a fourth, whom he does not name. They were taken to Rome. Many of the Rishonim and Poskim came from these families, and many halachos we follow rest on books written by their members. The Poskim take them seriously because they carry a direct tradition from the ancient customs of Eretz Yisrael: “They came from the destruction of the Second Temple, and they went and they never changed. They lived in Rome for hundreds of years.”

III. Atah Hareisa and Vayehi Binsoa HaAron

On Shabbos the Torah is taken out to the verse אתה הראת לדעת. Why on Shabbos, and not during the week, when the congregation says אל ארך אפים? The weekday verse speaks of forgiveness and pardon, “a weekday matter.” אתה הראת was said of the giving of the Torah, in Parshas Vaeschanan, and the Torah was given on Shabbos; therefore, before the scroll is taken out on Shabbos, we recall that fact. The lecturer points to the Tur at Orach Chayim 281.[14]

אַתָּה הָרְאֵתָ לָדַעַת כִּי יְהוָה הוּא הָאֱלֹהִים אֵין עוֹד מִלְּבַדּוֹ
You have been shown, that you may know, that Hashem is God; there is none else besides Him.
Devarim 4:35

Here the two rites differ again, as they did over the song of the day. The Tur, who lived in Spain in the second half of his life, reports that the Spanish custom is to say אתה הראת; Nusach Sefard adopted it, while Nusach Ashkenaz, the lecturer says, does not say it at all. On Shabbos it takes the place of the weekday אל ארך אפים. The lecturer adds that the other weekday verses are left out on Shabbos, though he had said earlier that Shabbos has other verses of its own, and the point is not worked out here. After it comes ויהי בנסע הארן, which, as noted, is not in Soferim but is entirely fitting: the Torah is being taken out of the ark, and the ark is moving.[15] Then בריך שמיה.

IV. The Mi Sheberach After Each Aliyah

On Shabbos a Mi Sheberach (מי שברך) is said after every aliyah (עליה). On Monday and Thursday there is none, unless it is for a woman who has given birth or for someone sick. Where does the practice of stopping in the middle of the reading come from? The lecturer’s first answer is brief: “For money.”

The Pledge

The Midrash in Vayikra Rabbah and Kohelet Rabbah, he says, reflects an ancient custom of giving the man called to the Torah a blessing, מי שברך אבותינו אברהם יצחק ויעקב, in the merit of the Torah open on the bimah.[16] The point of the Mi Sheberach, in the lecturer’s view, is the gift: the pledge to the synagogue. One who pledges charity in public and fails to pay brings a warning that, the lecturer says, he does not want to repeat from the Midrash “because it is very scary.” The Gemara, in Shabbos 32b, says that through the sin of unfulfilled vows (בעוון נדרים) a man’s wife and children die, Heaven forbid; the lecturer, who named Nedarim, cites it together with the verses מוצא שפתיך תשמור and לא יחל דברו ככל היוצא מפיו יעשה.[17] For this reason the gabbai should always add bli neder.

As to practice, when the gabbai gives him an aliyah and recites בעבור שצוה לברכם, the lecturer tells him that it should be בעבור שיתן מתנה, “because he will give a gift,” to the synagogue, “otherwise there is no point to the Mi Sheberach.” The other wording is how rabbis think they can “get away” without a pledge. This, he says, was the original custom; he gives no source for it.

The Prayer for the Sick

The lecturer gave special attention to the Mi Sheberach for the sick after a woman of the ezras nashim told him that she comes to shul on Shabbos morning above all for that moment. She has a list of sick people for whom she prays, and she asked why everyone was talking. He took it seriously, and the gabbai now calls for quiet before it. People wait a whole week to say a Mi Sheberach when the Torah is open, so that the merit of the Torah may bring healing.

But how can healing be requested on Shabbos? The Shabbos Amidah has no רפאנו: personal requests are not made on the day when it is as though all one’s work is done (כל מלאכתך עשויה), a day that is all Shabbos (יום שכולו שבת). The Beis Yosef was asked exactly this and answered, in the responsum Avkas Rochel, that a Mi Sheberach is not a prayer (tefillah) but a blessing (berachah): it lets the family know that we are thinking of the patient, and that the merit of the Torah should bring healing; it is “a simple blessing,” not a request. And therefore, he concludes, הנח להם לישראל: let Israel follow its custom.[18]

V. The Haftarah: Two Accounts of Its Origin

After the seven aliyos of Shabbos comes the haftarah (הפטרה), and with it the question of this discourse: where does it come from, and why do we read it? (The word is haftarah, from maftir; the common pronunciation haftorah is a mistake, though “you are not going to go to Gehinnom for it.”) The lecturer says that the Rishonim are in “a very radical machloket, extreme from one to the other,” and that “this is all Jewish history.”

For Rashi, as recorded in the Sefer HaPardes, the reading of the Prophets was once a daily act of study, performed by every Jew after prayer, which survived only on Shabbos. For the Avudraham it was an emergency measure, enacted when a decree forbade the reading of the Torah itself, and it outlived the decree. The accounts differ in what the reading was at first (a custom of study, or a substitute for the Torah), in how often it was read (every day, or on Shabbos), and in its relation to the Torah portion (none at first, or the very subject of what could not be read). The two accounts are taken in turn; between them stands the later history of how the haftarah came to match the day.

VI. The First Account: Rashi and the Daily Reading of the Prophets

Before Work, a Chapter of Nevi’im

Rashi, as the lecturer relays him from the Sefer HaPardes, a book that is not Rashi’s commentary on Talmud or Torah but quotes much from him, says that in earlier times every single day, and not only on Monday and Thursday, the custom was that after prayer everyone sat down and read a chapter of Nevi’im. “You ever knew this? Every day after Shacharis, you close the siddur.” The purpose was that before going to work a man should have read mikra (מקרא, the Chumash), navi (נביא), mishnah and beraisa, and, as the lecturer gives the list, perhaps also gemara.[19]

The lecturer shows that the daily prayers already fulfil most of this. Mikra is the Shema. The Gemara in Menachos 99b teaches that one who recites the Shema morning and evening has fulfilled והגית בו יומם ולילה, but that it is forbidden to say this before amei ha’aretz, who would conclude that they need do nothing more.[20] Mishnah is איזהו מקומן (“What if you don’t say it? You’re in big trouble”) and אלו דברים after the blessing over the Torah. Beraisa is רבי ישמעאל אומר and others throughout the service. “But when do we say Navi?” A member of the congregation pointed to ובא לציון גואל.

The lecturer restates Rashi’s list and, in his second statement, it has no Gemara in it: Scripture (the Prophets and the Writings), Mishnah and Beraisa. This, he says, is why he suspects that Rashi is quoting a custom from before the Gemara was written at all. It is his surmise, and he says so. What was read is not known. They may have begun from Yehoshua and read a chapter a day; “I don’t know if they read two chapters a day.” “You would happily be well versed in Tanach if we did this every day.”

Why the Reading Lapsed

The reason it stopped, the lecturer says, is socioeconomic. Rashi writes רבתה העניות, “poverty has grown,” and the lecturer finds it “sad to read”: people were poor and had to earn a living, and one cannot keep them in shul an extra half hour for a set study. “You can invite them to a daf, but to force them to say it’s part of davening, don’t take off your tefillin until you read a chapter in Nevi’im, that we can’t do anymore.” The custom was nullified except on Shabbos, when no one is rushing to work.

On this account the haftarah is a law of every day, with at first nothing to do with the weekly Torah portion, kept only on Shabbos because it could no longer be kept daily. The lecturer calls this “a startling chiddush; people don’t know this.” He adds, without citing a source, that in Rashi’s view the reading had blessings before and after, and that it did not have to match the event (מעין המאורע) but could be simply the next chapter.

VII. How the Haftarah Came to Match the Day

On any account the haftarah we read is tied to the day, or to the portion. The lecturer treats this as the development of the first account, but it is not Rashi’s own view, and some of his examples are later than Rashi. They are gathered here as a separate matter.

The Haftarah of the Day, and the Groom

On Yom Tov, on Rosh Chodesh, and on a Shabbos that falls the day before Rosh Chodesh, a haftarah of the day is read in place of one that matches the portion. The lecturer reports a custom, which he attributes to the Rema, that when there was a groom in shul on the Shabbos of his aufruf, the haftarah שוש אשיש was read, a chapter of Yeshayahu from the seven of consolation (שבע דנחמתא) that speaks of a groom and a bride. The Sefardim adopted it, he says, and “we don’t do it.” He asked how a large Sefardic shul with a groom every week could manage, since the haftarah of the week would then never be read.[21]

The Death of the Rambam and of Rav Sherira Gaon

The lecturer gives a still greater chiddush, which, he says, he learned only that morning. The Rishonim write, he reports, that when something significant happened that week they did not read the haftarah of the week but the one that fit the event. When the Rambam died, on 20 Tevet in the year 1204 and in Egypt, a chapter of Shmuel was read that Shabbos: the capture of the ark of the covenant by the Philistines (I Shmuel 4). The Rambam, the lecturer says, was “like the ark of the covenant, the power of Torah of his generation”; the ark was taken into exile, and his soul went up to Heaven. When Rav Sherira Gaon died, in the Geonic era, around the year 1000, a special chapter was read on the death of a righteous man. “We don’t do that today.” This is a tradition the lecturer relates, and he names none of the Rishonim who record it. He himself gave the date of the Rambam’s yahrzeit only as “somewhere in Shevat,” and spoke of Eretz Yisrael; the date and place are given here as they are known.[22]

The Triennial Cycle and the Haftarah of the Portion

Where the Torah was read over three years, as it was in Eretz Yisrael, each portion was much shorter, and the Acharonim point out that the haftarah was chosen to match whatever small portion was read that week; there were therefore many more haftaros than there are now. The lecturer brings Benjamin of Tudela, the twelfth-century traveller, who writes that in Cairo, “right next door to the Rambam’s shul,” there was another congregation that kept the ancient custom, completing the Torah only once in three years, with a haftarah connected to each portion.[23]

Machar Chodesh

One haftarah seems to match neither the portion nor the day: Machar Chodesh (מחר חודש), which concerns tomorrow and not today. The lecturer calls it an excellent question, which the Magen Avraham raises, and says that he has no good answer: “the Magen Avraham doesn’t like it.” Our practice, he says, is to read the haftarah of Rosh Chodesh when Shabbos is itself Rosh Chodesh, and not Machar Chodesh; what he says of the practice of others is not clear.[24]

VIII. The Second Account: The Avudraham and the Greek Decree

A Substitute for the Torah

The second view is that of the Avudraham. He brings, the lecturer says, that once there was a decree against reading from the Torah, against reading the portion. The Sages, “or whoever was there at the time,” feared that the people would forget the Torah reading: they would come to shul, pray Shacharis, then Musaf, “no break,” and go home. So they enacted that, since the Torah could not be read, at least twenty-one verses of the Prophets should be read, and someone rises and makes a blessing. The haftarah was to be on the subject of what would have been read had it not been for the decree, that Shabbos’s portion; so that, “as if you read in the Torah,” the people would not forget what reading is. When the decree was lifted they returned to reading the Torah, and the haftarah remained.[25]

On this account there was never a custom of reading the haftarah every day, as Rashi has it, and the lecturer calls this too “a major machloket in Jewish history.” What then of the daily reading of the Prophets? The point made by the member of the congregation stands: ובא לציון גואל is a verse of Nevi’im, and a few other verses of the Prophets are said in the prayers, so that every Jew fulfils the duty of learning Nevi’im each day through them.

Why Twenty-One, and Why Seven?

Twenty-one, the lecturer explains, is seven aliyos with a minimum of three verses each (3 × 7). The reasoning is that the haftarah stands in the place of the reading that was forbidden, and so takes its measure from it: the missing reading was seven readers each reading at least three verses, and the substitute is of the same measure and of the same subject. The lecturer confirms the logic from Yom Tov, where the reading is shorter and there are fewer aliyos: there, fewer verses are needed. The requirement of twenty-one, as the Rema puts it, applies to Shabbos.

Why there are seven aliyos at all is a separate question. The Gemara in Megillah 23a gives several reasons, one of which is the seven officers who saw the king’s face at the beginning of the Megillah of Esther, “a very mystical piece of Gemara that nobody really understands.” The lecturer adds an idea from one of the Acharonim, which he thinks the Levush brings and which the Rishonim do not give: some people in earlier times came to shul only on Shabbos, villagers who had no minyan at home; having missed the Barchu of the week, they hear seven aliyos, each with a blessing, to make up for it.[26]

When Was the Decree? Elijah Levita and the Greek Kingdom

The Avudraham gives no date. The lecturer puts the question: when was there a decree that one could pray but not read from the Torah, though one could read from the Prophets? The answer, he says, is in the Sefer HaTishbi (ספר התשבי), written in the sixteenth century by Rabbi Eliyahu Bachur, Elijah Levita, and printed in his lifetime. According to the Tishbi, as the lecturer relays it, the decree was that of the Greek kingdom (malchus Yavan, מלכות יון), in the days of Antiochus, who ruled Eretz Yisrael and forbade the reading of the Torah each Shabbos. Since then, Levita writes, they read from the Prophets every week, and the enactment remained.[27]

Sidebar: Elijah Levita and the Christian Hebraists

Elijah Levita (אליהו בחור), whom the lecturer calls “more known by Christians than by Jews,” was born in Germany and lived in Italy in the sixteenth century, a brilliant man and one of the most prolific writers on Hebrew grammar. He taught Jews and, more importantly, the early Christian Hebraists: the priests and cardinals of the age who took up Hebrew and studied Tanach, Mishnah, Tosefta and Gemara. Some became real scholars, the lecturer says, and many Christian theologians afterward took Chazal and dressed them in Christian garb. Levita boarded in the house of one of the leading cardinals of his day, whose name the lecturer could not recall, edited his books, and taught him; he was unmarried (a bachur, whence his name). Another of his students, the lecturer remembers, was Sebastian Münster.[28]

The lecturer observes that teaching Torah to a non-Jew is forbidden (Sanhedrin 59a), and that Rav Moshe Feinstein ruled that where a rabbi teaches the Jewish public and a non-Jew happens to enter, there is no prohibition. Levita, he supposes, drew criticism in his own day, and it is hard to say what came of it: his students did not convert. Later Acharonim were not sure that all he wrote could be relied on, but it entered our literature and the Poskim.[29]

The Levush: Why Never Kesuvim

The Levush adds, the lecturer says, something “startling” that he does not understand: the decree forbade not only the Torah but also the Writings (Kesuvim, כתובים), and permitted only the Prophets. “Maybe the Greeks thought the Prophets were all right to read.” That, according to the Levush, is why a haftarah is never taken from Kesuvim, a rule that the lecturer says is found in a Gemara in Megillah “but nobody knows why.” The Levush, in the lecturer’s words, tells us why; “it’s all history.”[30]

IX. Two Accounts of One Reading

The lecturer does not say which account he prefers. Set side by side, they agree on one thing: the haftarah as read on Shabbos is a remnant. On Rashi’s account it is what remains of a daily study that people could no longer afford, kept on the one day when no one is rushing to work. On the Avudraham’s it is what remains of a substitute for a Torah reading that people were once forbidden to make, kept after the decree was lifted. The accounts need not exclude each other entirely. The lecturer shows that the verses of the Prophets already in the daily prayers, among them ובא לציון גואל, answer the duty of learning Nevi’im each day, which is how the Avudraham’s view meets Rashi’s. Both then lead to the haftarah as we have it, tied to the portion and to the day, as described in section VII. Which of the two is the older the lecturer does not decide; he presents both, as he says, as history.
