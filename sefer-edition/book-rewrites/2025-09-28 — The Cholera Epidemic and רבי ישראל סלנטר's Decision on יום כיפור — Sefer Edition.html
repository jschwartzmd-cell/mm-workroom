The Cholera Epidemic and Rabbi Yisrael Salanter’s Decision on Yom Kippur
Leadership, Halachic Process, and Pikuach Nefesh in Vilna, 5609 (1848)
28 September 2025  ·  Sefer Edition  ·  ו׳ תשרי תשפ״ו

Few episodes of nineteenth-century Lithuanian Torah (תורה) have been so widely told, or so sharply disputed, as the decision of Rabbi Yisrael Salanter (רבי ישראל סלנטר) on Yom Kippur (יום כיפור) during the cholera epidemic that struck Vilna in the autumn of 1848. The episode touches the deepest questions of halachic (הלכה) process: the authority of the beis din (בית דין), the duty of a rav (רב) to act without delay where life is at stake, and the boundary between pikuach nefesh (פיקוח נפש) and the integrity of communal halachah (הלכה).

The story survives in two versions that differ on the central facts. In the first, preserved in the Mekor Baruch (מקור ברוך) of Rabbi Baruch Epstein, the young Rabbi Yisrael rises in the main synagogue, announces that all must eat, and makes kiddush (קידוש) publicly on the bimah (בימה) until the congregation follows him. In the second, set down on the testimony of an eyewitness, he goes quietly from person to person with small measured portions, makes no kiddush, and does not eat himself. The discourse sets the versions side by side, with the questions each raises, and then turns to the defences that have been offered for the ruling.

Along the way it considers the life of Rabbi Yisrael as a gaon (גאון) before he became the father of the mussar (מוסר) movement; the sources of the story; the clash between a venerable dayan (דיין) and a young rabbi; the law of a sick person on Yom Kippur; the precedent of the Chasam Sofer (חתם סופר); and the later reception of the incident.

I. The gaon (גאון) Who Became a darshan (דרשן)

Rabbi Yisrael Salanter is remembered as the founder of the mussar (מוסר) movement, and it is sometimes assumed that he was therefore a man of middos (מידות) and tzidkus (צדקות), of unusual sensitivity in bein adam lachaveiro (בין אדם לחברו), and little more. The assumption is mistaken. He was a gaon of the first rank, brilliant in Talmud (תלמוד) and in lamdanus (למדנות); as a young bachur (בחור) and avreich (אברך) he was known first of all for his learning and not for his piety. His standing as a master of halachah (הלכה) is the necessary background to the episode of Yom Kippur (יום כיפור), for it explains both why a young rabbi could expect his ruling to be heeded and why a court of elders, confronted with a question on the laws of gittin (גיטין), found itself without an answer.

The turning point came early in his life. Under the influence of Rabbi Yosef Zundel of Salant (רבי יוסף זונדל מסלנט), a man of mussar, he changed the whole direction of his work. Instead of delivering shiurim (שיעורים) within the yeshivah (ישיבה) of Vilna, he turned outward and addressed the common people (המונים). He was an extraordinary orator and darshan, and the people flocked to hear him.

The Social Revolution in Vilna

The novelty of this can be appreciated only against the Vilna of the period, the “Jerusalem of Lithuania” (ירושלים דליטא). There a man was respected and valued if he was a lamdan (למדן) and a talmid (תלמיד); wealth, as always in Jewish communal life, also counted. But a man who was merely a decent Jew, who excelled in bein adam lachaveiro, who made others feel valued, who was God-fearing, went unnoticed. Rabbi Yisrael set out to change this. He wished to bring to the forefront of Jewish consciousness the ideal of the Jew who is careful not to embarrass or humiliate others. His influence was so great that several yeshivos (ישיבות) adopted his approach, and the mussar movement was born.

Opposition Within the Yeshivah World

A considerable number of the leading Torah scholars opposed his method. Excessive study of mussar in the yeshivos, they argued, would detract from lamdanus, from the study of Gemara (גמרא). Today the great majority of yeshivos include mussar in their programme, with one notable exception: Brisk. In the Brisk yeshivah in Jerusalem, in which the lecturer studied, there are no sifrei (ספרי) mussar on the shelves and no set time for mussar study; Brisk is described as the only yeshivah in the world that has kept the original opposition to this day.

An Aside — The Stroke and the Carpenter’s Bench

Late in life, about a decade before his death, Rabbi Yisrael suffered a stroke. He collapsed in the street and was paralysed for a time. Many legends surround the event. According to one, the physicians told him that he was thinking too much about learning, and prescribed two hours each day of activity that was not intellectual. What does a brilliant mind do for two hours when it is not learning? He apprenticed himself to a cabinetmaker (נגר) and became a skilled maker of cabinets (ארונות), working with his own hands for two hours daily. Occupying his mind in a different way, he recovered his health.

II. The Sources of the Story

The Making of a Gadol

The best-known modern treatment is The Making of a Gadol,[1] by Rabbi Nosson Kamenetsky (רבי נתן קמנצקי), son of Rabbi Yaakov Kamenetsky (רבי יעקב קמנצקי) and brother of Rabbi Shmuel Kamenetsky (רבי שמואל קמנצקי); he was a rosh yeshivah (ראש ישיבה) in Itri (איטרי) and an exceptional talmid chacham (תלמיד חכם). When the book appeared, about fifteen years before the discourse, many rabbis of the ultra-Orthodox world reacted with great severity. They placed it in cherem (חרם) and declared it forbidden to buy, and, naturally, it became a bestseller. The book is controversial because it records details not meant for public consumption, down to the fact that Rabbi Yisrael smoked. It is an academic work, and its reading is demanding. A lengthy discussion of the kiddush (קידוש) incident on Yom Kippur is found at p. 1136, with a further discussion, on the variation among the versions, at p. 1137.

Mekor Baruch of Rabbi Baruch Epstein

Rabbi Baruch Epstein (רבי ברוך אפשטיין)[2] was the son of the Aruch HaShulchan (ערוך השלחן) and nephew of the Netziv (נציב). He was the author of the Torah Temimah (תורה תמימה) and of Baruch SheAmar (ברוך שאמר), and of an autobiography, Mekor Baruch (מקור ברוך), later translated into English as My Uncle the Netziv. He was at once a gaon and talmid chacham and a wealthy banker, who managed one of the principal banks of Vilna. His book is a treasury of information about the Lithuanian Torah world, about the Netziv, about Rabbi Chaim of Brisk (רבי חיים בריסקר), and about his father. He visited America for three years in the 1920s. The English translation (1988) was abridged after complaints and has lost much of the flavour of the original; whoever wants the original must go to the Hebrew. His detailed account of the incident is the version most people know.

III. The Cholera Epidemic of 5609

The Medical Crisis

In 1848[3] a great cholera epidemic struck Eastern Europe. Cholera epidemics were frequent in the nineteenth century, mainly in Eastern Europe: in Poland, in Germany, in parts of Belarus and Russia. The Hebrew name was כולרא, understood as חולי רע, “the evil disease.” It is a terrible illness. The victim cannot digest food, vomits continuously, suffers severe diarrhoea, is rapidly dehydrated, and very quickly reaches a state of pikuach nefesh (פיקוח נפש).

Vilna Under Siege

The epidemic raged in Vilna precisely in the months of Elul (אלול) and Tishrei (תשרי). Vilna, the “Jerusalem of Lithuania,” was the foremost centre of Torah scholarship of that region, with countless talmidei chachamim (תלמידי חכמים), rabbis and authors of sefarim (ספרים). And the cholera struck in Elul and Tishrei.

The Communal Deliberation

Throughout the Aseres Yemei Teshuvah (עשרת ימי תשובה) everyone in Vilna spoke of one thing only. All the physicians of the city, religious Jewish doctors, non-religious Jewish doctors, and gentile doctors alike, said the same thing: on the medical knowledge of the time, a person who neither ate nor drank for twenty-four to twenty-seven hours became significantly more susceptible to the disease. (The disease, it may be added, spread through contaminated water.) If the body’s resistance was not strengthened, there was grave concern that a person would fall ill, and once he fell ill it was a matter of pikuach nefesh. People were falling like flies.

The memory of the COVID-19 epidemic lets the modern reader identify with the fear, but the cholera was much worse and far more died. In the cramped Jewish quarter of Vilna, where people lived one on top of another, the disease spread easily. The physicians said unanimously: there can be no fasting; fasting will make you vulnerable.

Every Jew in Vilna, every talmid chacham, every pious man, debated back and forth: should we eat or not? Many did not want to eat. Even healthy people who had not caught the disease did not want to eat. It seems that the physicians of the time did not understand what is now known, that such a disease has an incubation period of from two days to a week, so that a person may already be ill without showing symptoms.

IV. The Decision on Yom Kippur: The Version of Mekor Baruch

Rabbi Yisrael’s Bold Action

On Yom Kippur of that year Rabbi Yisrael went to the main synagogue of Vilna. He was then, on the lecturer’s reckoning, thirty-six or thirty-seven years old: a young man, well known in Vilna but very far from the senior position he later held. Present in the city were dayanim (דיינים) in their eighties, far more experienced, far better known, far more widely accepted. He rose on his own initiative, without consulting the senior dayanim of Vilna, so intent was he on saving lives.

As Mekor Baruch records, he declared in the name of the Torah (תורה), in the manner of the formula that is recited before Kol Nidrei (כל נדרי),[4]

על דעת המקום ועל דעת הקהל, אנו מתירים …
With the consent of the Omnipresent and with the consent of the congregation, we permit …

that all must eat and make kiddush on Yom Kippur at once. The sources differ on the details, and even on the date; the lecturer is concerned less with the date than with the halachic (הלכה) details, which vary from version to version.

The Congregation’s Response

When he gave this ruling the people did not move. One must picture the scene: a thousand or fifteen hundred Jews standing rooted to the spot, unable to bring themselves to act. Who eats on Yom Kippur? The barrier was psychological, and it could not be overcome by words alone. Rabbi Yisrael understood that leadership at such a moment demands more than instruction. He went up to the bimah (בימה), took from his pocket a bottle of wine and a bag of cake, made kiddush on Yom Kippur, and ate, there on the bimah, in front of the assembled congregation. When they saw their rabbi eat, they came up one by one to the bimah, where he had prepared in advance cake and cookies, and a “kiddush club” formed, each man making kiddush in turn.

This was personal leadership of the highest order. He exposed himself to risk, physically, by breaking the fast when perhaps he himself had no need to, and spiritually and socially, by acting first, visibly and irreversibly.

V. The Rebuke of the Elder dayan (דיין)

After Yom Kippur, the oldest and most respected dayan of Vilna, Rabbi Itzel HaKohen (רבי איצל הכהן), eighty years old, chief dayan of the city and one of the foremost Acharonim (אחרונים) of his generation, the author, as the lecturer describes him, of a work called Bikurim (ביכורים),[5] delivered a sharp rebuke. (It is a tragedy that the Lithuanian gedolim (גדולים) are insufficiently known today. The world of the yeshivos keeps their memory alive only through a few institutions, Ponevezh, Telz and Brisk; Lithuania was destroyed in the Holocaust, and many towering figures have been forgotten. Rabbi Itzel was among the greatest.)

His rebuke to the whole congregation was blunt:

“You all ate on Yom Kippur while healthy? What were you doing in synagogue? If you were sick you should have been at home, and eating there. If you came to synagogue, you were healthy, and you ate on Yom Kippur? חוצפה!”

His fundamental objection was to the halachic process. Rabbi Yisrael had not consulted the beis din (בית דין) before issuing so sweeping a permission. “You cannot cancel Yom Kippur for an entire congregation on the strength of your own apprehension (חשש). Whoever has not yet eaten must not eat. A person who feels faint or weak must consult a rabbi. Every case is a question (שאלה) in itself.”

The Institutional Picture

The dynamic is easily pictured. A rabbi in his thirties rises and tells everyone to eat on Yom Kippur; and the most venerable authority in the city, a man of eighty, publicly rebukes him. The lecturer draws the modern comparison: were he to do such a thing, and the leading gadol (גדול) of the generation, nearly ninety years old, were to rebuke him in public, he would become the laughingstock of the city. This was precisely the position of Rabbi Yisrael.

The Koidenover Chasidim

Vilna, though a heartland of Lithuanian Torah, had some Chasidim (חסידים). The Chasidim of the Koidenover Rebbe (רבי מקוידנוב), a dynasty of Chasidim that flourished before the war, heard of the incident and reported it to their Rebbe, who spoke against Rabbi Yisrael: how could he do such a thing? The Chasidim later ascribed the stroke that Rabbi Yisrael suffered to the Rebbe’s displeasure, his kefeidah (כפידה), a matter that in the Chasidic idiom is grave, for a mishap that befalls a man is attributed to his having wounded the feelings of a Rebbe.[6]

VI. The Clash of Two Principles

Two fundamental principles of the service of God collided here.

Pikuach nefesh supersedes everything. In a cholera epidemic the danger was real and immediate. Fasting could weaken people and make them more susceptible, and Rabbi Yisrael, seeing the emergency, acted decisively to save lives: ונשמרתם מאד לנפשתיכם.[7]

Halachic process and authority must be preserved. Rabbi Itzel represented the traditional structure: that major decisions affecting the entire community are brought before the beis din; that individual questions require individual rulings; that one may not unilaterally override established halachah, however good the intention.

The dispute was therefore not merely over a ruling. It was a defining moment in the understanding of what rabbinic leadership in a crisis looks like.

VII. The Summons to beis din and the Kashya on gittin

The Summons

Rabbi Itzel HaKohen made a great dispute (מחלוקת) of the incident. He summoned Rabbi Yisrael to the beis din after Yom Kippur, to apologise and to receive punishment. His argument was unsparing:

“What happened, happened because of you. People desecrated Yom Kippur because of you, and we must punish you. You should not have ruled on your own without first consulting the beis din. Had you come to us in Vilna and argued it out with us, perhaps we should have agreed, and announced before Yom Kippur that everyone should eat; you might have convinced us. But you did not come.”

This was an age in which the beis din wielded great authority, and everyone was bound to obey it. (The beis din has authority today also, but few, unfortunately, listen.) The court told him to choose his own punishment for not having asked first.

The Kashya on gittin

Rabbi Yisrael answered, in the famous version: “I shall choose my own punishment. But first permit me to ask a question.” He turned to the beis din, men much older than he, and said:

“You are great geonim (גאונים). I shall ask you a question (קושיא) that I guarantee none of you can answer. It concerns the laws of gittin. For three decades you have been writing gittin in a certain manner, and I have a question on the way you write the get (גט). According to my question, every get you have written for thirty years is invalid. Do you want to go there? I shall ask. If you have an answer, I shall accept the punishment. If not, nothing will happen to me.”

It is well known that he asked an extraordinarily powerful question on a ruling of one of the Acharonim, the very ruling on which gittin are still written today. The question stunned them, and no one could answer it. They said: “We did not realise that he was such a great man in halachah!” They understood that he stood on a higher level in learning than they, and that they had no right to question his instruction to eat on Yom Kippur.[8]

The question itself is not known. It may be taken as certain that either he had an answer in mind, or he held that the ruling had a good answer; for no gittin were in fact invalidated, and had any been, the result would have been a catastrophe of agunah (עגונה) cases, a general chaos (בלגן). He merely put the question, they could not answer, and they said: “We shall not punish him.”

VIII. Two Halachic Questions

A pair of halachic questions was put in the name of Rabbi Baruch (רבינו ברוך),[9] and they ought to trouble every reader.

The First Question — Why Not Less Than the Shiur?

The Shulchan Aruch (שולחן ערוך) devotes an entire chapter, siman (סימן) 618,[10] to the law of a sick person on Yom Kippur: דין חולה ביום הכפורים. Every year the most common question that a rabbi receives in the days before and on Yom Kippur is precisely this, because Yom Kippur is the only fast that is from the Torah itself and the most stringent of all fasts, and many sick persons, chas veshalom (חס ושלום), ask whether they must fast or are forbidden to.

The Shulchan Aruch is clear. If a sick person must eat, he is obligated to eat. The same God who commanded the fast tells him that if he is sick and needs to eat, he is commanded to eat. Many people become anxious at this, and it is difficult to tell a person: “You are doing the will of God.” But to fast when one is forbidden to fast is a great transgression, comparable to desecrating Yom Kippur itself, like kindling a fire on the holiest day. The few things that are not set aside for pikuach nefesh are the three cardinal sins, and this is not one of them. If the physician says that one must eat, one must eat.

The Shulchan Aruch adds a qualification: there is a way of eating on Yom Kippur. One eats less than the measure, and less again (פחות פחות מכשיעור), in small portions at intervals, so that the portions do not combine to the shiur (שיעור). For liquids the measure is a cheekful (מלא לוגמיו). As the lecturer describes the working method: one fills the mouth with liquid until the cheeks bulge, spits it into a cup, divides it in half, and drinks slightly less than that half. All depends on the size of one’s own cheeks, as the Gemara and the Shulchan Aruch say; some people have larger cheeks and some smaller. It is easier to measure with both cheeks than with one. The lecturer advises that the measure be worked out before Yom Kippur by one who may need it.

There is a great dispute as to how long one must wait between one portion of less than the measure and the next: some say two minutes and some nine. The lecturer advises that one who can be stringent should drink slightly less than that half-cheekful every nine minutes. (These are the lecturer’s practical remarks and are not a ruling for any individual; each case is put to a rabbi.)

The question is therefore sharp. This is not a new halachah. Why would Rabbi Yisrael permit all, even people who came to synagogue and appeared healthy, to eat as they pleased? He should have said: “Gentlemen, this is the measure. Each of you must measure.” If he held that they were all in a state of doubt as to danger to life (ספק פיקוח נפש), let them eat less than the measure. How could he abrogate, which is the correct word, the fast on Yom Kippur for the whole congregation, so that all might eat as they wished?

The Second Question — Why Kiddush?

The second question is more astonishing. The version of Mekor Baruch has Rabbi Yisrael make kiddush on Yom Kippur. Kiddush? Has anyone heard of making kiddush on Yom Kippur? Even a sick person who must eat does not make kiddush. It is held that when Yom Kippur falls on Shabbos (שבת) and a person is sick, then from the side of Shabbos he must make kiddush;[11] but on an ordinary Yom Kippur? One might say Yaaleh VeYavo (יעלה ויבוא), perhaps, on some view; but a complete kiddush would be a blessing said in vain (ברכה לבטלה). Something does not add up: no one in his right mind would make kiddush on Yom Kippur even if obliged to eat. The Sages instituted no kiddush for a sick person who eats by dispensation; at most one adds Yaaleh VeYavo, and the Name is mentioned in the prayers in any case.

The question is therefore twofold: why did Rabbi Yisrael not instruct the people to eat less than the measure, and what is the kiddush about?

IX. The Eyewitness Version: Shimon Strashun

The versions of the story differ widely, and this is why biographies of the gedolim are valuable: they show a figure from different angles. A Yiddish work, Gedolim fun Unzer Zeit (גדולים פון אונזער צייט, “Great Ones of Our Time”),[12] brings the testimony of an eyewitness, a Jew named Shimon Strashun (שמעון שטראשון), who was in that very synagogue in Vilna in 1848. (He is not to be confused with the famous Rashash (רש״ש), Shmuel Strashun, who also lived in Vilna at the time.) He says: it is a great mistake; the story was not like that at all. Stories take on a life of their own.

The Brisker Rav’s Aside

A Jew once came to the Brisker Rav (הרב מבריסק) and told him a story, and the Rav responded with a story of his own. As the man left the room the Brisker Rav turned to his children and said: “I guarantee you that by the time he is out of the door he has added more details to the story.” And so it was. By the time the man had gone out of the house, he had already embellished it. Stories decorate themselves.

The testimony of Strashun is as follows. Before the festival, Rabbi Yisrael prepared many small bags, there being no plastic bags in those days, only small bags; each held a thin slice of cake, less than the volume of an egg (כביצה), together with small cups, so that each person might measure for himself. There was very little grape juice or tea, or whatever was to be had.

In the middle of the prayers he went about the synagogue of Vilna. This is what a gadol does. He forgot his own prayer, his own Yom Kippur, his own self-perfection, and his own relation to the Holy One, blessed be He: he was concerned only with pikuach nefesh. He went from person to person, looked into their faces to see whether they were weak, and wherever he saw weakness he said at once: “Here, take this. Eat less than the measure. Say the blessing.”

There was no kiddush on the bimah. He went from person to person and told them to eat, without their having to seek out a rabbi to ask. “I am the rabbi,” he said, in effect; “I rule that you must eat,” and this only for those who were sick. And he himself did not eat at all.

Shalom Almanori (שלום עלמנורי),[13] in his book on those who died on Yom Kippur, gives the same version. The story, it is said, was so significant that even the Mishnah Berurah (משנה ברורה) and the Biur Halachah (ביאור הלכה) refer to it in passing. Almanori writes of the eyewitness: “He was right. Rabbi Yisrael did not make kiddush; there is no need to make kiddush. He did not abrogate the fast for everybody; it was only for the sick, and for those who felt themselves becoming sick.”

On this version the anger of the elderly beis din reduces to a much narrower complaint: “You should have sent the people to us. You should have sent them to the beis din. Why did you decide on their behalf, as though you alone were the rabbi?”

X. The Deepest Halachic Defence: The Chasid Shoteh

Why, then, did Rabbi Yisrael permit eating, even person by person, without first asking the beis din? Who gave him the right? What was the emergency measure (הוראת שעה) that justified it?

The Making of a Gadol opens up the question. Its author was the son of Rabbi Yaakov Kamenetsky, and Rabbi Yaakov had a friend, Rabbi David Leibowitz (רבי דוד ליבוביץ), a grandson of the brother of the Chofetz Chaim (חפץ חיים) on his mother’s side. The well-known Rabbi Henoch Leibowitz (רב חנוך ליבוביץ), associated with the Chofetz Chaim yeshivos in America (the lecturer calls him their founder), was his son.[14] Rabbi David told Rabbi Yaakov Kamenetsky: “I heard this story from the Chofetz Chaim himself.” The Chofetz Chaim was a child living in Vilna at the time of the epidemic: ten or twenty years old, according to whether he died at ninety-four or one hundred and four, which is a famous dispute, and a gap of a decade. His own father fell ill and died in that very epidemic.

What the Chofetz Chaim related was this. When Rabbi Itzel HaKohen summoned Rabbi Yisrael and said, “You had no right to rule in this way without asking the beis din,” Rabbi Yisrael answered, in addition to the matter of the question on gittin, which may also be true, by invoking a halachah of the sick person on Shabbos. The Jerusalem Talmud (ירושלמי) puts the case thus:[15] when one encounters pikuach nefesh, the man who runs first to a rabbi to ask before saving a life is “spilling blood” (שופך דמים), and the rabbi who is asked is shamed (מגונה). The Beis Yosef (בית יוסף) explains the Yerushalmi: a rabbi who has not stood up in the synagogue or in the city to announce publicly that where life is in question one does not ask but acts, has failed. It is not the fault of the man who asked that he asked; it is the rabbi’s fault that the message had not been issued.

The Gemara in Sotah (סוטה)[16] calls such a person a chasid shoteh (חסיד שוטה), a “pious fool”: a man who runs from a possible danger to life to ask his rabbi before saving it. The Tur (טור)[17] calls him one who spills blood.

In this light, the Chofetz Chaim’s tradition holds, Rabbi Yisrael’s defence was complete. Precisely because the beis din had not gone from synagogue to synagogue before Yom Kippur and announced unequivocally that fasting was forbidden to anyone who felt himself becoming weak, he could not afford to wait. Asking would itself have been a form of spilling blood.

An Incident in Shavel

Such things do happen. In the Lithuanian city of Shavel (שאוועל),[18] a man came to the rabbi, who stood in the front row, five minutes before the end of Neilah (נעילה) on Yom Kippur. “Rabbi, I feel weak. I must eat.” The rabbi took him by the hand and said: “It is only five minutes, friend. Calm down. What can five minutes do to you?” The man went back to his place and collapsed. It was not the only time that such a thing happened.

The Brisker Approach

Pikuach nefesh is no joke. The Torah knows what it is saying: one may be as stringent as one wishes in everything else, but where there is a doubt of a doubt of a doubt of life, one is extraordinarily lenient. The bar for pikuach nefesh is set very low. In Brisk it was set lower than the Shulchan Aruch: Rabbi Chaim of Brisk was more lenient still, a revolutionary position that most poskim (פוסקים) do not accept, and in Brisk, as the lecturer reports, they ate on the minor fast days even for a small headache.[19] The famous Brisker joke captures the attitude exactly.

The Famous Brisker Joke

A young man marries into a Brisker family. On the first fast day after the wedding, he comes home from the first seder (סדר) and sees the kitchen table bare. “Rebbetzin, nu, nu!” “What do you mean, ‘nu, nu’? It is a fast.” “Ah! In Brisk we are stringent about pikuach nefesh. We are frummer than the rest of the world. I do not feel well. Let us go to lunch.” She leaves the room, returns, and sets the table for two. “No!” he says. “But you told me that in Brisk one eats on a fast.” “Yes,” he says, “I do! But I am not going to be pious at your expense. You can fast.”

XI. The Chasam Sofer’s Earlier Precedent

The responsa of the Chasam Sofer[20] present a striking parallel. Twelve years earlier, in 1836, the same situation is said to have arisen in Pressburg (פרשבורג) during a cholera epidemic, and the Chasam Sofer was asked what to do. His ruling was essentially that of Rabbi Yisrael: not a blanket permission for all to eat as usual, but a direction that all eat less than the measure. Where the danger is established as certain, there is no question at all, and all must eat; the question is how, and the Chasam Sofer ruled: less than the measure.

The reasoning is presented thus. Take the established law of a pregnant woman (עוברה) on Yom Kippur. If she is healthy, but medical opinion holds that if she does not drink, dehydration could create a doubt as to danger to life for the unborn child (ולד) in her womb, she must drink; but not a full measure at once. She drinks small amounts, about every nine minutes, so that the doubt of danger to life is averted without breaking the fast more than necessary. How much more so where one’s own life, on the medical opinion of the day, may be in doubt by not eating: one eats, but only less than the measure.

XII. The bein adam lachaveiro Dimension: Rabbi Yaakov Kamenetsky

Rabbi Yaakov Kamenetsky discusses the incident in his Emes LeYaakov (אמת ליעקב),[21] and brings out an element that is rarely stressed. Rabbi Yisrael, he explains, refrained from approaching the beis din of Vilna not only on halachic grounds but on grounds of bein adam lachaveiro.

His reasoning was this. Had he gone to the beis din and asked, “What shall we do? There are people who may fall sick and perhaps die; how are we to proceed?” the very question would have been a public shaming of the court. It was the beis din who should have taken the initiative before Yom Kippur: to visit every synagogue, to post notices, and to proclaim: “The rabbi rules that it is forbidden to fast if you feel even slightly faint. If you are apprehensive (חושש) that you may fall ill, it is forbidden to fast.” They had not done so. Rabbi Yisrael did not wish to put them to shame in public by asking. Therefore, on Yom Kippur itself, seeing no alternative, he rose and decided on his own authority: not despite his sensitivity in bein adam lachaveiro, but because of it.

XIII. The Later Reception

The story spread far beyond Lithuania. Even in Jerusalem it was discussed at length, because the same cholera epidemic later struck the Land of Israel, and the same question arose. The Rishon LeTzion (ראשון לציון), the title by which the Sephardim call the chief rabbi of Jerusalem, Rabbi Chaim David Chazan (רבי חיים דוד חזן),[22] is reported to have said: “I know that some years ago in Europe a rabbi stood up in the synagogue and permitted everyone to eat. But here I shall not do this. How can one do this?” Everyone knew the story; it had become a defining narrative.

Fifty to a hundred years afterwards the Acharonim discussed the case at length. They argued the reasoning of those who held that the action was forbidden and the reasoning of those who held it permitted. Rabbi Baruch Ber Leibowitz (רבי ברוך בער ליבוביץ) of Kamenetz (קמניץ), one of the great roshei (ראשי) yeshivah of Lithuania, devoted an entire shiur to the subject in his yeshivah; he sided with Rabbi Itzel HaKohen and held the action forbidden, disagreeing with Rabbi Yisrael’s permission.[23]

XIV. Conclusion: The Consistency of the Ruling

Read together, the eyewitness version, the Chasam Sofer’s parallel ruling, and Rabbi Yaakov Kamenetsky’s insight in bein adam lachaveiro sharpen the picture. Rabbi Yisrael’s actual ruling, on the eyewitness account, to give small portions, less than the measure, only to those in danger or possible danger, is exactly what the Shulchan Aruch prescribes. Why should one eat more than the measure when it is unnecessary? The fast is broken only when one eats more than the measure, and a person who eats only less than the measure because he is permitted to has not broken it. On this account Rabbi Yisrael’s ruling was, in the lecturer’s estimate, entirely correct. That conclusion rests on the eyewitness version; on the version of Mekor Baruch, with a public kiddush and a blanket permission, the two questions of Section VIII remain.

[Editorial coda — not part of the delivered discourse.] The episode may be read as a study in the two sides of rabbinic leadership in a crisis: the bold, unilateral act that saves lives, and the process of consultation and authority on which the community’s halachic order depends. On the version of the eyewitness and of Rabbi Yaakov Kamenetsky, Rabbi Yisrael walked the narrow line between the two, with halachic precision and with unusual regard for the feelings of others.
