בָּרוּךְ שֵׁם כְּבוֹד מַלְכוּתוֹ לְעוֹלָם וָעֶד

The Whispered Line — On the Second Line of the Shema
Hilchos Krias Shema — Chol HaMoed Chanukah
Recorded 29 December 2024 (Asarah B'Teves week · Chanukah)  ·  Sefer Edition

L'ilui nishmas (לעילוי נשמת) the mother of Rav Chaim Adler, whose yahrtzeit (יאָרצייט) falls on Asarah b'Teves (עשרה בטבת). In her memory the shiur (שיעור) and seudah (סעודה) of that morning were dedicated by the kehillah (קהילה); in her memory this chapter, on the whispered line by which every Jew crowns the malchus (מלכות) of Heaven, is offered now.

A single line of the siddur (סידור) — whispered every morning and every evening of a Jew's life, and lifted to full voice only once each year — contains within it the deathbed testimony of Yaakov Avinu (יעקב אבינו), a machlokes (מחלוקת) between the Tzelach (צל״ח) and the Midrash (מדרש) that reorganizes the entire sugya (סוגיא), the startling psak (פסק) of an Italian posek (פוסק) whose name the world has forgotten, the Vilna Gaon (הגר״א)'s reading of the wings of the Malachei HaShareis (מלאכי השרת), and a bond to the miracle of Chanukah itself. This chapter follows that line, word by word, from its source to its kavanah (כוונה).

I. Hakdamah — Chanukah, and Hands That Do Not Slacken

Before entering the sugya (סוגיא) itself, a brief word is in order on the season in which the teaching was first delivered — for the Chanukah frame will return, at the close, as more than a coincidence of the calendar.

The Gemara (גמרא) designates Chanukah a yom ha'pagra (יום הפגרא) — a day of respite. But the Shelah HaKadosh (של״ה הקדוש),[1] in Maseches Chanukah (מסכת חנוכה), protests that the yeshivos which read this designation as license for bein ha'zmanim (בין הזמנים) have inverted the original minhag (מנהג). In the sedarim of earlier generations, the zman (זמן) that opened after Sukkos ran roughly two months, and Chanukah was the appointed period of chazarah (חזרה) upon those two months' limudim (לימודים) — a time not for the closing of the beis midrash (בית מדרש) but for the deepening of what had already been acquired within it.

The Bach (ב״ח)[2] adds a second layer. The Yevanim (יוונים) temporarily prevailed over the Chashmonaim (חשמונאים), he explains, because Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל)'s hands had grown slack in avodas Hashem (עבודת ה') — refyon yadayim (רפיון ידיים). Chanukah is therefore not the season of bittul Torah (ביטול תורה) but of its opposite: of hands strengthened, of avodah (עבודה) redoubled. Both readings converge on a single point. Chanukah is a light kindled precisely against slackness; a day of respite that is at the same time a call to renewed engagement. It is fitting, then, that the teaching which follows — on a line of the siddur whose central avodah is to hold the malchus of Heaven steadily upon one's lips — was first delivered in the week of Chanukah itself.

II. The Source — A Line That Is Not a Pasuk

The line Baruch Shem K'vod Malchuso L'Olam Va'ed (ברוך שם כבוד מלכותו לעולם ועד) occupies a peculiar station within the daily liturgy. All year it is whispered b'lachash (בלחש) immediately after Shema Yisrael (שמע ישראל); only on Yom Kippur (יום כיפור) is it raised to full voice. Where does it come from? Who composed it? It is not a pasuk (פסוק) — no such verse appears in Tanach (תנ״ך).

The Mishnah of the People of Yericho

The Mishnah (משנה) in Pesachim (פסחים)[3] records the local minhagim (מנהגים) of the people of Yericho (יריחו). Some Chazal (חז״ל) protested; others they permitted to stand. Among the practices they permitted was korchin es ha'Shema (כורכין את השמע) — the people of Yericho would "wrap" the Shema (שמע), joining Shema Yisrael directly to V'ahavta (ואהבת), omitting Baruch Shem altogether. Chazal's silence on this practice is not incidental. It will underwrite, five centuries later, one of the most startling piskei halachah (פסקי הלכה) in Hilchos Krias Shema (הלכות קריאת שמע).

The Deathbed of Yaakov Avinu

The Gemara (Pesachim 56a) then supplies the positive source. At the opening of Parshas Vayechi (פרשת ויחי),[4] Yaakov Avinu gathers his sons: Vayikra Yaakov el banav (ויקרא יעקב אל בניו). Rashi (רש״י) brings the midrash: Yaakov sought to reveal to his children the keitz (קץ) — the appointed date of the final geulah (גאולה). Later generations of chachamim (חכמים) would attempt such calculations and would meet the curse of the Gemara in Sanhedrin (סנהדרין):[5] tipach atzman shel mechashvei kitzin (תיפח עצמן של מחשבי קיצין). But Yaakov himself, on his deathbed, made the attempt.

At the very moment he was prepared to speak, nistalkah heimenu ru'ach ha'kodesh (נסתלקה הימנו רוח הקודש). The revelation was withheld; the knowledge withdrew. Yaakov feared the cause lay not in Heaven but among his own children. His grandfather Avraham had produced Yitzchak but also Yishmael; his father Yitzchak had produced Yaakov but also Eisav. Perhaps, among the twelve gathered before him, there stood a Yishmael or an Eisav whose presence had rendered the keitz unspeakable.

The shevatim (שבטים) answered as one:

שְׁמַע יִשְׂרָאֵל, ה' אֱלֹקֵינוּ, ה' אֶחָד.
Hear, Yisrael — the Lord is our God, the Lord is One.
Devarim 6:4, quoted here in the words of the shevatim as brought by the Gemara, Pesachim 56a

In this original setting, Shema Yisrael is heard in its most literal register. "Yisrael (ישראל)" is the address to Yaakov himself — to their father, whose name was Yisrael — and the declaration that follows is the answer his fear required. Hear, our father: as You are one, so are we; there is no Yishmael here, no Eisav; the House of Yaakov is undivided in its bond to the One.[6]

It is on account of this moment, the Gemara in Yevamos (יבמות) teaches, that Yaakov alone among the Avos (אבות) merits the description mitaso sheleimah (מיטתו שלמה) — his bed is whole. Reuvein bore his own ma'aseh (מעשה); Shimon and Levi bore Shechem (שכם); each of the twelve had his test. But upon the yichud Hashem (ייחוד ה'), all twelve stood as one.

Yaakov's Response

Hearing the declaration of his sons, Yaakov replied:

בָּרוּךְ שֵׁם כְּבוֹד מַלְכוּתוֹ לְעוֹלָם וָעֶד.
Blessed is the Name of the honor of His kingdom for ever and ever.

These are the first uttered breaths of the line. Baruch Shem is, in this sense, Yaakov's amen (אמן) upon his children's Shema — the father's response to the sons' declaration, sealing the yichud (ייחוד) that has just been made whole among them.

III. The Halachic Tension — Yaakov and Moshe

From the moment Yaakov's amen is claimed as the source of the line, a structural difficulty presents itself. Two obligations pull in opposite directions:

  • Yaakov Avinu uttered these words — and it is not fitting to say Shema in the presence of the Shechinah (שכינה) and pass over the words with which Yaakov himself sealed it. (So Rashi.)
  • Moshe Rabbeinu (משה רבינו) did not include this line in the parshah (פרשה) of Shema as transmitted in V'eschanan (ואתחנן).[7] To insert Yaakov's line between Shema Yisrael and V'ahavta would constitute a hefsek (הפסק) in the recitation of Krias Shema (קריאת שמע) — a forbidden interruption.

The resolution of Chazal: b'lachash. What Moshe did not write, one does not say aloud within the Shema; what Yaakov spoke, one does not entirely suppress. The line is uttered under the breath — silence-as-speech, a whispered amen smuggled between the words of Moshe.

The Mashal of Tzikei K'deirah

The Gemara illustrates the dynamic with a mashal (משל):

To what may the matter be compared? To the daughter of a king who smelled tzikei k'deirah — the fragrant crust that clings to the bottom of the pot. If she were to ask for it openly it would be a disgrace to her station; if she were to forgo it, she would suffer. So her servants brought her some in secret.
Pesachim 56a

So too here. The praise contained in Baruch Shem is of such weight that it cannot be voiced openly within the frame of the Krias Shema that Moshe fixed — and yet it cannot be renounced. The bas melech (בת מלך) receives her portion in the inner chamber; Klal Yisrael receives its Baruch Shem under the breath.

IV. The Tzelach — A Din in Krias Shema, Not in the Words

The Author

The Tzelach — an acronym for Tziyon L'Nefesh Chayah (ציון לנפש חיה) — is the commentary of the Noda B'Yehudah (נודע ביהודה), Rabbeinu Yechezkel HaLevi Segal Landau (רבינו יחזקאל הלוי סג״ל לנדא), Av Beis Din (אב״ד) of Prague in the eighteenth century. The name of the sefer (ספר) is itself an act of filial devotion: Chayah (חיה) was his mother, and the work was composed l'zecher nishmas (לזכר נשמת) her. He may have been the first author to name a sefer for his mother; the practice, once introduced, has been imitated by generations of mechabrim (מחברים) since.

The Chiddush

On Pesachim 56a the Tzelach[8] advances a reframing that reorganizes the entire sugya. The problem, he argues, is not that the words Baruch Shem are intrinsically forbidden aloud. Yaakov's words are wonderful in themselves; the bas melech mashal describes their weight but does not forbid their utterance. The problem is hefsek b'Krias Shema. To speak aloud between Shema Yisrael and V'ahavta — a parshah Moshe fixed as continuous — would be a forbidden interruption. Hence the eitzah (עצה) of b'lachash: a permitted, near-silent form of speech that satisfies both obligations at once, but which is required specifically within the frame of the Shema itself.

The Practical Nafka Mina

The consequence follows immediately. Wherever Baruch Shem is said outside the Shema, there is no issur (איסור) at all. It may be said b'kol ram (בקול רם):

  • On Yom Kippur at the close of Ne'ilah (נעילה): Shema Yisrael once, Baruch Shem three times, Hashem Hu HaElokim (ה' הוא האלקים) seven times — all aloud.
  • On Yom Kippur Katan (יום כיפור קטן) at the close of erev Rosh Chodesh (ערב ראש חודש) — as recited by certain kehillos (קהילות), though the practice varies from community to community.
  • After the accidental utterance of a brachah l'vatalah (ברכה לבטלה), as a form of tikun (תיקון).
  • In minhag Ashkenaz (מנהג אשכנז), after the second brachah (ברכה) on tefillin (תפילין).

The Kloyz Incident

The Tzelach himself provides the living illustration. He recounts that he arrived one erev Rosh Chodesh at the Kloyz (קלויז) in Prague for Yom Kippur Katan and instructed the tzibbur (ציבור) to raise Baruch Shem to full voice. The olam (עולם) was scandalized. He calmed them: the din (דין) of b'lachash, he explained, is a din b'Krias Shema; the words themselves bear no such restriction. Outside the Shema, the heter (היתר) is full.

V. The Midrash — A Different Yesod

Against the Tzelach's framework stands a midrash[9] on Parshas V'eschanan that grounds the whispering in an entirely different yesod (יסוד).

Moshe Rabbeinu ascended to receive the Torah. He heard the malachei ha'shareis saying Baruch Shem K'vod Malchuso L'Olam Va'ed. The praise pleased him. He said: I will bring this down to my talmidim. He descended, taught it to Yisrael, and warned them: Do not utter it aloud, for the malachim would grow jealous.
Devarim Rabbah on Parshas V'eschanan; cf. Rashi on Devarim 6:4

The image is startling and requires care. The teaching is not that the malachim (מלאכים) suffer human emotions; it is that the words of Baruch Shem pertain to a level of avodah that is proper to the malachim and, when uttered aloud in the physical world, encroach upon a domain not fully ours. The b'lachash, on this reading, is not a din local to the Krias Shema but a permanent condition of these words in any setting.

Why Yom Kippur Is the Exception

Once each year, on Yom Kippur, Klal Yisrael is k'malachim (כמלאכים): fasting, standing in kittel (קיטל), not bound to the physical needs that mark our ordinary station. On that day — and only on that day — the words may be raised to full voice, because the tzibbur has entered the register in which they properly belong.

The Nafka Mina Between the Two Yesodos

The machlokes is not merely conceptual. The two yesodos generate a real difference in practice. On the Tzelach's reading, the issur b'kol ram is a din in Krias Shema alone; outside that frame, aloud is fully permitted. On the Midrash's reading, the issur is a permanent property of the words themselves — permitted aloud only on Yom Kippur, when the station of the reciter has changed.

The Mishnah Berurah (משנה ברורה), in its practical piskei halachah, effectively follows the Tzelach: Baruch Shem after a brachah l'vatalah, after the second brachah of tefillin, and at Yom Kippur Katan, is said in the ordinary tone of speech, not b'lachash. The Midrash's yesod is preserved, however, in the machshavah (מחשבה): it is what supplies the kavanah content of the whispered recitation within Krias Shema, and it is why the words feel weightier than the technical din of hefsek would alone predict.

VI. Rav Sholom Shvadron — Where the Mind Is

Rav Sholom Shvadron, the Yerushalayimer maggid (ירושלמער מגיד),[10] sharpened the Midrash's framework with a question of remarkable precision. On Yom Kippur we recite Baruch Shem aloud because we are k'malachim — well and good. But consider the timing. At Kol Nidrei (כל נדרי), the tzibbur is bloated from the seudah ha'mafsekes (סעודה המפסקת) and heavy with water taken in anticipation of the fast; the twenty-five hours ahead promise the ordinary embarrassments of the body. Precisely then we say Baruch Shem aloud?

And at the reverse pole — at Maariv (מעריב) of Motzaei Yom Kippur (מוצאי יום כיפור), when the tzibbur has stood twenty-six hours in kittel, has taken neither food nor drink, and is at the physical peak of its tahoros (טהרות) for the year — we return the line to b'lachash. The body would suggest the reverse timing.

The Answer

Rav Shvadron answered: the malach (מלאך)-state does not reside in the body. It resides in the machshavah.

At Kol Nidrei, the body is heavy, but the machshavah is fixed on teshuvah (תשובה) and on the great gift of the day about to open — the gift that the Gemara in Shabbos (שבת) calls the treasure stored in the King's hidden chamber:[11] matanah tovah yesh li b'beis genazai (מתנה טובה יש לי בבית גנזי). In that orientation of mind, a person is k'malach. At Motzaei Yom Kippur, by contrast, the body has been purified, but the machshavah is on the coffee, the marble cake, the return to the ordinary. In that orientation, the malach-state has already departed — whatever the body's condition may report.

The halachah (הלכה) tracks the machshavah. Where the head is, there the man is. It is a piece of practical mussar (מוסר) the reader will find, once received, inseparable from every future Ne'ilah.

VII. Rabbeinu Yehoshua Boaz — The Shiltei Giborim

An Italian Posek Almost No One Names

The Bach[12] in Hilchos Krias Shema introduces a startling psak in the name of Rabbeinu Yehoshua Boaz (רבינו יהושע בועז), an Italian posek who was niftar (נפטר) in the year 5315 (1555). His name is unfamiliar to most, but his fingerprints appear on nearly every open page of Talmud Bavli (תלמוד בבלי) that a Jew opens today.

Three Quiet Monumental Contributions

Upon the margins of every standard daf (דף) stand three apparatuses, each the work of a single hand:

  • Mesoras HaShas (מסורת הש״ס) — the cross-references to parallel sugyos (סוגיות) throughout Shas (ש״ס);
  • Torah Or (תורה אור) — the precise identification of every pasuk the Gemara cites;
  • Ein Mishpat / Ner Mitzvah (עין משפט / נר מצוה) — the citation of the corresponding halachos (הלכות) in the Rambam (רמב״ם), the Tur (טור), and the Shulchan Aruch (שולחן ערוך).

All three were composed by Rabbeinu Yehoshua Boaz. Prior to his labor, a learner had no printed cross-reference for the sources upon which the Gemara drew or the halachos to which it flowed; one depended on bekius (בקיאות) and on memory. His bekius was so encompassing that he was able to prepare these apparatuses for the whole of Shas; his humility, so complete that later generations have inherited his work daily without so much as knowing his name.

The Heter for Sheitlach

The same posek, less commonly recognized, was also the first to permit a sheitel (שייטל) for a married woman. The issur at issue is se'ar b'ishah ervah (שער באשה ערוה)[13] — the Gemara's teaching that a married woman's own hair carries the status of ervah (ערוה). Whether that status extends to a wig fashioned from another woman's hair is a genuine question in halachah; Rabbeinu Yehoshua Boaz answered it in the permissive direction, and the Rema (רמ״א) and many acharonim (אחרונים) followed him. Much of contemporary Ashkenazi (אשכנזי) practice rests directly on his heter; certain Sefardishe (ספרדישע) batei din (בתי דין) today have taken the stricter side. Here too, the anonymity of the matir (מתיר) is instructive: the household practice of much of Klal Yisrael rests on his psak, and few of those it serves are aware of the man they are relying upon.

His Psak on Baruch Shem

The Bach brings Rabbeinu Yehoshua Boaz on a psak in Hilchos Krias Shema of comparable force. He is medayek (מדייק) from the Rambam that the chiyuv (חיוב) to recite Baruch Shem is not a takanah (תקנה) but a minhag. The Rambam's lashon (לשון), in Hilchos Krias Shema,[14] does not describe a formal enactment; it describes a received practice. From this the Shiltei Giborim (שלטי גיבורים) draws the sharp consequence: if a person omitted Baruch Shem — even l'chatchilah (לכתחילה), in the manner of the people of Yericho — he need not return to it. The line is minhag b'almah (מנהג בעלמא); the omission is not the end of the world.

VIII. The Kabbalistic Counter-View

The Kabbalistic tradition speaks with a different voice. To the Mekubalim (מקובלים), Baruch Shem is not minhag b'almah; it is inseparable from Krias Shema itself.

Nefesh HaChaim and Tanya

A word of context is necessary. Rav Chaim of Volozhin was the talmid muvhak (תלמיד מובהק) of the Vilna Gaon; the Baal HaTanya (בעל התניא) was the head of the emerging chassidus (חסידות), then in the years of the Vilna cherem (חרם). The Tanya (תניא) sets out the chassidic hashkafah (השקפה); the Nefesh HaChaim (נפש החיים) was composed, in part, as an answer to it. The two sefarim (ספרים) share much; they also diverge on many fine distinctions, and one who wishes to see the machlokes whole must learn both.

On the station of Baruch Shem, however, they converge. The Nefesh HaChaim[15] treats the line at length as the essential link between yichuda ila'ah (יחודא עילאה) — the upper unification, declared in Shema Yisrael — and yichuda tata'ah (יחודא תתאה) — the lower unification, by which the upper is drawn into the world of the reciter. The Baal HaTanya, in Sha'ar HaYichud V'HaEmunah (שער היחוד והאמונה),[16] teaches the same: Baruch Shem is part and parcel of Krias Shema, without which the declaration of the Shema remains, so to speak, incomplete.

How the Two Views Can Both Stand

How, then, can the Shiltei Giborim permit skipping what the mekubalim hold essential? The resolution the sefarim point to runs along the line between nigleh (נגלה) and nistar (נסתר). In the plane of revealed halachah, the chiyuv is a minhag, and its omission carries no obligation of chazarah. In the plane of penimiyus (פנימיות), the recitation is essential, and its omission leaves the yichud of Shema without its intended completion in this world. Both are true; each on its own level. A person who omitted the line has fulfilled the letter of the halachah and has forgone its inner form — a real loss, but not one that reopens the chiyuv of Krias Shema.

IX. Minhag or Takanah

Because so much halachic weight rests on this distinction, it deserves its own moment. The lashon "tiknu" (תיקנו) appears in the Gemara for many practices that later poskim nevertheless categorize as minhag rather than takanah. A takanah, in its strict sense, requires formal enactment — typically by a recognized beis din whose authority extends across Klal Yisrael. A minhag, by contrast, acquires its force through settled practice, absorbed into the halachic body through hispashtus (התפשטות) across communities and generations. The two carry different consequences: the omission of a takanah often obligates chazarah; the omission of a minhag ordinarily does not.

The Bach, following the Shiltei Giborim, paskens that Baruch Shem falls in the second category. The Mishnah Berurah[17] concurs: no chazarah is required for its omission. But the same Mishnah Berurah then rules that if the line was recited without kavanah, one does return and repeat it. The acharonim ask the obvious kushya (קושיא): if the line may be skipped l'chatchilah, why should an unintentional recitation obligate chazarah?

The Biur Halachah (ביאור הלכה) does not spell out the answer. A possibility that suggests itself — offered here in the humble register of proposal, not as the position of any earlier acharon — is that once the words have crossed the lips, the recitation acquires a status of its own, distinct from the chiyuv that first prompted it. Something analogous, in principle, to the brachah l'vatalah dynamic, though not identical to it: an act of speech directed toward Heaven, once undertaken, must be undertaken properly, whether or not the underlying chiyuv compelled the speaking. The suggestion is offered for further study; the acharonim's silence on the question leaves the field open.

X. Where the Hefsek Belongs

The Shulchan Aruch[18] requires a hefsek — a brief pause — between Shema Yisrael and V'ahavta. But where? Two shitos are offered:

  1. Pause after Shema Yisrael... echad; recite Baruch Shem...; pause again; continue with V'ahavta.
  2. Recite Shema Yisrael... echad and Baruch Shem... together as a single unit of kabbalas ol malchus shamayim (קבלת עול מלכות שמים); pause once; continue.

The second shitah reflects the Mekubalim's view that Baruch Shem is itself part of the acceptance of the yoke of Heaven; the hefsek therefore belongs after it, not before. Standard Nusach Ashkenaz (נוסח אשכנז) siddurim (סידורים) typically render the line without a comma between echad and Baruch Shem, aligning with this second shitah; the practical minhag in most Ashkenazi batei k'nesses (בתי כנסת) today is a single, brief pause after Baruch Shem before entering V'ahavta.

The Ben Ish Chai on Covering the Eyes

The Ben Ish Chai (בן איש חי),[19] teaching from the Mekubalim's side, holds that the hand which covers the eyes for Shema should remain in place through Baruch Shem. In practice, most communities — including many Sephardim (ספרדים) — do not extend the covering that far. The divergence is instructive. Where practice does not extend the covering, it reveals, in the language of custom itself, that Baruch Shem has not been fully integrated with Shema as one continuous act. The Mekubalim's theoretical claim and the tzibbur's bodily practice have drifted apart; the Ben Ish Chai's psak stands as an invitation to draw them back together.

XI. The Vilna Gaon — Six Wings and Four

The Stirah in Chagigah

The Gemara in Chagigah (חגיגה)[20] raises a stirah (סתירה) concerning the wings of the malachim. One pasuk[21] in Yeshayahu (ישעיהו) describes the serafim (שרפים) as bearing six wings apiece; another,[22] in Yechezkel (יחזקאל), describes the chayos ha'kodesh (חיות הקודש) as bearing four. The Gemara resolves:

כָּאן בִּזְמַן שֶׁבֵּית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ קַיָּם — שֵׁשׁ; כָּאן בִּזְמַן שֶׁאֵין בֵּית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ קַיָּם — אַרְבַּע.
Here — in the time when the Beis HaMikdash stood — six. Here — in the time when the Beis HaMikdash does not stand — four.
Chagigah 13b

Six Words on Six Wings

The Vilna Gaon[23] reads this stirah through the teaching of the Arizal (אריז״ל).[24] Upon the wings of the malachim, the Arizal teaches, are inscribed the six words of the line:

בָּרוּךְ    שֵׁם    כְּבוֹד    מַלְכוּתוֹ    לְעוֹלָם    וָעֶד.

When the Beis HaMikdash (בית המקדש) stood, all six wings were revealed and all six words could be read. After the churban (חורבן), the two middle wings — those bearing the words k'vod malchuso (כבוד מלכותו) — were folded under, in the manner of a bird that draws its wings against its body. Four remain to be seen; two are hidden. It is the k'vod malchus itself, precisely, that has become unseen.

Galei K'vod Malchuscha

This gives the Yom Tov (יום טוב) tefillah (תפילה) its exact meaning:

גַּלֵּה כְּבוֹד מַלְכוּתְךָ עָלֵינוּ, וְהוֹפַע!
Reveal Your k'vod malchus upon us, and appear!

The petition is not general; it is specific. We are asking that the two folded wings be unfurled; that the two hidden words be made visible once more; that the k'vod malchus which the churban withdrew be returned to the sight of all creation.

The True Kavanah

The Gaon's vort (ווארט) is not an ornament of learning; it is the operating kavanah of the whispered line. When a Jew says Baruch Shem, the sefarim teach, his kavanah is that the k'vod malchuso of the Holy One be nisgaleh la'kol (נתגלה לכל) — drawn out of hiddenness and displayed to all. That request, running under the breath twice daily across the whole of a Jew's life, is what the line most deeply is.

XII. Chanukah — The K'vod Malchus Made Visible

Here the Chanukah frame with which this chapter opened returns as more than a matter of season. The Bach's reading — that the Yevanim prevailed on account of refyon yadayim — can now be heard alongside the Vilna Gaon's teaching on the folded wings. What the churban hid, the Chashmonaim's victory, in its moment, restored to sight. Chanukah is a brief unfurling.

The verse in Tehillim (תהילים) gives the theme its most concentrated form:

אֵלֶּה בָרֶכֶב וְאֵלֶּה בַסּוּסִים, וַאֲנַחְנוּ בְּשֵׁם ה' אֱלֹקֵינוּ נַזְכִּיר.
These with the chariot, these with horses — and we, with the Name of Hashem our God we will call.
Tehillim 20:8[25]

The Yevanim came with rechev (רכב), susim (סוסים), and the entire koach ha'gashmi (כח הגשמי) of empire. The Chashmonaim came with k'vod malchuso alone — the folded wings briefly unfurled; the hidden two words briefly voiced. On Chanukah we saw what we ordinarily only whisper for; the yearning of every Baruch Shem received, for a moment, its answer. When we take up the whispered line again in the days that follow the chag (חג), we are asking that the unfurling not have been a moment only.

XIII. Targum Yerushalmi — A Sharper Emphasis

The Targum Yerushalmi (תרגום ירושלמי)[26] on Devarim (דברים) 6:4 — a fragmentary targum (תרגום) that survives in pieces alongside Onkelos (אונקלוס) and Yonasan (יונתן) — brings the same deathbed midrash in a slightly different key. In its version, all twelve shevatim uttered Shema Yisrael, and Yaakov, in reply, answered Baruch Shem. The framing is not merely narrative; it is structural. The line is response to a declaration — call and answer, address and reply — and the tefillah that has come down to us preserves the shape of that first exchange.

XIV. Rav Asher Weiss — The Outlet for the Whispered Line

Rav Asher Weiss, in Minchas Asher (מנחת אשר),[27] observes that a Yiddishe neshamah (אידישע נשמה) is not content with the whisper. Every year the tzibbur waits for the moment at the close of Ne'ilah when Baruch Shem may be lifted three times to full voice; the anticipation of that moment is real, and it reflects an urge that is real. What becomes of that urge in the ordinary year, when the halachah holds the line to b'lachash?

Rav Weiss answers: it is channeled into Amen Yehei Shemei Rabbah (אמן יהא שמיה רבה). The Gemara in Shabbos[28] teaches:

כָּל הָעוֹנֶה אָמֵן יְהֵא שְׁמֵיהּ רַבָּא בְּכָל כֹּחוֹ — קוֹרְעִין לוֹ גְּזַר דִּינוֹ.
Whoever answers Amen Yehei Shemei Rabbah with all his strength — his evil decree is torn up.

The tochen (תוכן) of Amen Yehei Shemei Rabbah is the same as the tochen of Baruch Shem: the declaration that the great Name of Hashem be blessed and elevated forever. What may not be raised in one setting is licensed — encouraged, in the words of the Gemara — in the other. The same energy of praise, denied its outlet within Krias Shema, finds its full-voiced expression in the response of Kaddish (קדיש).

XV. The Practical Kavanah

Two kavanos (כוונות) are set before the reciter, one pashut (פשוט) and one deep. Both are legitimate; the first is fully sufficient for the halachic requirement, and the second, for those able to enter it, adds the inner form.

The Pashut Kavanah (Aruch HaShulchan)

The Aruch HaShulchan (ערוך השולחן)[29] frames the kavanah pashutah (כוונה פשוטה) in three movements:

  • The yichud Hashem just declared in Shema Yisrael —
  • should endure — l'olam va'ed (לעולם ועד) —
  • and should be brought out of hiddenness into revelation, that the k'vod malchuso now folded away be shown to all creation, as taught by the Vilna Gaon.

Held even briefly at the moment of recitation, this kavanah satisfies the poskim (פוסקים)'s requirement in full.

The Deeper Kavanah

The Kabbalistic sefarim map a further kavanah: the declaration of yichuda ila'ah in Shema Yisrael and its drawing down as yichuda tata'ah in Baruch Shem; the movement by which the upper unification is received into the lower worlds and becomes real for the one who has just declared it. The interested reader is directed to Nefesh HaChaim Sha'ar (שער) 3, perakim (פרקים) 11–14, and to the Sha'ar HaYichud V'HaEmunah of the Tanya, where these matters are set out at length. For the reciter who does not enter these kavanos in detail, the pashut kavanah is enough — and it is enough, precisely, because the Vilna Gaon's vort has already given it real content to hold.

XVI. Closing

The whispered line, then, is not a mumble. Under the breath, twice each day, a Jew utters what the malachim bear upon their wings and asks that the two folded wings — the k'vod malchus hidden since the churban — be unfurled once more. What the Chashmonaim saw on Chanukah, the reciter petitions for on every ordinary morning; what Yaakov Avinu sealed on his deathbed with his sons, the reciter renews with his own children of a moment, the words Shema and Baruch Shem passing his lips in the same breath.

The yichud Hashem declared in Shema is meant to endure. That is the l'olam va'ed. And it is meant to grow — to move from the whispered chamber of the bas melech to the open courts of a world in which k'vod malchuso is at last unhidden. That is the kavanah we have been given.

Galei k'vod malchuscha is precisely the petition for those two folded wings to be unfurled again. ↩
  24. Arizal, Sha'ar HaKavanos, Inyan Krias Shema; Pri Etz Chaim. — The source for the inscription of the six words of Baruch Shem on the six wings of the malachim.   25. Tehillim 20:8. — אֵלֶּה בָרֶכֶב וְאֵלֶּה בַסּוּסִים, וַאֲנַחְנוּ בְּשֵׁם ה' אֱלֹקֵינוּ נַזְכִּיר. The Chanukah connection: the Yevanim came with the koach ha'gashmi; the Chashmonaim, with the Name alone. The victory was itself, in the Vilna Gaon's language, a brief unfurling of the folded wings — a moment in which the k'vod malchuso daily petitioned for was seen. ↩
  26. Targum Yerushalmi on Devarim 6:4 (the Fragmentary Targum). — An alternative version of the deathbed midrash: all twelve shevatim uttered Shema Yisrael; Yaakov, in reply, answered Baruch Shem. Sharper emphasis on the shape of tefillah as call and response — the structure preserved in the tefillah today. ↩
  27. Rav Asher Weiss, Minchas Asher al haTorah, Parshas V'eschanan. — The vort: the neshamah's urge to lift Baruch Shem to full voice, denied within Krias Shema for all but the Yom Kippur Ne'ilah, finds its licensed outlet in the loud response of Amen Yehei Shemei Rabbah. The tochen is the same praise; the vessel is different. ↩
  28. Shabbos קי״ט ע״ב (119b). — כָּל הָעוֹנֶה אָמֵן יְהֵא שְׁמֵיהּ רַבָּא בְּכָל כֹּחוֹ — קוֹרְעִין לוֹ גְּזַר דִּינוֹ. The Talmudic anchor for Rav Asher Weiss's reading: the explicit encouragement of full-strength response on Amen Yehei Shemei Rabbah, which becomes the licensed channel for the Baruch Shem impulse. ↩

Aruch HaShulchan, Orach Chaim 61. — The kavanah pashutah: the yichud Hashem declared in Shema Yisrael should endure — l'olam va'ed — and should be brought out of hiddenness into revelation, per the Vilna Gaon's reading. Sufficient to satisfy the poskim's requirement of kavanah when reciting Baruch Shem, without entering the deeper kabbalistic kavanos of yichuda ila'ah and yichuda tata'ah. ↩
