בָרוך׳ שֶאָמַר׳ ומִזמור׳ לְתודָה

Baruch She'amar and Mizmor L'Todah
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Sunday Morning Series on Tefillah
Recorded 11 August 2024 (Zayin Av)  ·  Reworked 30 April 2026  ·  Mareh Mekomos upgraded 5 May 2026  ·  Sefer Edition · Revised 10 August 2026

The mysterious origin of Baruch She'amar; the halachic implications of its non-Talmudic source; and the daily korban todah embodied in Mizmor L'Todah.

I. The Date — Zayin Av and the Three Days of Mourning

This shiur is delivered on the seventh of Av. In Ashkenazic tradition, the seventh, eighth, and ninth of Av were known as the shloshes yemei aveilah (שלושת ימי אבילה) — the three days of mourning — a play on the shloshes yemei afeilah (שלושת ימי אפילה), the three days of darkness in Mitzrayim.

On Zayin Av, Nevuzaradan — Rav HaTabachim, the chief executioner of Nevuchadnetzar — breached the walls of the Beis HaMikdash. For three full days he and his forces desecrated the sanctuary, partaking of its sacred precincts in an act of profound desecration.[1] Only on Tisha B'Av itself was the fire ignited, and the Beis HaMikdash burned through the tenth.

Nevuzaradan and the Bubbling Blood

The Gemara[2] records the astonishing aftermath. After the murder of the navi Zechariah, the prophet's blood would not stop bubbling on the floor of the Beis HaMikdash. Nevuzaradan tried to silence it; in a desperate, escalating frenzy he slaughtered eighty thousand kohanim upon it. Only then did the blood finally calm. Confronted with the magnitude of what he had witnessed — the blood of a murdered prophet, ceaselessly boiling on the sanctuary floor for decades — he was so utterly shaken that he converted: he became a ger tzedek. The Gemara states this in three short words; the theological weight is immense. It is among the more enigmatic pieces of Aggadah in Shas, and worthy of contemplation on Tisha B'Av.

Pogroms of Av

This is also a date that recurs in our martyrology. In the late 1300s, a horrific pogrom struck Seville, Spain — four thousand Jews were murdered al Kiddush Hashem, including the rebbetzin of the Rosh (the Rishon R' Asher b. Yechiel) and the daughter-in-law of the Ba'al HaTurim. These tragedies are mourned collectively on Tisha B'Av; the kinos hold that additional formal days of mourning are not multiplied, but each loss is absorbed into the central aveilus of the Churban.

II. Baruch She'amar — The Heavenly Note

Baruch She'amar has perhaps the strangest origin story in the entire siddur: a petek min ha'shamayim (פתק מן השמים) — a note that fell from Heaven with its full text. The tradition is transmitted by the Tur[3] as halacha l'ma'aseh, and the Taz reaffirms it. The Anshei Knesses HaGedolah saw the note and were mesaken the brachah as part of Pesukei d'Zimra.

Why Baruch She'amar Is Said Standing

Some Rishonim derive the practice of standing for Baruch She'amar from this very origin: because of the chashivus of a tefillah that came directly from Shamayim, the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah instituted that it be recited standing. This ruling has become accepted practice.

III. The Halachic Problem — Saying a Brachah Not in Chazal

The Pri Chadash's Sharp Question

The Pri Chadash — Rabbi Chizkiyahu di Silva, late-1600s Chief Rabbi of Yerushalayim, whose peirush on the Tur is printed in the back of standard editions — raises a fundamental difficulty.[4] There is a clear rule: one does not recite a brachah that does not appear in Chazal. Baruch She'amar is not in the Bavli or the Yerushalmi; it is mentioned by name in the Zohar HaKadosh, but as a brachah with Sheim u'Malchus — invoking Hashem's name — its recitation carries the risk of a brachah l'vatalah.

The Pri Megadim's Resolution

The Pri Megadim resolves it: when the Tur records that the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah received the petek from heaven, that implies a kabbalah on this brachah predating the Mishnah and Gemara by centuries. The Tannaim and Amoraim may have known it but not formalized it in their texts; the transmission was through the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah's own authority, not through the Talmudic record.

The Savoraim Alternative — Rabbeinu Shlomo of Worms

Rabbeinu Shlomo MiGermaiza (R' Shlomo of Worms / Vermayza) — a Rishon, contemporary, friend, and chavrusa of Rashi — offers an entirely different attribution in his own siddur:[5] Baruch She'amar was composed by the Savoraim (סבוראים), the sages of the period between Ravina v'Rav Ashi's sealing of the Gemara and the era of the Geonim — roughly 500 to 700 CE.

Aside on the Savoraim. This is a poorly-known stretch of Jewish history. After the Gemara was sealed and before the Geonim arose in Sura and Pumbedisa, a roughly 200-year period of approximately 189 years elapsed in which Klal Yisrael was led by the Rabbanan Savoraim. They edited certain passages in Shas — the Ritva at the start of Maseches Kiddushin[6] states that the opening sugya of the masechta is not from the Amoraim but from the Savoraim. Apart from such passages — and Rabbeinu Shlomo's claim about Baruch She'amar — almost nothing is preserved as their independent legacy. Modern scholarship, including extensive Cairo Geniza research, has filled in certain details, but the era remains largely silent.

The Savoraim attribution, however, sharpens the Pri Chadash's question rather than resolving it. The Anshei Knesses HaGedolah possessed full legislative authority to institute brachos; if they received a heavenly note, the Pri Megadim's resolution flows naturally. But the Savoraim, by consensus, did not possess that authority — their role was editorial and transmissive, not legislative. If they authored Baruch She'amar afresh, the Pri Chadash's difficulty has no fully clean resolution, and the accepted answer — that they too were transmitting an ancient kabbalah, not creating something new — remains a real and continuing difficulty in the Acharonim.

Rabbeinu Shlomo's Life

Rabbeinu Shlomo MiGermaiza is a remarkable historical figure in his own right. The most important Torah center of his era was the Kehillos Shum (קהילות שומ) — the three communities of Speyer, Worms, and Mainz (Shin-Vav-Mem: Shpira / Vermayza / Magenza). Rashi himself traveled from Troyes to Worms to study under Rabbeinu Yaakov bar Yakar, and Rabbeinu Shlomo was Rashi's contemporary and close friend, despite their many disputes on nusach ha'tefillah.

Rabbeinu Shlomo enjoyed a unique relationship with Heinrich IV (later Holy Roman Emperor). He persuaded Heinrich to permit Jews to live in Worms without burdensome additional taxes — Heinrich agreed not out of affection for Jews, but because of his own conflict with the Pope, who despised them. He awarded Rabbeinu Shlomo the unprecedented title "Bishop of the Jews." This rare era of relative safety ended in 4856 / 1096, when Rabbeinu Shlomo was murdered al Kiddush Hashem in the First Crusade — together with thousands of Yidden in Speyer, Worms, and Mainz — losses commemorated annually in the kinos of Tisha B'Av.

IV. The Inner Structure and Symbolism of Baruch She'amar

The Ten Beruchin

The word Baruch appears ten times in Baruch She'amar. The Rishonim connect this to the Asarah Ma'amaros[7] — the ten utterances with which HaKadosh Baruch Hu created the world (Bereishis Rabbah; Pirkei Avos). The brachah is fundamentally a praise of Creation: "Baruch she'amar v'haya ha'olam" — Blessed is He who spoke and the world came into being.

87 Words = Paz

The brachah contains exactly 87 words, the gematria of Paz (פז) — fine gold. The Tur connects this to the pasuk in Shir HaShirim[8] describing HaKadosh Baruch Hu: "Rosho kesem paz" (ראשו׹ כתם פָז) — His head is like the finest gold. Baruch She'amar is the crown of pure gold that prefaces the daily chapters of praise.

Baruch Merachem al Ha'aretz, Baruch Merachem al HaBriyos

Among the Baruch phrases is: "Baruch merachem al ha'aretz, Baruch merachem al ha'briyos" — Blessed is He who has compassion on the earth, blessed is He who has compassion on His creatures. The Ben Ish Chai[9] records a vivid illustration: a poor Yid in Baghdad came on a muddy day to a wealthy man's home, dripping water onto the elegant carpets. The owner protested. The poor man replied: "I see you are 'merachem al ha'aretz' — compassionate to the earth (the floor). Now let me see if you are 'merachem al ha'briyos' — compassionate to the creature." The phrasing of the brachah demands both: HaKadosh Baruch Hu's mercy extends to both the world He created and the creatures who dwell in it.

U'V'Shirei David Avdecha — David's Eternal Plea

The brachah concludes "U'v'shirei David avdecha nehallelcha" — and we praise You with the songs of David, Your servant. A midrash records that David HaMelech petitioned HaKadosh Baruch Hu based on the pasuk "Aguro b'oholcha olamim"[10] — "Let me dwell in Your tents for eternities" (plural). The plural, said David, must mean both this world and the next: not only while he lived, but after his departure, Yidden should still recite his words, so that he dwells in the tents of HaKadosh Baruch Hu eternally. HaKadosh Baruch Hu agreed. Every morning that promise is fulfilled.

V. The Halachic Status of Baruch She'amar

Hefsek Between Baruch She'amar and Yishtabach

From Baruch She'amar through Yishtabach, interruption through speech is forbidden. The Mishnah Berurah[11] rules that one who spoke between the two brachos must return to the beginning and repeat Baruch She'amar, the pesukim, and Yishtabach. He frames the ruling with an expression of hesitancy, signaling that he is not entirely certain — but practically, the ruling stands as stated. The simplest observance of the law is simply to avoid interruption.

The Magen Avraham's Leniency

Because of the unique non-Talmudic origin of Baruch She'amar, the Magen Avraham[12] rules that this brachah has a different status from ordinary brachos. Specifically, answering amen in the middle of Baruch She'amar is permitted — something not allowed during a normal brachah. This leniency extends through the body of Pesukei d'Zimra as well: one may respond to Kaddish, Kedushah, and other devarim shebikdushah, since Pesukei d'Zimra itself is praise of Hashem, and answering further praises is not deemed a contradictory interruption.

The Aruch HaShulchan's Structural Chiddush

The Aruch HaShulchan[13] notes a structural oddity: in every other brachah, one opens with the standard "Baruch Atah Hashem Elokeinu Melech HaOlam..." formula. Baruch She'amar begins instead with "Baruch she'amar v'haya ha'olam..." — and only several lines later does the standard formula appear, embedded mid-brachah: "Baruch Atah Hashem Elokeinu Melech HaOlam, hakeil ha'av harachaman...".

The Aruch HaShulchan's chiddush: the actual brachah begins from Baruch Atah Hashem; everything before that is a haktamah — an introductory preamble — and is not technically part of the brachah body. The practical implications for hefsek rules in the opening lines are discussed among the poskim.

VI. Hodu, Nusach, and the Yemenite Exception

The dispute over the placement of Hodu — before or after Baruch She'amar — depends entirely on whether Hodu is part of Pesukei d'Zimra or part of Korbanos.

The Rambam does not bring Hodu at all. The Yemenite Jews (Teimanim) follow the Rambam exclusively — even more strictly than other Sephardic communities follow the Shulchan Aruch — and accordingly, they do not recite Hodu. The Arizal, building on the Zohar, advocated strongly for its daily recitation. The Yemenites do not recognize the authority of the Zohar in practical halacha.

The Dardaim. A subset of Yemenite Jewry — the Dardaim (דרדעים), from Dor De'ah ("Generation of Knowledge," referencing the Dor HaMidbar) — went further and rejected the entire Zohar outright. They hold that the Rambam did not possess the Zohar, and on that basis they discard Kabbalistic elements from liturgy. Many are concentrated near Rosh HaAyin in Eretz Yisrael. They were excommunicated by the Badatz of Yerushalayim over this position, though they remain part of Klal Yisrael.

The Ba'al HaTanya argues, by contrast, that the Rambam certainly knew Kabbalah but deliberately set it aside in his writings, because his generation was steeped in philosophy and unprepared to absorb mystical teaching.[14]

The original takanah, predating both Hodu and Mizmor Shir Chanukas HaBayis, was simply: Baruch She'amar → Halleluyos → Yishtabach. Every addition was incorporated later, which is why Nusach Sefard places Hodu outside that frame — it has nothing to do with Pesukei d'Zimra proper, and belongs with Korbanos.

By contrast, Mizmor L'Todah — which follows Baruch She'amar in all major nusachos — belongs to an altogether different category, as becomes clear from its structure and its connection to the daily korban todah.

VII. Mizmor L'Todah — The Daily Korban Todah

Source and Practice

Mizmor L'Todah (Tehillim 100) is recited daily after Baruch She'amar. The Siddur HaRashi[15] and Rabbi Yaakov Emden in his Siddur Yaavetz explain that the Levi'im in the Beis HaMikdash would sing precisely this mizmor while a person offered a korban todah. In the absence of the korbanos, daily recitation of Mizmor L'Todah constitutes the daily korban todah.

The Four Who Must Give Thanks

The Gemara[16] identifies four categories of people obligated to bring a korban todah: Choleh (one who recovered from illness), Yam (one who completed an ocean journey safely), Yesurim (one released from captivity), and Midbar (one who safely traversed a desert). The Tur[17] arranges the four into a siman: "Chayim b'chol ha'chayim yoduchaselo chayim", with the four initials marking Choleh, Yam, Yesurim, Midbar. Today, the same four recite Birkas HaGomel instead.

The poskim debate extensively whether modern air travel obligates birkas hagomel — a question that arises with particular frequency in communities with close connections to Eretz Yisrael.

Why the Mizmor Is Said Daily — Even Without a Specific Yeshuah

The Yaavetz writes that every single day one is saved from countless dangers — many of which pass unnoticed. The act of waking in good health, with one's household well, and completing the morning's journey safely is itself a daily miracle. Therefore, every morning the Mizmor L'Todah is recited in place of an actual korban todah: the daily obligation of gratitude precedes any specific yeshuah.

Numerical Symbolism

Two structural details confirm this identification:

  • Forty words — corresponding to the forty loaves of bread that accompanied a korban todah (ten each of four kinds).
  • Four names of Hashem after the opening pasuk, corresponding to the four categories obligated to give thanks.

When the Mizmor Is Omitted

The mizmor is omitted on certain days because the underlying korban todah could not be brought on those days:

  • Shabbos and Yom Tov — no nedavah offerings (voluntary korbanos) are brought on Shabbos.
  • Erev Pesach — the forty loaves include chametz bread, which could not be consumed before z'man achilas chametz ended. To avoid the loaves becoming nosar and pasul, the korban was not brought.
  • Erev Yom Kippur — consuming the requisite forty loaves before the fast was simply not feasible.

(There is a separate machloikis among the poskim regarding whether Mizmor L'Todah is recited on Tisha B'Av; Luach Eretz Yisrael by Rav Tukachinsky and the Hinkis luach address this question explicitly with differing conclusions.)

VIII. The Forgotten Halachah — Singing Mizmor L'Todah

The Shulchan Aruch[18] rules a striking and almost universally neglected halachah:

צריך לאומרו בנעימה, שכל השירות עתידות ליבטל חוץ ממזמור לתודה.
"One must recite it with a melody, for all songs of praise are destined to be nullified in the future, except for Mizmor L'Todah."

This ruling is sourced in the Beis Yosef from the Meiri and the Mahari Abuhav — not a fringe opinion. Mizmor L'Todah is to be sung, not merely recited. Yet in nearly every Ashkenazic congregation, it is read silently. Sephardic communities are in general more careful, but even there the practice is uneven.

A contemporary Torah authority — the author of a sefer devoted entirely to Mizmor L'Todah, composed under the encouragement of Rav Gamliel Rabinovitch of Yerushalayim — records the following account. At Shtilei Zeisim, a Sephardic congregation in Bnei Brak known for extraordinary precision in nusach, a ba'al tefillah proposed reinstating the Shulchan Aruch's ruling and singing Mizmor L'Todah with the tzibbur daily. The kahal agreed.

Some months later, this ba'al tefillah was diagnosed with what physicians said was a metastasized growth requiring urgent surgery. He traveled to Tzfas, to the kever of the Beis Yosef — the very posek who codified this halachah in the Shulchan Aruch — and pleaded: "In the merit of cherishing your ruling, which almost no one keeps, please grant me a yeshuah." When he returned for the pre-operative scan a week later, the physicians reported that the growths had vanished. (Such accounts are to be received with both reverence and discernment; the underlying halachah, in any event, stands on its own authority.) At minimum, the account is a call to restore this ruling to its rightful place in practice.

Endnote A — Attribution of the Petek Min HaShamayim Tradition

A. In the oral shiur, the tradition of the heavenly petek was attributed to "the grandson of the Ramban, in a Sefer Or Zarua." As transmitted in the write-up, this was rendered as "The Or Zarua (the grandson of the Ramban — distinct from the famous Or Zarua of Rabbeinu Yitzchak of Vienna)." However, no well-documented sefer titled Or Zarua attributed to a grandson of the Ramban has been positively identified. The famous Or Zarua is Rabbeinu Yitzchak of Vienna, entirely unrelated to the Ramban. The Tur in Orach Chaim 51 preserves the petek tradition, and the Beis Yosef elaborates its chain of transmission; the precise identity of the source the Tur is drawing on in this context should be verified against the primary texts before any attribution to "Ramban's grandson" is stated. Until such verification is possible, fn. 3 in this edition cites the Tur and Taz directly without a secondary attribution.
