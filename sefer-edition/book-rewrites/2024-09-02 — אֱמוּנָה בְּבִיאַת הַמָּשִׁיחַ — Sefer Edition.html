Emunah B’Bias HaMashiach: Belief, Anticipation, and the Limits of Both
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Legal-Holiday Series
Delivered 2 September 2024  ·  Sefer Edition

L’ilui nishmas the mother of Rav Feige. Delivered the morning after the murder of six chatufim in captivity — at roughly day 330 of the war. Hashem yirachem.

I. The Twelfth Ikar and Its Two Components

At the close of Shacharis in many siddurim stands the recitation of Ani Ma’amin b’emunah sheleimah, the thirteen ikrei emunah. Yet neither Torah Shebiksav nor Torah She’baal Peh contains a formal enumeration of thirteen. The formulation derives from the Rambam’s Hakdamah to the tenth perek of Sanhedrin, known as Perek Cheilek, in his Peirush HaMishnayos—the perek that treats the coming of Mashiach, olam ha’ba, and techiyas ha’meisim.[1] There the Rambam distills the entire fabric of emunah into thirteen principles, insisting that he did not do so casually—he was not boyde milibo—but arrived at the count after prolonged investigation of the pesukim and the ma’amrei Chazal. The twelfth of these ikrim is emunah b’Bias HaMashiach.

The Text in the Siddur and Its Traditional Provenance

The precise formulation of Ani Ma’amin printed in the standard siddur derives from the Rambam but is not itself his own composition; its author is unknown. Its first surviving printed appearance is often said to belong to a Portuguese siddur of 1492—the year of the Spanish Expulsion.[2] Whether or not the historical attribution is bibliographically settled, the traditional coupling is itself instructive. Klal Yisrael reached for a fixed formulaic emunah b’Bias HaMashiach at precisely the moment when the ground beneath their feet was giving way. The Ani Ma’amin is, in this reading, the liturgical response of a people whose immediate world has collapsed and whose only recourse is the fixed certainty of a future geulah. The same emunah that answered the Expulsion is the emunah that answers every subsequent tzarah.

Belief and Chikui: Two Distinct Obligations

The twelfth ikar does not contain a single demand but two. The first is belief—that there will indeed be a personal Mashiach, descended from David HaMelech through Shevet Yehudah, of the line of Shlomo specifically, who will rebuild the Beis HaMikdash, gather the exiles, and restore the avodah. The second is active anticipation—chikui—an ongoing disposition of readiness, hope, and yearning that permeates a Jew’s daily life.

The Rambam formulates the obligation with striking severity. One who is merely safek—uncertain—whether Mashiach will come is thereby a koifer in the Torah, in the Nevi’im, and in HaKadosh Baruch Hu. Denial of any of the specific dynastic details—zera Beis David, through Shlomo—carries the same weight. In Hilchos Melachim[3] he codifies the operational criteria by which the messianic era is to be identified: the restoration of malchus Beis David, the rebuilding of the Beis HaMikdash, kibbutz galuyos, and the return of all the mishpatim of the Torah. In Hilchos Teshuvah[4] he counts ha’kofrim b’Vias HaGo’el among those who forfeit their cheilek l’olam ha’ba.

That the Rambam should treat mere doubt as kefirah is itself worthy of pause. Most of the ikrim tolerate safek up to the point of positive denial; this one does not. The reason lies in what the ikar is asked to bear. Of all the principles, this is the one most exposed to the erosion of lived experience—century after century of galus, of tzaros, of persecutions that would seem to falsify the promise. For an ikar so vulnerable, only a fence higher than the ordinary threshold of denial can preserve it intact.

II. The Prophetic Foundation

The Rambam’s formulation rests upon explicit pesukim. Four of them stand at the center.

וְיָצָא חֹטֶר מִגֵּזַע יִשַׁי, וְנֵצֶר מִשָּׁרָשָׁיו יִפְרֶה. וְנָחָה עָלָיו רוּחַ ה’...
A shoot shall come forth from the stump of Yishai; a branch shall grow from his roots. The spirit of Hashem shall rest upon him.
Yeshayahu 11:1–2

The classical messianic prophecy of a descendant of Yishai—and thus of David, and of Shlomo—who will execute justice and bring universal peace. This prophecy has been drawn deeply into the calendar of Klal Yisrael: it stands as the haftarah read on the last day of Pesach,[5] and the Chief Rabbinate of the State of Israel enacted in 1948 that it be read on Yom Ha’atzmaut. The prophet Malachi speaks in the same register:

הִנְנִי שֹׁלֵחַ מַלְאָכִי וּפִנָּה דֶרֶךְ לְפָנָי, וּפִתְאֹם יָבוֹא אֶל הֵיכָלוֹ הָאָדוֹן.
Behold, I send My messenger, and he shall clear the way before Me; and suddenly the master shall come to his palace.
Malachi 3:1

The mefarshim identify ha’adon here not as HaKadosh Baruch Hu Himself but as a designated agent—angelic or human—who will effect the geulah. And in nevuas Bilam, the Pentateuchal source cited by the Rambam himself in Hilchos Melachim: דָּרַךְ כּוֹכָב מִיַּעֲקֹב וְקָם שֵׁבֶט מִיִּשְׂרָאֵל—Chazal read this as referring to the future Mashiach. Rabbi Akiva famously extended the identification to Bar Kochba; whether Rabbi Akiva himself formally retracted or whether the identification simply collapsed with Bar Kochba’s defeat is disputed among the Rishonim, but the messianic reading of the pasuk stands.[6]

The fourth pasuk is the source of the second component of the ikar—the obligation of anticipation itself:

כִּי עוֹד חָזוֹן לַמּוֹעֵד, וְיָפֵחַ לַקֵּץ וְלֹא יְכַזֵּב; אִם יִתְמַהְמָהּ חַכֵּה לוֹ, כִּי בֹא יָבוֹא לֹא יְאַחֵר.
For there is still a vision for the appointed time… if it tarries, await it, for it will surely come; it will not delay.
Chavakuk 2:3

This pasuk is the explicit textual foundation for chikui: not merely to believe, but to await; and to await precisely under conditions of apparent delay. It is the second component of the twelfth ikar, and every subsequent debate about the meaning of chikui is, in effect, a debate about how to read the words chakeh lo.

III. The Plurality of Frameworks Among the Rishonim

The Rambam’s formulation was influential, but it was not the only such formulation, nor was it universally accepted as authoritative. Rav Saadia Gaon, in Emunos v’De’os,[7] presents ten foundational principles. Rabbi Yosef Albo, in Sefer HaIkarim,[8] reduces the count to three core principles—metzius Hashem, Torah min haShamayim, and schar v’onesh—from which the others derive. Still other Rishonim arrive at six, at eight, at ten.

Most of these disagreements are categorical rather than substantive: one principle in one framework encompasses two or three in another. On the ground, Klal Yisrael accepted the Rambam’s thirteen as the working list, and did so with particular firmness regarding the twelfth ikar. But the plurality of frameworks itself matters, because it establishes that the number thirteen and the specific taxonomy are not themselves the object of emunah—the underlying beliefs are.

The Maharshal, the Arizal, and Liturgical Dissent

Not every gadol recited Ani Ma’amin or Yigdal daily. The Maharshal[9] raised a principled objection: who gave the Rambam the authority to isolate thirteen matters as the ikrim? Every detail of the Torah is essential; there is no warrant to designate a hierarchy of importance among them. The Arizal, similarly, did not recite Yigdal—the poetic version of the thirteen ikrim attributed to Immanuel of Rome[10]—and many siddurim note explicitly, Ha’Arizal lo amar Yigdal. Following the Arizal, many Chassidishe communities do not say it either.

The dissent is liturgical rather than doctrinal. None of these gedolim denied the underlying beliefs; they declined to ritualize them in fixed daily formulae. This is a critical distinction, and it defines the terrain on which a later academic challenge to the Rambam’s framework must be measured.

IV. The Meta-Halachic Question — Dr. Marc Shapiro’s Challenge

The transition from the Rishonim’ own plurality of frameworks to modern academic reappraisal is a transition in register. The Rishonim operated within the tradition and disagreed within it; the academic historian stands outside it and describes it. Yet the academic case can raise questions that bear directly on how the tradition understands itself, and one such case has been pressed with unusual force.

Dr. Marc B. Shapiro, whose doctoral training is at Harvard and who serves as professor at the University of Scranton, published in 2003 a book titled The Limits of Orthodox Theology: Maimonides’ Thirteen Principles Reappraised.[11] The work catalogs, across the sweep of the Rishonim and Acharonim, the substantial number of authorities who diverged from one or another of the Rambam’s thirteen. The book caused an uproar in the yeshivishe world, and it deserves a careful response.

The key to responding to Shapiro is to recognize that his argument operates on two distinct levels. On the level of belief-content—whether the specific propositions the Rambam enumerates are true—he does not challenge them at all. On the level of meta-halachah—whether the denial of any of these propositions renders one a koifer with the halachic consequences the Rambam attaches—he assembles a serious documentary record showing that the classification is more contested than popular presentation admits. The two questions must be separated. The emunah of Klal Yisrael in the coming of Mashiach is unaltered by anything Shapiro writes. What is contested is the meta-halachic label.

The response of the tradition, faced with that meta-halachic claim, is not that the academic mapping is false—the mapping is what it is—but that the working reality of Klal Yisrael is not built on the widest possible base of Rishonim-averaging. It is built on mesorah: on which Rishonim and which Acharonim our rabbeim, our yeshivos, and our manhigim have taken as authoritative. On the twelfth ikar in particular, the community has cleaved to the Rambam’s framework, and the halachic teeth—safek-as-kefirah, exclusion from cheilek l’olam ha’ba—follow from that cleaving. The meta-halachic question is thus not settled by counting Rishonim; it is settled by the reception history of a particular Rishon’s framework within the mesorah-line that constitutes the living tradition.

V. The Difficult Gemara — Rabbi Hillel in Sanhedrin

The Gemara in Sanhedrin[12] records a statement that has troubled the Rishonim from the earliest generations. Rabbi Hillel—one of several Amoraim bearing that name in the Talmud, identified by some Rishonim as a grandson of Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi—said:

אֵין מָשִׁיחַ לְיִשְׂרָאֵל, שֶׁכְּבָר אֲכָלוּהוּ בִּימֵי חִזְקִיָּהוּ.
There is no Mashiach for Israel, for they have already consumed him in the days of Chizkiyahu.

An Amora denying the future Mashiach. Rashi[13] clarifies the intent: Rabbi Hillel did not deny the geulah—he denied a personal Mashiach, holding that HaKadosh Baruch Hu would bring the redemption directly, without an intermediary melech ha’mashiach. The Gemara immediately responds: Amar Rav Yosef: shari leih marei l’Hillel—may his Master forgive Hillel, for he erred in his interpretation.

The apparent difficulty admits of a formal resolution: Rabbi Hillel pre-dates the psak; the tradition that followed him crystallized in a decisive rejection of his view. What was, in his generation, an interpretive position within a live discussion became, after that discussion had closed, a rejected view. Denial of a personal Mashiach today, in the wake of the tradition’s definitive settlement of the question, carries the weight of kefirah that it did not carry in Rabbi Hillel’s own hour.

Yet a deeper tension remains. If Rabbi Hillel could hold this position without being classified as a koifer in his own generation, then either the twelfth ikar as the Rambam formulates it is itself the product of a later crystallization of the tradition—which is a version of the observation Shapiro presses—or the ikar was always in place and Rabbi Hillel was operating with a defective transmission of it. Neither disposition is fully comfortable. The most defensible reading is that the substantive content of the ikar—that there will be a redeemer of Klal Yisrael—was always in place; what crystallized later, in the wake of the Gemara’s decisive treatment of Rabbi Hillel’s view, was the specific requirement that the redeemer be personal. The category-boundaries of an ikar can sharpen over time even as its core stands unchanged. That the tradition itself displays this development is not a scandal; it is what it looks like for the mesorah to be alive.

VI. The Central Machloikes on the Meaning of Chikui

The Rambam’s requirement of chikui admits of two readings, and the great poskim of the modern era have divided along the fault-line between them.

The Brisker Position

Rav Moshe Feinstein, in a teshuvah in Igros Moshe,[14] takes a maximalist position. It is not enough to believe that perhaps Mashiach will come today; that is too weak a formulation. The Rambam’s true requirement is that one believe certainly Mashiach will come today; if not today, then certainly tomorrow; if not tomorrow, then certainly the day after. Anything less than positive daily certainty falls short of chikui. The Brisker Rav[15] holds the same. Confronted with the obvious kashya—how can one believe that Mashiach will certainly come today when the Gemara teaches that he does not come on Erev Shabbos, and the Shaagas Aryeh[16] displays the machloikes over whether he comes in Nisan or in Tishrei—he famously replied: When Mashiach comes, I’ll answer that question too.

The Brisker Rav extended the principle further. The brachah in Shemoneh Esrei reads ki l’yeshuascha kivinu kol ha’yom—not merely every day, but the entire day: constant, moment-to-moment expectancy. Mi zoche—who reaches such a madreigah? Few. But that is the standard the Brisker Rav identified in the words of the tefillah themselves.

The Chofetz Chaim’s Position

The Chofetz Chaim[17] read the Rambam’s requirement of chikui differently—not as positive daily certainty but as the absence of yeush. A Jew must never despair; must never conclude that Mashiach is not coming. So long as his tefillah contains sincere, hopeful yearning for the geulah, the ikar is fulfilled. Lacking moment-to-moment certainty is not kefirah; only the positive despair of the eventual geulah is.

The Chofetz Chaim’s reading and the Brisker reading are not simply two rival readings of the same words; they are two different accounts of what chikui asks of a Jew. The Brisker reads chikui as a demand on the content of belief—certainty about a specific metzius at every moment. The Chofetz Chaim reads chikui as a demand on disposition—the orientation of the nefesh toward hope and away from despair. The two can be viewed as floor and ceiling of a single continuum, with the Chofetz Chaim identifying the threshold below which one fails the ikar and the Brisker Rav describing the height toward which one aspires. This picture holds both positions within a single frame without collapsing the difference between them.

Reb Chaim Brisker’s Distinction Between the Am Ha’aretz and the Koifer

Rav Chaim Brisker is reported to have drawn a distinction that undergirds the Brisker side of the discussion. A Jew who fails to keep halachah out of ignorance is an am ha’aretz, not a koifer. He can do al cheit on Yom Kippur, learn the halachah for next year, and be forgiven. But a Jew who is missing one of the thirteen ikrim—who genuinely does not believe in Mashiach, even if the failure is "by mistake"—is, in the factual reality of his soul, a koifer. Emunah is not graded by intent; it is graded by content. The Brisker Rav holds the same. Whether this distinction should be pressed to its full halachic conclusion in the public arena is precisely what divides the Brisker camp from the Chofetz Chaim: the Chofetz Chaim, in effect, redefines the content-threshold downward to the absence of despair, thereby placing the ordinary Jew safely inside the ikar so long as he does not affirmatively give up.

A Contemporary Application

Some years before the present shiur, Rav Aharon Leib Shteinman publicly transmitted the Chofetz Chaim’s reading of chikui. Extreme chareidi voices in Eretz Yisrael responded with pashkevilim that—according to accounts—charged him with kefirah in emunas Bias HaMashiach.[18] Whatever the precise wording of the pashkevilim, the underlying phenomenon is the misuse of the label koifer against a gadol ha’dor who was transmitting a position that stands squarely within the spectrum of the Acharonim. To call a gadol a koifer for repeating the Chofetz Chaim—the most widely accepted halachic authority of the modern era—discredits the accusers, not the accused. The discussion between Brisk and the Chofetz Chaim is a real machloikes; a position within that machloikes is not kefirah.

VII. The Yesod: Doctrine and Operation as Two Distinct Layers

The machloikes between Brisk and the Chofetz Chaim comes into focus only when a deeper distinction is named. Emunah operates on one layer; halachic action operates on another. The twelfth ikar demands a daily doctrinal disposition of anticipation. It does not thereby demand that a Jew’s halachic conduct be reshaped as though Mashiach were arriving in the next hour. The doctrinal and the operational are two layers, and the halachah itself insists on their distinction. This is the yesod that underlies the entire cluster of sugyos that follow, and it is the yesod that quietly qualifies the Brisker maximalism from within the Rambam himself.

Kohanim and Wine

The Gemara[19] considers the case of the kohen. The Beis HaMikdash rotated its priestly service through twenty-four mishmaros, each serving its designated week. A kohen who knows his mishmar may not drink wine during that week—lest the Beis HaMikdash be suddenly rebuilt and he be summoned to avodah, which may not be performed under the influence. If the kohen does not know his mishmar—as is true of virtually every kohen today—the initial view holds that he should abstain from wine all year, for he might be precisely the kohen needed when Mashiach arrives.

Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi rejected this stringency. He could not accept that every kohen must abstain from wine all year on the assumption of shema yibaneh ha’Beis HaMikdash. The Rambam himself follows Rabbi: every kohen may drink. If the Beis HaMikdash is suddenly rebuilt, kohanim who happen not to have drunk that day will be found to perform the avodah; the entire community of kohanim need not abstain in perpetuity.

The point is decisive. The very Rambam who requires daily chikui as emunah—on the doctrinal layer—rules on the operational layer that a kohen need not abstain from wine on the assumption of imminent avodah. The emunah is doctrinal; the operational halachah is not maximized to match. This is not an oversight but a principled two-layer architecture—and whether Rav Moshe himself in the teshuvah addresses this internal Rambam is a question that remains open in the record as it currently stands.

Haircuts Before Yom Tov

The Shulchan Aruch[20] rules that one takes a haircut erev Yom Tov. Beyond k’vod ha’yom, some poskim add the rationale of shema yibaneh ha’Beis HaMikdash. If Mashiach arrives during Yom Tov, a Jew must be ready to run to Eretz Yisrael to bring korbanos; the halachah forbids entering the Azarah in a menuval state. So one trims before Yom Tov, ready for the call. Here the operational layer does register a ripple from the doctrinal chikui—but only a ripple, not a wholesale reorganization of conduct.

Pregnant Women and the Beis HaChaim

There is a widespread minhag that pregnant women do not enter a beis ha’chaim. A range of reasons is offered: concerns of tumah, of slipping, of spiritual sensitivity. One striking reason connects the practice to Mashiach: the future need for kohanim untouched by tumas meis to draw the mei chatas of the parah adumah. Maseches Parah[21] records that in the era of the Beis HaMikdash, women would relocate to a special section of Yerushalayim to give birth, and walkways were built with deliberate gaps between the bridge surface and the ground, so that tumah min ha’meis from any hidden burial below could not rise through to contaminate the children. From the moment of birth these kohanim were preserved in absolute taharah; at three or four years of age they were the ones who drew the water. Some poskim apply this reasoning today: a pregnant woman who enters a beis ha’chaim may transmit tumah to her unborn child, and precisely such kohanic children may be needed when Mashiach comes. Whether the rationale is compelling, the minhag stands across communities.

The Nazir Whose Vow Begins When Mashiach Comes

A Jew may take a neder: on the day Mashiach comes, I will become a nazir. Once such a vow has been made, he may never again drink wine—because at any moment Mashiach may come, his nezirus begin, and any wine in his system render him a nazir tamei retroactively. Yet the Gemara rules that he may drink wine on Shabbos and Yom Tov, because Mashiach does not come on Shabbos or Yom Tov. The Shaagas Aryeh raises the obvious challenge: if Mashiach also does not come on Shabbos or Yom Tov, and there is a machloikes whether he comes only in Nisan or only in Tishrei, what remains of the emunah that he could come every day? The Brisker Rav’s answer—when Mashiach comes, we’ll answer that too—preserves the doctrinal certainty at the cost of leaving the operational question suspended. The Chofetz Chaim’s reading resolves the tension from the other side: the daily chikui operates on the level of nefesh and disposition, not on the level of metzius, and no operational limitation on when Mashiach can arrive threatens a demand pitched at the level of the soul.

VIII. Living the Ikar

Given the two-layer architecture, the practical shape of daily chikui for the ordinary Jew is neither the Brisker Rav’s kol ha’yom nor a vague gesture toward hope. It is a graded structure whose base and summit have both been named.

The base, in the reading of the Chofetz Chaim, is the disciplined refusal of yeush. The Jew who says the brachos of Es tzemach David, V’lirushalayim ircha, and Tek’a b’shofar gadol—whose text is saturated with anticipation—and who says them meaning what they say, without collapsing into despair, has fulfilled the ikar. From the base, one moves upward toward the Brisker kol ha’yom by learning to hold, moment by moment, the specific expectation that today, this hour, this tefillah, may be the one at whose end Mashiach arrives. The movement between the two is the work of a lifetime, and the two positions are best understood as marking the terrain of that work rather than as competitors for the reader’s allegiance.

One boundary marker deserves separate note. The Rambam himself insists lo yeida adam heiach yih’yu ad she’yih’yu—no one knows how these events will unfold until they unfold. Speculative timelines and date-setting are not part of the emunah. The emunah is in that Mashiach will come; it is not in when. Every attempt to fix a date has ended in the humiliation of the fixer and the wounding of the emunah of those who trusted him. The doctrinal certainty of the twelfth ikar is coupled with a strict operational reticence about the details.

IX. Closing

Klal Yisrael has carried this emunah for nineteen hundred years through every conceivable tzarah. The six chatufim buried in Eretz Yisrael are not the first korbanos of the ongoing exile, and the refusal to despair on their behalf is the same refusal that has been carried by every generation since the Churban. The same emunah that reached for a formulaic Ani Ma’amin in Portugal in 1492 is the emunah that allows American parents to send their children to yeshiva ketana this morning while six families in Eretz Yisrael sit shiva. HaKadosh Baruch Hu is not addressed from a position of comfort; He is addressed from within the tension the ikar was built to bear.

אִם יִתְמַהְמָהּ חַכֵּה לוֹ, כִּי בֹא יָבוֹא לֹא יְאַחֵר.
If it tarries, await it; for it will surely come, it will not delay.
Chavakuk 2:3

Maharshal (R’ Shlomo Luria), drashos (general reference). — The Maharshal’s principled objection to the Rambam’s selection of thirteen ikrim: every detail of the Torah is essential, and there is no authority to designate a hierarchy of importance. He himself did not recite Ani Ma’amin daily. The objection is liturgical rather than doctrinal. ↩
  10. Arizal, Sha’ar HaKavanos — non-recitation of Yigdal; Immanuel of Rome as traditional author. — The Arizal did not recite Yigdal, and many siddurim preserve the note Ha’Arizal lo amar Yigdal. Many Chassidishe communities follow the Arizal in omitting it. Yigdal itself — the poetic version of the thirteen ikrim — is widely attributed to Immanuel of Rome (Immanuel ben Solomon, c. 1261–c. 1328), the Italian-Jewish poet and author of Machbarot Immanuel. Alternative attribution is sometimes given to R’ Daniel ben Yehudah HaDayan. The poem is included in many siddurim regardless of which attribution is accepted. ↩
  11. Marc B. Shapiro, The Limits of Orthodox Theology: Maimonides’ Thirteen Principles Reappraised (Littman Library / Oxford, 2003). — Academic survey cataloging Rishonim and Acharonim who diverged from one or another of the Rambam’s thirteen. The book’s argument operates at the meta-halachic level (whether denial constitutes kefirah) rather than at the level of belief-content; the tradition’s response is that the meta-halachic classification follows the mesorah-reception of a specific Rishon’s framework, not the widest possible base of Rishonim-averaging. Shapiro received his doctorate from Harvard and teaches at the University of Scranton. ↩
  12. Sanhedrin צ״ט ע״א (99a) — Rabbi Hillel’s statement. — אֵין מָשִׁיחַ לְיִשְׂרָאֵל, שֶׁכְּבָר אֲכָלוּהוּ בִּימֵי חִזְקִיָּהוּ. Rav Yosef’s response: shari leih marei l’Hillel — may his Master forgive him for the interpretive mistake. Rabbi Hillel is one of several Amoraim bearing that name in the Talmud; some Rishonim identify him as a grandson of Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi, though the identification is not uniform in the Rishon literature. ↩
  13. Rashi on Sanhedrin 99a, s.v. ein Mashiach l’Yisrael. — Rashi clarifies that Rabbi Hillel did not deny geulah; he denied a personal Mashiach, holding that HaKadosh Baruch Hu would redeem directly. Once Chazal rejected this view and paskened for a personal Mashiach, denying that today is kefirah. Rabbi Hillel pre-dates the psak; we live after it. ↩
  14. R’ Moshe Feinstein, Igros Moshe, Yoreh De’ah IV, siman 36 (general reference to the teshuvah on daily chikui). — Rav Moshe’s maximalist reading of the Rambam’s chikui: not perhaps today, but certainly today; if not today, then certainly tomorrow. Anything less than positive daily certainty falls short of the ikar. Aligns with the Brisker camp. The teshuvah is customarily cited to Yoreh De’ah, though editions vary; the substantive position is consistent across the printed corpus. ↩
  15. The Brisker Rav (R’ Yitzchak Ze’ev Soloveitchik), oral teachings preserved by talmidim. — The maximalist position of kol ha’yom — continuous, moment-to-moment chikui — and the celebrated response to the Shaagas Aryeh’s kashya: when Mashiach comes, I’ll answer that question too. The Brisker Rav refuses to allow the Gemara’s operational limitations on when Mashiach can arrive to erode the doctrinal chikui. ↩
  16. Shaagas Aryeh (R’ Aryeh Leib Gunzberg), teshuvos — the kashya on daily chikui. — The kashya: the Gemara teaches that Mashiach does not come on Erev Shabbos, Shabbos, or Yom Tov, and the Acharonim debate whether he comes only in Nisan or only in Tishrei. How, then, can he "come every day"? The classic Brisker reply preserves the doctrinal certainty at the cost of suspending the operational question; the Chofetz Chaim resolves the tension by locating chikui on the level of disposition rather than metzius. ↩
  17. Chofetz Chaim — drashos and teshuvos on Bias HaMashiach (general reference). — The Chofetz Chaim reads the Rambam’s chikui not as positive daily certainty but as the absence of yeush: a Jew must never despair, must never conclude that Mashiach is not coming. Sincere yearning in the brachos saturated with chikui fulfills the ikar; lacking moment-to-moment certainty is not kefirah. The reading transmitted by Rav Aharon Leib Shteinman. ↩
  18. The pashkevil campaign against Rav Aharon Leib Shteinman — contemporary press accounts. — Rav Aharon Leib Shteinman was the subject of a sustained pashkevil campaign by extreme chareidi factions in Eretz Yisrael. The bulk of the documented grievances concerned chinuch and military-service questions; the specific charge that he was a koifer in emunas Bias HaMashiach for transmitting the Chofetz Chaim’s reading of chikui is a distinct thread within that broader campaign and worth verifying against the pashkevil texts themselves before pressing it as a firm historical assertion. The point of principle stands regardless of the precise wording: the label koifer cannot properly be applied to a gadol transmitting a position that lies within the recognized Acharonim-spectrum on the meaning of chikui. ↩
  19. Ta’anis י״ז ע״א (17a–b); cf. Sanhedrin 22b for related material. — The sugya on kohanim and wine, the twenty-four mishmaros, and Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi’s rejection of a year-round universal stringency. The Rambam (Hilchos Bias HaMikdash) follows Rabbi: every kohen may drink. The primary sugya is Ta’anis 17; Sanhedrin 22b is a parallel reference. Structurally the ruling is the sharpest single indication that chikui is doctrinal, not maximally operational. ↩
  20. Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chaim 529 (haircut erev Yom Tov); cf. 260 (Erev Shabbos). — Beyond k’vod ha’yom, some poskim add the rationale of shema yibaneh ha’Beis HaMikdash: if Mashiach arrives during Yom Tov, one must run to Eretz Yisrael for the avodah and may not enter the Azarah in a menuval state. An operational ripple of the doctrinal chikui. ↩
  21. Mishnayos Parah, ch. 3; cf. Rambam, Hilchos Parah Adumah. — The protocol for preserving kohanic children in absolute taharah for the drawing of the mei chatas: a special section of Yerushalayim for births, walkways built with deliberate gaps to prevent tumah min ha’meis from rising through, isolation through age three or four. The basis for the contemporary minhag that pregnant women avoid the beis ha’chaim — preserving the possibility of parah adumah-eligible kohanim for the era of Mashiach. ↩
