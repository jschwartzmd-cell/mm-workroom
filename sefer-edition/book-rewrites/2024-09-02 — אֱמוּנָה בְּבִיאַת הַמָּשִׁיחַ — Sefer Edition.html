אֱמוּנָה בְּבִיאַת הַמָּשִׁיחַ — אֱמוּנָה, צִפִּיָּה וּגְבוּלוֹתֵיהֶן

Emunah B’Bias HaMashiach: Belief, Anticipation, and the Limits of Both
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Legal-Holiday Series
Delivered 2 September 2024  ·  Sefer Edition

L’ilui nishmas (לְעִלּוּי נִשְׁמַת) the mother of Rav Feige. Delivered the morning after the murder of six chatufim (חֲטוּפִים) in captivity — at roughly day 330 of the war. Hashem yirachem (ה’ יְרַחֵם).

I. The Twelfth Ikar and Its Two Components

At the close of Shacharis (שַׁחֲרִית) in many siddurim (סִדּוּרִים) stands the recitation of Ani Ma’amin b’emunah sheleimah (אֲנִי מַאֲמִין בֶּאֱמוּנָה שְׁלֵמָה), the thirteen ikrei emunah (עִקְּרֵי אֱמוּנָה). Yet neither Torah Shebiksav (תּוֹרָה שֶׁבִּכְתָב) nor Torah She’baal Peh (תּוֹרָה שֶׁבְּעַל פֶּה) contains a formal enumeration of thirteen. The formulation derives from the Rambam’s Hakdamah (הַקְדָּמָה) to the tenth perek (פֶּרֶק) of Sanhedrin (סַנְהֶדְרִין), known as Perek Cheilek (פֶּרֶק חֵלֶק), in his Peirush HaMishnayos (פֵּירוּשׁ הַמִּשְׁנָיוֹת)—the perek that treats the coming of Mashiach (מָשִׁיחַ), olam ha’ba (עוֹלָם הַבָּא), and techiyas ha’meisim (תְּחִיַּת הַמֵּתִים).[1] There the Rambam distills the entire fabric of emunah (אֱמוּנָה) into thirteen principles, insisting that he did not do so casually—he was not boyde milibo (בּוֹדֶה מִלִּבּוֹ)—but arrived at the count after prolonged investigation of the pesukim (פְּסוּקִים) and the ma’amrei Chazal (מַאַמְרֵי חַזַ"ל). The twelfth of these ikrim (עִקָּרִים) is emunah b’Bias HaMashiach (אֱמוּנָה בְּבִיאַת הַמָּשִׁיחַ).

The Text in the Siddur and Its Traditional Provenance

The precise formulation of Ani Ma’amin printed in the standard siddur derives from the Rambam but is not itself his own composition; its author is unknown. Its first surviving printed appearance is often said to belong to a Portuguese siddur of 1492—the year of the Spanish Expulsion.[2] Whether or not the historical attribution is bibliographically settled, the traditional coupling is itself instructive. Klal Yisrael (כְּלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל) reached for a fixed formulaic emunah b’Bias HaMashiach at precisely the moment when the ground beneath their feet was giving way. The Ani Ma’amin is, in this reading, the liturgical response of a people whose immediate world has collapsed and whose only recourse is the fixed certainty of a future geulah (גְּאוּלָּה). The same emunah that answered the Expulsion is the emunah that answers every subsequent tzarah (צָרָה).

Belief and Chikui: Two Distinct Obligations

The twelfth ikar (עִיקָר) does not contain a single demand but two. The first is belief—that there will indeed be a personal Mashiach, descended from David HaMelech (דָּוִד הַמֶּלֶךְ) through Shevet Yehudah (שֵׁבֶט יְהוּדָה), of the line of Shlomo (שְׁלֹמֹה) specifically, who will rebuild the Beis HaMikdash (בֵּית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ), gather the exiles, and restore the avodah (עֲבוֹדָה). The second is active anticipation—chikui (חִיכּוּי)—an ongoing disposition of readiness, hope, and yearning that permeates a Jew’s daily life.

The Rambam formulates the obligation with striking severity. One who is merely safek (סָפֵק)—uncertain—whether Mashiach will come is thereby a koifer (כּוֹפֵר) in the Torah, in the Nevi’im (נְבִיאִים), and in HaKadosh Baruch Hu (הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא). Denial of any of the specific dynastic details—zera Beis David (זֶרַע בֵּית דָּוִד), through Shlomo—carries the same weight. In Hilchos Melachim (הִלְכוֹת מְלָכִים)[3] he codifies the operational criteria by which the messianic era is to be identified: the restoration of malchus Beis David (מַלְכוּת בֵּית דָּוִד), the rebuilding of the Beis HaMikdash, kibbutz galuyos (קִבּוּץ גָּלוּיּוֹת), and the return of all the mishpatim (מִשְׁפָּטִים) of the Torah. In Hilchos Teshuvah (הִלְכוֹת תְּשׁוּבָה)[4] he counts ha’kofrim b’Vias HaGo’el (הַכּוֹפְרִים בְּבִיאַת הַגּוֹאֵל) among those who forfeit their cheilek l’olam ha’ba (חֵלֶק לְעוֹלָם הַבָּא).

That the Rambam should treat mere doubt as kefirah (כְּפִירָה) is itself worthy of pause. Most of the ikrim tolerate safek up to the point of positive denial; this one does not. The reason lies in what the ikar is asked to bear. Of all the principles, this is the one most exposed to the erosion of lived experience—century after century of galus (גָּלוּת), of tzaros (צָרוֹת), of persecutions that would seem to falsify the promise. For an ikar so vulnerable, only a fence higher than the ordinary threshold of denial can preserve it intact.

II. The Prophetic Foundation

The Rambam’s formulation rests upon explicit pesukim (פְּסוּקִים). Four of them stand at the center.

וְיָצָא חֹטֶר מִגֵּזַע יִשַׁי, וְנֵצֶר מִשָּׁרָשָׁיו יִפְרֶה. וְנָחָה עָלָיו רוּחַ ה’...
A shoot shall come forth from the stump of Yishai; a branch shall grow from his roots. The spirit of Hashem shall rest upon him.
Yeshayahu 11:1–2

The classical messianic prophecy of a descendant of Yishai—and thus of David, and of Shlomo—who will execute justice and bring universal peace. This prophecy has been drawn deeply into the calendar of Klal Yisrael (כְּלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל): it stands as the haftarah (הַפְטָרָה) read on the last day of Pesach,[5] and the Chief Rabbinate of the State of Israel enacted in 1948 that it be read on Yom Ha’atzmaut. The prophet Malachi speaks in the same register:

הִנְנִי שֹׁלֵחַ מַלְאָכִי וּפִנָּה דֶרֶךְ לְפָנָי, וּפִתְאֹם יָבוֹא אֶל הֵיכָלוֹ הָאָדוֹן.
Behold, I send My messenger, and he shall clear the way before Me; and suddenly the master shall come to his palace.
Malachi 3:1

The mefarshim (מְפָרְשִׁים) identify ha’adon (הָאָדוֹן) here not as HaKadosh Baruch Hu Himself but as a designated agent—angelic or human—who will effect the geulah (גְּאוּלָּה). And in nevuas Bilam (נְבוּאַת בִּלְעָם), the Pentateuchal source cited by the Rambam himself in Hilchos Melachim: דָּרַךְ כּוֹכָב מִיַּעֲקֹב וְקָם שֵׁבֶט מִיִּשְׂרָאֵל—Chazal (חַזַ"ל) read this as referring to the future Mashiach. Rabbi Akiva famously extended the identification to Bar Kochba; whether Rabbi Akiva himself formally retracted or whether the identification simply collapsed with Bar Kochba’s defeat is disputed among the Rishonim (רִאשׁוֹנִים), but the messianic reading of the pasuk (פָּסוּק) stands.[6]

The fourth pasuk is the source of the second component of the ikar (עִיקָר)—the obligation of anticipation itself:

כִּי עוֹד חָזוֹן לַמּוֹעֵד, וְיָפֵחַ לַקֵּץ וְלֹא יְכַזֵּב; אִם יִתְמַהְמָהּ חַכֵּה לוֹ, כִּי בֹא יָבוֹא לֹא יְאַחֵר.
For there is still a vision for the appointed time… if it tarries, await it, for it will surely come; it will not delay.
Chavakuk 2:3

This pasuk is the explicit textual foundation for chikui (חִיכּוּי): not merely to believe, but to await; and to await precisely under conditions of apparent delay. It is the second component of the twelfth ikar, and every subsequent debate about the meaning of chikui is, in effect, a debate about how to read the words chakeh lo (חַכֵּה לוֹ).

III. The Plurality of Frameworks Among the Rishonim

The Rambam’s formulation was influential, but it was not the only such formulation, nor was it universally accepted as authoritative. Rav Saadia Gaon, in Emunos v’De’os (אֱמוּנוֹת וְדֵעוֹת),[7] presents ten foundational principles. Rabbi Yosef Albo, in Sefer HaIkarim (סֵפֶר הָעִיקָרִים),[8] reduces the count to three core principles—metzius Hashem (מְצִיאוּת ה’), Torah min haShamayim (תּוֹרָה מִן הַשָּׁמַיִם), and schar v’onesh (שָׂכָר וָעֹנֶשׁ)—from which the others derive. Still other Rishonim (רִאשׁוֹנִים) arrive at six, at eight, at ten.

Most of these disagreements are categorical rather than substantive: one principle in one framework encompasses two or three in another. On the ground, Klal Yisrael (כְּלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל) accepted the Rambam’s thirteen as the working list, and did so with particular firmness regarding the twelfth ikar (עִיקָר). But the plurality of frameworks itself matters, because it establishes that the number thirteen and the specific taxonomy are not themselves the object of emunah (אֱמוּנָה)—the underlying beliefs are.

The Maharshal, the Arizal, and Liturgical Dissent

Not every gadol (גָּדוֹל) recited Ani Ma’amin (אֲנִי מַאֲמִין) or Yigdal (יִגְדַּל) daily. The Maharshal[9] raised a principled objection: who gave the Rambam the authority to isolate thirteen matters as the ikrim (עִקָּרִים)? Every detail of the Torah is essential; there is no warrant to designate a hierarchy of importance among them. The Arizal, similarly, did not recite Yigdal—the poetic version of the thirteen ikrim attributed to Immanuel of Rome[10]—and many siddurim (סִדּוּרִים) note explicitly, Ha’Arizal lo amar Yigdal (הָאֲרִיזַ"ל לֹא אָמַר יִגְדַּל). Following the Arizal, many Chassidishe communities do not say it either.

The dissent is liturgical rather than doctrinal. None of these gedolim (גְּדוֹלִים) denied the underlying beliefs; they declined to ritualize them in fixed daily formulae. This is a critical distinction, and it defines the terrain on which a later academic challenge to the Rambam’s framework must be measured.

IV. The Meta-Halachic Question — Dr. Marc Shapiro’s Challenge

The transition from the Rishonim (רִאשׁוֹנִים)’ own plurality of frameworks to modern academic reappraisal is a transition in register. The Rishonim operated within the tradition and disagreed within it; the academic historian stands outside it and describes it. Yet the academic case can raise questions that bear directly on how the tradition understands itself, and one such case has been pressed with unusual force.

Dr. Marc B. Shapiro, whose doctoral training is at Harvard and who serves as professor at the University of Scranton, published in 2003 a book titled The Limits of Orthodox Theology: Maimonides’ Thirteen Principles Reappraised.[11] The work catalogs, across the sweep of the Rishonim and Acharonim (אַחֲרוֹנִים), the substantial number of authorities who diverged from one or another of the Rambam’s thirteen. The book caused an uproar in the yeshivishe (יְשִׁיבִישֶׁע) world, and it deserves a careful response.

The key to responding to Shapiro is to recognize that his argument operates on two distinct levels. On the level of belief-content—whether the specific propositions the Rambam enumerates are true—he does not challenge them at all. On the level of meta-halachah—whether the denial of any of these propositions renders one a koifer (כּוֹפֵר) with the halachic consequences the Rambam attaches—he assembles a serious documentary record showing that the classification is more contested than popular presentation admits. The two questions must be separated. The emunah (אֱמוּנָה) of Klal Yisrael (כְּלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל) in the coming of Mashiach (מָשִׁיחַ) is unaltered by anything Shapiro writes. What is contested is the meta-halachic label.

The response of the tradition, faced with that meta-halachic claim, is not that the academic mapping is false—the mapping is what it is—but that the working reality of Klal Yisrael is not built on the widest possible base of Rishonim-averaging. It is built on mesorah (מְסוֹרָה): on which Rishonim and which Acharonim our rabbeim (רַבֵּיהֶם), our yeshivos (יְשִׁיבוֹת), and our manhigim (מַנְהִיגִים) have taken as authoritative. On the twelfth ikar (עִיקָר) in particular, the community has cleaved to the Rambam’s framework, and the halachic teeth—safek (סָפֵק)-as-kefirah (כְּפִירָה), exclusion from cheilek l’olam ha’ba (חֵלֶק לְעוֹלָם הַבָּא)—follow from that cleaving. The meta-halachic question is thus not settled by counting Rishonim; it is settled by the reception history of a particular Rishon’s framework within the mesorah-line that constitutes the living tradition.

V. The Difficult Gemara — Rabbi Hillel in Sanhedrin

The Gemara (גְּמָרָא) in Sanhedrin (סַנְהֶדְרִין)[12] records a statement that has troubled the Rishonim (רִאשׁוֹנִים) from the earliest generations. Rabbi Hillel—one of several Amoraim (אָמוֹרָאִים) bearing that name in the Talmud (תַּלְמוּד), identified by some Rishonim as a grandson of Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi—said:

אֵין מָשִׁיחַ לְיִשְׂרָאֵל, שֶׁכְּבָר אֲכָלוּהוּ בִּימֵי חִזְקִיָּהוּ.
There is no Mashiach for Israel, for they have already consumed him in the days of Chizkiyahu.

An Amora (אָמוֹרָא) denying the future Mashiach (מָשִׁיחַ). Rashi[13] clarifies the intent: Rabbi Hillel did not deny the geulah (גְּאוּלָּה)—he denied a personal Mashiach, holding that HaKadosh Baruch Hu (הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא) would bring the redemption directly, without an intermediary melech ha’mashiach (מֶלֶךְ הַמָּשִׁיחַ). The Gemara immediately responds: Amar Rav Yosef: shari leih marei l’Hillel (שָׁרֵי לֵיהּ מָרֵיהּ לְהִלֵּל)—may his Master forgive Hillel, for he erred in his interpretation.

The apparent difficulty admits of a formal resolution: Rabbi Hillel pre-dates the psak (פְּסָק); the tradition that followed him crystallized in a decisive rejection of his view. What was, in his generation, an interpretive position within a live discussion became, after that discussion had closed, a rejected view. Denial of a personal Mashiach today, in the wake of the tradition’s definitive settlement of the question, carries the weight of kefirah (כְּפִירָה) that it did not carry in Rabbi Hillel’s own hour.

Yet a deeper tension remains. If Rabbi Hillel could hold this position without being classified as a koifer (כּוֹפֵר) in his own generation, then either the twelfth ikar (עִיקָר) as the Rambam formulates it is itself the product of a later crystallization of the tradition—which is a version of the observation Shapiro presses—or the ikar was always in place and Rabbi Hillel was operating with a defective transmission of it. Neither disposition is fully comfortable. The most defensible reading is that the substantive content of the ikar—that there will be a redeemer of Klal Yisrael (כְּלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל)—was always in place; what crystallized later, in the wake of the Gemara’s decisive treatment of Rabbi Hillel’s view, was the specific requirement that the redeemer be personal. The category-boundaries of an ikar can sharpen over time even as its core stands unchanged. That the tradition itself displays this development is not a scandal; it is what it looks like for the mesorah (מְסוֹרָה) to be alive.

VI. The Central Machloikes on the Meaning of Chikui

The Rambam’s requirement of chikui (חִיכּוּי) admits of two readings, and the great poskim (פּוֹסְקִים) of the modern era have divided along the fault-line between them.

The Brisker Position

Rav Moshe Feinstein, in a teshuvah (תְּשׁוּבָה) in Igros Moshe (אִגְּרוֹת מֹשֶׁה),[14] takes a maximalist position. It is not enough to believe that perhaps Mashiach (מָשִׁיחַ) will come today; that is too weak a formulation. The Rambam’s true requirement is that one believe certainly Mashiach will come today; if not today, then certainly tomorrow; if not tomorrow, then certainly the day after. Anything less than positive daily certainty falls short of chikui. The Brisker Rav[15] holds the same. Confronted with the obvious kashya (קֻשְׁיָא)—how can one believe that Mashiach will certainly come today when the Gemara (גְּמָרָא) teaches that he does not come on Erev Shabbos, and the Shaagas Aryeh (שַׁאֲגַת אַרְיֵה)[16] displays the machloikes (מַחְלוֹקֶת) over whether he comes in Nisan (נִיסָן) or in Tishrei (תִּשְׁרֵי)—he famously replied: When Mashiach comes, I’ll answer that question too.

The Brisker Rav extended the principle further. The brachah (בְּרָכָה) in Shemoneh Esrei (שְׁמוֹנֶה עֶשְׂרֵה) reads ki l’yeshuascha kivinu kol ha’yom (כִּי לִישׁוּעָתְךָ קִוִּינוּ כָּל הַיּוֹם)—not merely every day, but the entire day: constant, moment-to-moment expectancy. Mi zoche (מִי זוֹכֶה)—who reaches such a madreigah (מַדְרֵגָה)? Few. But that is the standard the Brisker Rav identified in the words of the tefillah (תְּפִלָּה) themselves.

The Chofetz Chaim’s Position

The Chofetz Chaim[17] read the Rambam’s requirement of chikui differently—not as positive daily certainty but as the absence of yeush (יֵאוּשׁ). A Jew must never despair; must never conclude that Mashiach is not coming. So long as his tefillah contains sincere, hopeful yearning for the geulah (גְּאוּלָּה), the ikar (עִיקָר) is fulfilled. Lacking moment-to-moment certainty is not kefirah (כְּפִירָה); only the positive despair of the eventual geulah is.

The Chofetz Chaim’s reading and the Brisker reading are not simply two rival readings of the same words; they are two different accounts of what chikui asks of a Jew. The Brisker reads chikui as a demand on the content of belief—certainty about a specific metzius (מְצִיאוּת) at every moment. The Chofetz Chaim reads chikui as a demand on disposition—the orientation of the nefesh (נֶפֶשׁ) toward hope and away from despair. The two can be viewed as floor and ceiling of a single continuum, with the Chofetz Chaim identifying the threshold below which one fails the ikar and the Brisker Rav describing the height toward which one aspires. This picture holds both positions within a single frame without collapsing the difference between them.

Reb Chaim Brisker’s Distinction Between the Am Ha’aretz and the Koifer

Rav Chaim Brisker is reported to have drawn a distinction that undergirds the Brisker side of the discussion. A Jew who fails to keep halachah (הֲלָכָה) out of ignorance is an am ha’aretz (עַם הָאָרֶץ), not a koifer (כּוֹפֵר). He can do al cheit (עַל חֵטְא) on Yom Kippur (יוֹם כִּפּוּר), learn the halachah for next year, and be forgiven. But a Jew who is missing one of the thirteen ikrim (עִקָּרִים)—who genuinely does not believe in Mashiach (מָשִׁיחַ), even if the failure is "by mistake"—is, in the factual reality of his soul, a koifer. Emunah (אֱמוּנָה) is not graded by intent; it is graded by content. The Brisker Rav holds the same. Whether this distinction should be pressed to its full halachic conclusion in the public arena is precisely what divides the Brisker camp from the Chofetz Chaim: the Chofetz Chaim, in effect, redefines the content-threshold downward to the absence of despair, thereby placing the ordinary Jew safely inside the ikar so long as he does not affirmatively give up.

A Contemporary Application

Some years before the present shiur, Rav Aharon Leib Shteinman publicly transmitted the Chofetz Chaim’s reading of chikui. Extreme chareidi (חֲרֵדִי) voices in Eretz Yisrael (אֶרֶץ יִשְׂרָאֵל) responded with pashkevilim (פַּשְׁקְוִילִים) that—according to accounts—charged him with kefirah in emunas Bias HaMashiach (אֱמוּנַת בִּיאַת הַמָּשִׁיחַ).[18] Whatever the precise wording of the pashkevilim, the underlying phenomenon is the misuse of the label koifer against a gadol ha’dor (גָּדוֹל הַדּוֹר) who was transmitting a position that stands squarely within the spectrum of the Acharonim (אַחֲרוֹנִים). To call a gadol (גָּדוֹל) a koifer for repeating the Chofetz Chaim—the most widely accepted halachic authority of the modern era—discredits the accusers, not the accused. The discussion between Brisk and the Chofetz Chaim is a real machloikes; a position within that machloikes is not kefirah.

VII. The Yesod: Doctrine and Operation as Two Distinct Layers

The machloikes (מַחְלוֹקֶת) between Brisk and the Chofetz Chaim comes into focus only when a deeper distinction is named. Emunah (אֱמוּנָה) operates on one layer; halachic action operates on another. The twelfth ikar (עִיקָר) demands a daily doctrinal disposition of anticipation. It does not thereby demand that a Jew’s halachic conduct be reshaped as though Mashiach (מָשִׁיחַ) were arriving in the next hour. The doctrinal and the operational are two layers, and the halachah (הֲלָכָה) itself insists on their distinction. This is the yesod (יְסוֹד) that underlies the entire cluster of sugyos (סוּגְיוֹת) that follow, and it is the yesod that quietly qualifies the Brisker maximalism from within the Rambam himself.

Kohanim and Wine

The Gemara (גְּמָרָא)[19] considers the case of the kohen (כֹּהֵן). The Beis HaMikdash (בֵּית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ) rotated its priestly service through twenty-four mishmaros (מִשְׁמָרוֹת), each serving its designated week. A kohen who knows his mishmar (מִשְׁמָר) may not drink wine during that week—lest the Beis HaMikdash be suddenly rebuilt and he be summoned to avodah (עֲבוֹדָה), which may not be performed under the influence. If the kohen does not know his mishmar—as is true of virtually every kohen today—the initial view holds that he should abstain from wine all year, for he might be precisely the kohen needed when Mashiach arrives.

Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi rejected this stringency. He could not accept that every kohen must abstain from wine all year on the assumption of shema yibaneh ha’Beis HaMikdash (שֶׁמָּא יִבָּנֶה בֵּית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ). The Rambam himself follows Rabbi: every kohen may drink. If the Beis HaMikdash is suddenly rebuilt, kohanim (כֹּהֲנִים) who happen not to have drunk that day will be found to perform the avodah; the entire community of kohanim need not abstain in perpetuity.

The point is decisive. The very Rambam who requires daily chikui (חִיכּוּי) as emunah—on the doctrinal layer—rules on the operational layer that a kohen need not abstain from wine on the assumption of imminent avodah. The emunah is doctrinal; the operational halachah is not maximized to match. This is not an oversight but a principled two-layer architecture—and whether Rav Moshe himself in the teshuvah (תְּשׁוּבָה) addresses this internal Rambam is a question that remains open in the record as it currently stands.

Haircuts Before Yom Tov

The Shulchan Aruch (שֻׁלְחָן עָרוּךְ)[20] rules that one takes a haircut erev Yom Tov (עֶרֶב יוֹם טוֹב). Beyond k’vod ha’yom (כְּבוֹד הַיּוֹם), some poskim (פּוֹסְקִים) add the rationale of shema yibaneh ha’Beis HaMikdash. If Mashiach arrives during Yom Tov, a Jew must be ready to run to Eretz Yisrael (אֶרֶץ יִשְׂרָאֵל) to bring korbanos (קָרְבָּנוֹת); the halachah forbids entering the Azarah (עֲזָרָה) in a menuval (מְנֻוָּל) state. So one trims before Yom Tov, ready for the call. Here the operational layer does register a ripple from the doctrinal chikui—but only a ripple, not a wholesale reorganization of conduct.

Pregnant Women and the Beis HaChaim

There is a widespread minhag (מִנְהָג) that pregnant women do not enter a beis ha’chaim (בֵּית הַחַיִּים). A range of reasons is offered: concerns of tumah (טֻמְאָה), of slipping, of spiritual sensitivity. One striking reason connects the practice to Mashiach: the future need for kohanim untouched by tumas meis (טֻמְאַת מֵת) to draw the mei chatas (מֵי חַטָּאת) of the parah adumah (פָּרָה אֲדֻמָּה). Maseches Parah (מַסֶּכֶת פָּרָה) records that in the era of the Beis HaMikdash, women would relocate to a special section of Yerushalayim (יְרוּשָׁלַיִם) to give birth, and walkways were built with deliberate gaps between the bridge surface and the ground, so that tumah min ha’meis (טֻמְאָה מִן הַמֵּת) from any hidden burial below could not rise through to contaminate the children. From the moment of birth these kohanim were preserved in absolute taharah (טָהֳרָה); at three or four years of age they were the ones who drew the water. Some poskim apply this reasoning today: a pregnant woman who enters a beis ha’chaim may transmit tumah to her unborn child, and precisely such kohanic children may be needed when Mashiach comes. Whether the rationale is compelling, the minhag stands across communities.

The Nazir Whose Vow Begins When Mashiach Comes

A Jew may take a neder (נֶדֶר): on the day Mashiach comes, I will become a nazir (נָזִיר). Once such a vow has been made, he may never again drink wine—because at any moment Mashiach may come, his nezirus (נְזִירוּת) begin, and any wine in his system render him a nazir tamei (נָזִיר טָמֵא) retroactively. Yet the Gemara rules that he may drink wine on Shabbos (שַׁבָּת) and Yom Tov, because Mashiach does not come on Shabbos or Yom Tov. The Shaagas Aryeh raises the obvious challenge: if Mashiach also does not come on Shabbos or Yom Tov, and there is a machloikes whether he comes only in Nisan (נִיסָן) or only in Tishrei (תִּשְׁרֵי), what remains of the emunah that he could come every day? The Brisker Rav’s answer—when Mashiach comes, we’ll answer that too—preserves the doctrinal certainty at the cost of leaving the operational question suspended. The Chofetz Chaim’s reading resolves the tension from the other side: the daily chikui operates on the level of nefesh (נֶפֶשׁ) and disposition, not on the level of metzius (מְצִיאוּת), and no operational limitation on when Mashiach can arrive threatens a demand pitched at the level of the soul.

VIII. Living the Ikar

Given the two-layer architecture, the practical shape of daily chikui (חִיכּוּי) for the ordinary Jew is neither the Brisker Rav’s kol ha’yom (כָּל הַיּוֹם) nor a vague gesture toward hope. It is a graded structure whose base and summit have both been named.

The base, in the reading of the Chofetz Chaim, is the disciplined refusal of yeush (יֵאוּשׁ). The Jew who says the brachos (בְּרָכוֹת) of Es tzemach David (אֶת צֶמַח דָּוִד), V’lirushalayim ircha (וְלִירוּשָׁלַיִם עִירְךָ), and Tek’a b’shofar gadol (תְּקַע בְּשׁוֹפָר גָּדוֹל)—whose text is saturated with anticipation—and who says them meaning what they say, without collapsing into despair, has fulfilled the ikar (עִיקָר). From the base, one moves upward toward the Brisker kol ha’yom by learning to hold, moment by moment, the specific expectation that today, this hour, this tefillah (תְּפִלָּה), may be the one at whose end Mashiach (מָשִׁיחַ) arrives. The movement between the two is the work of a lifetime, and the two positions are best understood as marking the terrain of that work rather than as competitors for the reader’s allegiance.

One boundary marker deserves separate note. The Rambam himself insists lo yeida adam heiach yih’yu ad she’yih’yu (לֹא יֵדַע אָדָם אֵיךְ יִהְיוּ עַד שֶׁיִּהְיוּ)—no one knows how these events will unfold until they unfold. Speculative timelines and date-setting are not part of the emunah (אֱמוּנָה). The emunah is in that Mashiach will come; it is not in when. Every attempt to fix a date has ended in the humiliation of the fixer and the wounding of the emunah of those who trusted him. The doctrinal certainty of the twelfth ikar is coupled with a strict operational reticence about the details.

IX. Closing

Klal Yisrael (כְּלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל) has carried this emunah (אֱמוּנָה) for nineteen hundred years through every conceivable tzarah (צָרָה). The six chatufim (חֲטוּפִים) buried in Eretz Yisrael (אֶרֶץ יִשְׂרָאֵל) are not the first korbanos (קָרְבָּנוֹת) of the ongoing exile, and the refusal to despair on their behalf is the same refusal that has been carried by every generation since the Churban (חֻרְבָּן). The same emunah that reached for a formulaic Ani Ma’amin (אֲנִי מַאֲמִין) in Portugal in 1492 is the emunah that allows American parents to send their children to yeshiva ketana (יְשִׁיבָה קְטַנָּה) this morning while six families in Eretz Yisrael sit shiva (שִׁבְעָה). HaKadosh Baruch Hu (הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא) is not addressed from a position of comfort; He is addressed from within the tension the ikar (עִיקָר) was built to bear.

אִם יִתְמַהְמָהּ חַכֵּה לוֹ, כִּי בֹא יָבוֹא לֹא יְאַחֵר.
If it tarries, await it; for it will surely come, it will not delay.
Chavakuk 2:3

  1. Rambam, Hakdamah to Perek Cheilek (Peirush HaMishnayos on Sanhedrin, ch. 10), the twelfth ikar. — The locus classicus of the thirteen ikrim. The Rambam emphasizes that he did not proceed casually (boyde milibo); the count is the product of prolonged investigation of the pesukim and ma’amrei Chazal. The twelfth ikar, on Bias HaMashiach, includes both belief in the coming and active chikui for it; the Rambam classifies mere safek as kefirah. ↩
  2. Traditional attribution: first printed appearance of Ani Ma’amin in a Portuguese siddur of 1492. — The attribution to a Portuguese 1492 printing is transmitted in traditional accounts of the origin of the printed Ani Ma’amin text; the bibliographic record of the earliest printed siddurim (Italian Soncino prints of the late 1480s and after) does not cleanly confirm this specific attribution, and it is best treated as a traditional identification rather than a bibliographically settled fact. What is not in dispute is that the fixed formulation gained currency in the wake of the Iberian expulsions and that Klal Yisrael received it as the standard poetic distillation of the Rambam’s twelfth ikar. ↩
  3. Rambam, Mishneh Torah, Hilchos Melachim u’Milchamos, chs. 11–12. — The halachic codification of the messianic era: restoration of malchus Beis David, rebuilding of the Beis HaMikdash, kibbutz galuyos, and the return of all the mishpatim of the Torah. The chapters also supply the criteria by which a claimant’s status as Mashiach is to be evaluated. ↩
  4. Rambam, Mishneh Torah, Hilchos Teshuvah 3:6. — Counts ha’kofrim b’Vias HaGo’el among those without cheilek l’olam ha’ba. Provides the halachic teeth of the twelfth ikar: not merely a doctrinal claim but one with codified consequences for a Jew’s ultimate portion. ↩
  5. Yeshayahu 10:32–12:6; read as haftarah on the last day of Pesach. — The messianic prophecy of וְיָצָא חֹטֶר מִגֵּזַע יִשַׁי. Read as the haftarah of the last day of Pesach (the eighth day in chutz la’aretz; the seventh day in Eretz Yisrael). The Chief Rabbinate of the State of Israel enacted in 1948 that it also be read on Yom Ha’atzmaut. ↩
  6. Bamidbar 24:17 — nevuas Bilam; cf. Rambam, Hilchos Melachim 11:1. — דָּרַךְ כּוֹכָב מִיַּעֲקֹב וְקָם שֵׁבֶט מִיִּשְׂרָאֵל. Read by Chazal as referring to Mashiach; cited by the Rambam as a Pentateuchal source for the messianic nevuah. Rabbi Akiva extended the identification to Bar Kochba (see Yerushalmi Ta’anis 4:5); whether Rabbi Akiva himself formally retracted the identification or whether the identification simply collapsed with Bar Kochba’s defeat is disputed. The classical rebuke of R’ Yochanan ben Torta — Akiva, grass will grow from your cheeks and Mashiach still will not have come — is preserved in the same sugya. ↩
  7. R’ Saadia Gaon, Emunos v’De’os — ten foundational principles of emunah. — Pre-Rambam articulation of an ikrei emunah framework, with ten principles rather than thirteen. Often the same content as the Rambam’s, organized differently. The plurality of frameworks in the Rishonim is part of what makes it possible for later poskim to insist that the number thirteen and the specific taxonomy are not themselves the object of emunah. ↩
  8. R’ Yosef Albo, Sefer HaIkarim, Ma’amar I — three ikarim with derivative principles. — Albo’s reduction to three core ikrim (metzius Hashem, Torah min haShamayim, schar v’onesh), with the others as derivative. Treats Bias HaMashiach as derivative rather than foundational; a fifteenth-century framework that shaped the broader Rishon conversation. ↩
  9. Maharshal (R’ Shlomo Luria), drashos (general reference). — The Maharshal’s principled objection to the Rambam’s selection of thirteen ikrim: every detail of the Torah is essential, and there is no authority to designate a hierarchy of importance. He himself did not recite Ani Ma’amin daily. The objection is liturgical rather than doctrinal. ↩
  10. Arizal, Sha’ar HaKavanos — non-recitation of Yigdal; Immanuel of Rome as traditional author. — The Arizal did not recite Yigdal, and many siddurim preserve the note Ha’Arizal lo amar Yigdal. Many Chassidishe communities follow the Arizal in omitting it. Yigdal itself — the poetic version of the thirteen ikrim — is widely attributed to Immanuel of Rome (Immanuel ben Solomon, c. 1261–c. 1328), the Italian-Jewish poet and author of Machbarot Immanuel. Alternative attribution is sometimes given to R’ Daniel ben Yehudah HaDayan. The poem is included in many siddurim regardless of which attribution is accepted. ↩
  11. Marc B. Shapiro, The Limits of Orthodox Theology: Maimonides’ Thirteen Principles Reappraised (Littman Library / Oxford, 2003). — Academic survey cataloging Rishonim and Acharonim who diverged from one or another of the Rambam’s thirteen. The book’s argument operates at the meta-halachic level (whether denial constitutes kefirah) rather than at the level of belief-content; the tradition’s response is that the meta-halachic classification follows the mesorah-reception of a specific Rishon’s framework, not the widest possible base of Rishonim-averaging. Shapiro received his doctorate from Harvard and teaches at the University of Scranton. ↩
  12. Sanhedrin צ״ט ע״א (99a) — Rabbi Hillel’s statement. — אֵין מָשִׁיחַ לְיִשְׂרָאֵל, שֶׁכְּבָר אֲכָלוּהוּ בִּימֵי חִזְקִיָּהוּ. Rav Yosef’s response: shari leih marei l’Hillel — may his Master forgive him for the interpretive mistake. Rabbi Hillel is one of several Amoraim bearing that name in the Talmud; some Rishonim identify him as a grandson of Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi, though the identification is not uniform in the Rishon literature. ↩
  13. Rashi on Sanhedrin 99a, s.v. ein Mashiach l’Yisrael. — Rashi clarifies that Rabbi Hillel did not deny geulah; he denied a personal Mashiach, holding that HaKadosh Baruch Hu would redeem directly. Once Chazal rejected this view and paskened for a personal Mashiach, denying that today is kefirah. Rabbi Hillel pre-dates the psak; we live after it. ↩
  14. R’ Moshe Feinstein, Igros Moshe, Yoreh De’ah IV, siman 36 (general reference to the teshuvah on daily chikui). — Rav Moshe’s maximalist reading of the Rambam’s chikui: not perhaps today, but certainly today; if not today, then certainly tomorrow. Anything less than positive daily certainty falls short of the ikar. Aligns with the Brisker camp. The teshuvah is customarily cited to Yoreh De’ah, though editions vary; the substantive position is consistent across the printed corpus. ↩
  15. The Brisker Rav (R’ Yitzchak Ze’ev Soloveitchik), oral teachings preserved by talmidim. — The maximalist position of kol ha’yom — continuous, moment-to-moment chikui — and the celebrated response to the Shaagas Aryeh’s kashya: when Mashiach comes, I’ll answer that question too. The Brisker Rav refuses to allow the Gemara’s operational limitations on when Mashiach can arrive to erode the doctrinal chikui. ↩
  16. Shaagas Aryeh (R’ Aryeh Leib Gunzberg), teshuvos — the kashya on daily chikui. — The kashya: the Gemara teaches that Mashiach does not come on Erev Shabbos, Shabbos, or Yom Tov, and the Acharonim debate whether he comes only in Nisan or only in Tishrei. How, then, can he "come every day"? The classic Brisker reply preserves the doctrinal certainty at the cost of suspending the operational question; the Chofetz Chaim resolves the tension by locating chikui on the level of disposition rather than metzius. ↩
  17. Chofetz Chaim — drashos and teshuvos on Bias HaMashiach (general reference). — The Chofetz Chaim reads the Rambam’s chikui not as positive daily certainty but as the absence of yeush: a Jew must never despair, must never conclude that Mashiach is not coming. Sincere yearning in the brachos saturated with chikui fulfills the ikar; lacking moment-to-moment certainty is not kefirah. The reading transmitted by Rav Aharon Leib Shteinman. ↩
  18. The pashkevil campaign against Rav Aharon Leib Shteinman — contemporary press accounts. — Rav Aharon Leib Shteinman was the subject of a sustained pashkevil campaign by extreme chareidi factions in Eretz Yisrael. The bulk of the documented grievances concerned chinuch and military-service questions; the specific charge that he was a koifer in emunas Bias HaMashiach for transmitting the Chofetz Chaim’s reading of chikui is a distinct thread within that broader campaign and worth verifying against the pashkevil texts themselves before pressing it as a firm historical assertion. The point of principle stands regardless of the precise wording: the label koifer cannot properly be applied to a gadol transmitting a position that lies within the recognized Acharonim-spectrum on the meaning of chikui. ↩
  19. Ta’anis י״ז ע״א (17a–b); cf. Sanhedrin 22b for related material. — The sugya on kohanim and wine, the twenty-four mishmaros, and Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi’s rejection of a year-round universal stringency. The Rambam (Hilchos Bias HaMikdash) follows Rabbi: every kohen may drink. The primary sugya is Ta’anis 17; Sanhedrin 22b is a parallel reference. Structurally the ruling is the sharpest single indication that chikui is doctrinal, not maximally operational. ↩
  20. Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chaim 529 (haircut erev Yom Tov); cf. 260 (Erev Shabbos). — Beyond k’vod ha’yom, some poskim add the rationale of shema yibaneh ha’Beis HaMikdash: if Mashiach arrives during Yom Tov, one must run to Eretz Yisrael for the avodah and may not enter the Azarah in a menuval state. An operational ripple of the doctrinal chikui. ↩
  21. Mishnayos Parah, ch. 3; cf. Rambam, Hilchos Parah Adumah. — The protocol for preserving kohanic children in absolute taharah for the drawing of the mei chatas: a special section of Yerushalayim for births, walkways built with deliberate gaps to prevent tumah min ha’meis from rising through, isolation through age three or four. The basis for the contemporary minhag that pregnant women avoid the beis ha’chaim — preserving the possibility of parah adumah-eligible kohanim for the era of Mashiach. ↩
