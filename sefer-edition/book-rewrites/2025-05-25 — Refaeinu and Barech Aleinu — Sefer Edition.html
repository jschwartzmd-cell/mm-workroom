Refaeinu and Barech Aleinu — Healing, Sustenance, and Rain
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Sunday Morning Series on Tefillah
Recorded 25 May 2025  ·  Sefer Edition

The eighth and ninth ברכות of שמונה עשרה, taken together with the הלכה of ותן טל ומטר that is embedded in the ninth. Three times each day the mispallel asks the Ribbono Shel Olam for the refuah and the parnasah of כלל ישראל — and in the ninth brachah he adds, in its season, the request for the rain upon which all of life rests.

Part One  ·  בִּרְכַּת רְפָאֵנוּ

I. The Eighth Brachah

Why the Eighth Slot

Three times each day the mispallel is afforded the opportunity to ask the Ribbono Shel Olam for refuah (רפואה). The content of the brachah carries two distinct requests at once: that those who are cholim (חולים) be restored to health, and that those who are not yet cholim be guarded from becoming so. The pastoral opening of the brachah is universal in scope — every member of כלל ישראל stands in need of one or the other.

The Gemara in Megillah[1] teaches that this brachah was placed in the eighth position by the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah in correspondence with the mitzvah of bris milah (ברית מילה), which is performed on the eighth day. The Gemara’s formulation is refuah kenegeд милаh she’nitnah ba’shemini (רפואה כנגד מילה שנתנה בשמיני) — every Jew is in need of refuah after his bris, and the brachah whose entire subject is refuah is set in the slot that marks the day on which the need first arises.

The First Baruch Rofei Cholim

The Midrash[2] records that the very first time the words Baruch Rofei Cholim (ברוך רופא חולים) were uttered in this world was in response to the refuah that the Ribbono Shel Olam brought to Avraham Avinu immediately after his bris. The Midrash’s word is miyad (מיד) — without delay the Ribbono Shel Olam healed him; the malachei ha’shareis (מלאכי השרת) opened their mouths and uttered the brachah. The formula of the eighth brachah traces, in this reading, directly to that moment.

A seeming difficulty presents itself. Avraham Avinu was not himself nimol on the eighth day of his life; his bris was at the age of ninety-nine. In what sense, then, does the eighth-day pattern begin with him? The answer is that the brachah models the standard case rather than the unique one. Avraham inaugurated the mitzvah; every subsequent bris performed within כלל ישראל is performed on the eighth day; and the brachah is set to the standard. The refuah that the Ribbono Shel Olam brought to Avraham is paradigmatic in content — refuah after bris — even where it is not paradigmatic in timing.

The Chasimah — Rofei Cholei Amo Yisrael

The chasimah of the brachah reads Baruch Attah Hashem, rofei cholei amo Yisrael (ברוך אתה ה' רופא חולי עמו ישראל). A question arises directly from the Midrash above. The malachim at the moment of Avraham’s refuah uttered only Baruch Rofei Cholim — universal in scope, no reference to עם ישראל, which did not yet exist. Why then does our chasimah narrow the refuah to כלל ישראל?

One answer flows from the structural tie to bris milah: the brachah as instituted by the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah is the refuah that follows the bris, and that bris is a mitzvah given only to עם ישראל. The chasimah tracks the shape of the brachah. A deeper answer emerges from the yesod of cholei ha’nefesh developed below in Section IV: the brachah contains within it a request for refuas ha’nefesh, and cholei ha’nefesh in the sense of a neshamah in need of teshuvah is a category that applies uniquely to a Jew. The opening Refaeinu addresses the universal human need for healing; the chasimah narrows the frame to the refuas ha’nefesh that is particular to כלל ישראל. Opening and closing in this brachah are not symmetrical, and the architecture is deliberate.

II. The Permission to Insert a Yehi Ratzon

The brachah of Refaeinu occupies a distinguished place among the middle brachos of Shemoneh Esrei: it is one of only two brachos — the other being Birkas HaShanim, the subject of Part Two below — in which Chazal expressly permit the mispallel to pause and insert a personal bakashah on behalf of a specific individual. The Gemara’s basis is the principle of me’ein ha’brachah (מעין הברכה) — a bakashah that lies within the subject matter of the brachah may be added at that point. For Refaeinu, the permitted addition is a yehi ratzon on behalf of a specific choleh; for Birkas HaShanim, the permitted addition is a bakashah for relief from dochak parnasah (דוחק פרנסה).[3]

The standard formulation of the yehi ratzon for a choleh reads:

יְהִי רָצוֹן מִלְּפָנֶיךָ, ה' אֱלֹקַי וֵאלֹקֵי אֲבוֹתַי, שֶׁתִּשְׁלַח מְהֵרָה רְפוּאָה שְׁלֵמָה מִן הַשָּׁמַיִם — רְפוּאַת הַנֶּפֶשׁ וּרְפוּאַת הַגּוּף — לַחוֹלֶה פְּלוֹנִי בֶּן פְּלוֹנִית בְּתוֹךְ שְׁאָר חוֹלֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל.
May it be Your will, Hashem my God and the God of my fathers, that You send swiftly a complete healing from Heaven — healing of the soul and healing of the body — to the ill one, [name] son of [mother’s name], among the other ill of Yisrael.

Where the Yehi Ratzon Is Said

A machlokes acharonim addresses whether the yehi ratzon is best inserted within Refaeinu itself or deferred until Shema Koleinu (שמע קולנו). The Ran[4] rules that one may say it in Refaeinu, on the strength of the me’ein ha’brachah principle: Refaeinu is the brachah whose topic is refuah, and the insertion sits naturally within it. Some later acharonim prefer to defer the bakashah to Shema Koleinu, which is the general-purpose brachah for personal requests. The practice preserved as the principal shitah in the standard siddurim is to say it in Refaeinu.

A practice worth preserving in the name of Rabbi Ephrati is to insert a yehi ratzon within every Shemoneh Esrei, three times a day, on the ground that there is always a choleh somewhere in כלל ישראל. The mispallel need not supply a specific name on every occasion; the yehi ratzon can be said with the collective cholei amo Yisrael in view. The brachah’s permission is not conditioned on an active emergency; its exercise models the mispallel’s standing awareness that illness is somewhere present in the community that the brachah names.

A Kashya Left Open on the Siddurim

A simple empirical observation generates a kashya that admits no comfortable answer. In every standard siddur, the yehi ratzon for the choleh is printed in the margin or at the foot of Refaeinu, ready to hand. In the companion brachah of Birkas HaShanim, however — where the Gemara and the Shulchan Aruch grant exactly parallel permission to insert a personal bakashah for parnasah — no printed text appears. The asymmetry is universal across the siddur traditions. Why the siddur-printers of every generation and every edition should have supplied one and not the other is a question Rabbi Ephrati leaves open; a sevara worth considering is that a choleh is an identifiable individual whose name and condition must be fitted to the moment of tefillah, whereas dochak parnasah is a diffuse condition that the mispallel himself already carries without needing a scripted insertion. The siddur-printers may have calibrated to the typical practical case rather than to the full halachic permission. The kashya remains worth sitting with.

III. Mentioning the Name — and Why the Mother’s

When the Choleh Is Present

The Gemara in Berachos[5] derives from the shortest tefillah in the Torah — Moshe Rabbeinu’s Keil na refa na la (א-ל נא רפא נא לה) over Miriam — a specific halachah: when the choleh is present in the same room or beis ha’knesses as the mispallel, there is no need to pronounce the name; the tefillah reaches its target through proximity. Moshe stood beside Miriam and did not need to say Miriam bas Yocheved. When the choleh is absent — in another room, another city, another country — the mispallel pronounces the name so that the tefillah is directed to its intended target.

Why the Mother’s Name

For kerias ha’Torah and for calling an aliyah, the minhag is uniform: the father’s name is used — ya’amod ploni ben ploni. For tefillah on behalf of a choleh, however, the minhag inverts: the mother’s name is used — ploni ben plonis. What accounts for the inversion?

A simple pshat reason is grounded in the sugyos of Chullin and Kiddushin:[6] maternity is empirically certain, whereas paternity is presumptive (through the chazakah of rov be’ilos achar ha’ba’al). When the tefillah’s effectiveness depends upon the precise identification of the choleh in the heavenly registry, only maternity provides identity beyond doubt.

The deeper reason is developed by the Pele Yo’etz:[7]

Mentioning the father’s name in the beis din shel ma’alah invites kitrug (קטרוג). Why specifically when the choleh is a man? Because men carry the chiyuv of talmud Torah that women do not. The opening question that arises in the beis din shel ma’alah upon the mention of a man’s name is “where is the Torah he learned today?” — a question that initiates din at the moment the tefillah seeks rachamim. Mentioning the mother — a yiddishe mamme who lit neiros Shabbos, prepared the Shabbos, carried her family’s yiddishkeit — invites middas ha’rachamim rather than din. The precedent is Dovid HaMelech’s Anna Hashem ki ani avdecha, ani avdecha ben amasecha[8] — I am Your servant, the son of Your maidservant. Dovid bypasses the father and reaches the Ribbono Shel Olam through the mother. The mispallel does the same.

The Ben Ish Chai[9] rules accordingly: in tefillah for a choleh, only the mother’s name is used. The pesak is followed across most contemporary kehillos and is the standard in every siddur-text of the yehi ratzon.

IV. Refuas HaNefesh Before Refuas HaGuf

The Order in the Yehi Ratzon

The order of the two refuos in the yehi ratzon is deliberate: refuas ha’nefesh u’refuas ha’guf — the nefesh first, the guf second. The order is at first glance surprising. In the structure of Shemoneh Esrei, Teshuvah, Selichah, and Mechilah have already been davened for in the preceding brachos. Why return in Refaeinu to the nefesh?

The answer is given by the Rambam in his derashos, with parallel treatment in Shemoneh Perakim:[10] every choli ha’guf traces, in its structural diagnosis, to a choli ha’nefesh that lies beneath it. The physical symptom is the surface expression of a deeper spiritual disturbance. The doctor properly identifies the natural diagnosis; the Rambam’s teaching does not displace the natural diagnosis but overlays it with a deeper one. The tefillah on behalf of the choleh therefore addresses the deeper layer first and the surface layer second. The order of the yehi ratzon encodes the Rambam’s theology of illness.

The Register of Choli HaNefesh in This Brachah

The term choli ha’nefesh can be read in two distinct registers. In contemporary usage, choleh nefesh denotes mental or emotional illness — a register in which the present generation is be’sheler lo rich with cases. In the older halachic-musar register, choli ha’nefesh denotes a neshamah in need of teshuvah — a soul estranged from its proper station through aveiros. Both registers are live; the Rambam’s teaching in the derashos operates in the older register, and that is the register in which the brachah is to be read. The chasimah of rofei cholei amo Yisrael, read against this register, resolves naturally: cholei ha’nefesh in the sense of the neshamah in need of teshuvah is a category that applies specifically to כלל ישראל, whose neshamah is bound to the Torah’s standard of what constitutes its ill health. The tikkun for which we daven in the deeper layer of the brachah is the tikkun nishmas Yisrael.

V. Refaeinu Hashem Ve’Nerafei

The pasuk that opens the brachah — Refaeinu Hashem ve’nerafei, hoshi’einu ve’nivashei’ah — sounds at first redundant. Heal us, and we will be healed: does not the first clause already contain the second? The source is Yirmiyahu[11]:

רְפָאֵנִי ה' וְאֵרָפֵא, הוֹשִׁיעֵנִי וְאִוָּשֵׁעָה, כִּי תְהִלָּתִי אָתָּה.
Heal me, Hashem, and I will be healed; save me, and I will be saved — for You are my praise.
Yirmiyahu 17:14

The Anshei Knesses HaGedolah transposed the pasuk from lashon yachid into lashon rabbim for the communal tefillah. The redundancy is dissolved by the mefarshim in a single stroke: a doctor’s refuah is contingent and reversible — it may succeed, it may fail, and the condition may return. The Ribbono Shel Olam’s refuah, when the choleh recognizes it as His, is of a different order: durable, complete, not subject to recurrence. Rafa’eini Hashem — heal me, and — and only then — I will actually be healed. The pasuk contrasts two refuos; the second clause specifies the first.

VI. The Doctor as Shaliach

The Source of the Doctor’s Permission

The Gemara in Bava Kamma[12] derives from the pasuk v’rapo yerapei (ורפא ירפא) both the heter and the chiyuv of medical practice. One who possesses the skill to heal another is permitted — and, where capable, obligated — to exercise it. The doctor’s years of training, the vast investment of time and money that produces him, the full body of his acquired skill, is a chesed from the Ribbono Shel Olam and a shelichus (שליחות) as His shaliach for the refuah of those entrusted to his care.

The nisayon that attaches to this shelichus is subtle and persistent: the doctor is positioned to mistake himself for the source of the refuah rather than its instrument. The slide into gaavah is a professional hazard. The recognition that ha’mo’ach ve’ha’koach me’ha’Kadosh Baruch Hu — the mind and the strength both come from the Ribbono Shel Olam — is itself part of the mitzvah. A doctor who holds this recognition in the moment of his most impressive technical success is a doctor who has internalized the full yesod of Refaeinu.

An Illustration from a Non-Jewish Surgeon

Rabbi Ephrati relates an account that illustrates the yesod with a mussar-sharpness that only life experience supplies. A child in his family was in need of open-heart surgery and was brought to Dr. Frank Hanley at Stanford — among the world’s foremost specialists in the pediatric procedure in question, to whom thousands come from every continent. The expectation, given the surgeon’s unique standing, was arrogance; what the family encountered was its opposite. After a surgery that extended for nineteen to twenty hours, late in the night, the surgeon brought the father in to tell him how it had gone. The child was alive. The father, overwhelmed, took the surgeon’s hand and said simply, “Doctor, thank you so much.” He had been saying Tehillim for the duration; he had been thanking the Ribbono Shel Olam; but in the moment he clung to the surgeon as if he were the savior. The surgeon answered with full menuchas ha’nefesh: “This is my job. My hands are guided by God.” The surgeon, a non-Jew, had modeled the yesod of the shaliach-not-source distinction more cleanly than many a ba’al tefillah; and in modeling it, he quietly became the maggid shiur’s teacher.

VII. The Nisayon of Bikkur Cholim

The mitzvah of bikkur cholim (ביקור חולים) carries a specific nisayon that belongs to this brachah’s subject matter rather than to a general mussar topic. The visitor arrives in the hospital room as the healthy one; the choleh is the one in need. The structural asymmetry invites a quiet thought that is itself the nisayon: “I am healthy; you are not. You need me.” Over time, the thought can harden into gaavah — the visitor’s sense of his own standing in performing the mitzvah, independent of its object.

The self-awareness that defuses the nisayon is a work of tefillah, not merely of general mussar. Rabbi Ephrati recounts that as a bachur he spent weeks of bein ha’zemanim in Eretz Yisrael walking the halls of Hadassah Hospital, bringing food and visits. He noticed that he came home each evening feeling better, fuller — and when he analyzed what had happened, the answer was that he had felt needed. The feeling itself was neither good nor bad; the awareness of the feeling is what matters. A visitor who notices that he is being nourished by being needed can hold the nourishment at arm’s length and keep the mitzvah directed outward. A visitor who does not notice is on his way to the slow transformation of chesed into display.

The Gemara adds a datum that bears on the structure of bikkur cholim: a visitor who is ben gilo (בן גילו) — sharing a certain resonance with the patient — takes away a sixtieth of the illness. The Rishonim and the Rashba debate the precise meaning of ben gilo; the pointer in every reading is to some shared register between visitor and choleh, such that the visit is efficacious at a level deeper than its surface sociability.

VIII. The Brachah of Parnasah

Agricultural Form, Universal Content

The ninth brachah — Barech Aleinu (ברך עלינו) in the nusach for the rainy season, Barech-einu (ברכנו) in the summer formulation, together known as Birkas HaShanim — reads on its surface as a thoroughly agricultural tefillah. Every lashon of the brachah — the tevuah (תבואה), the adamah, the produce, the harvest — is drawn from the vocabulary of farming. In an agricultural economy, this is the brachah for parnasah, and it would have been said with full kavvanah by every member of the kehillah.

In the economy of the present day, the agricultural idiom has stretched to cover every form of livelihood. The brachah is to be davened by the dentist, the surgeon, the broker, the merchant, the practitioner of every trade; the bakashah for the tevuah of the field encompasses the full range of callings by which the mispallel feeds his household.

The Chabad Kavvanah for the Mitzvos of the Year

A particular kavvanah is preserved in the Chabad tradition and recorded in collections of Minhagei Chabad[13]: when the mispallel recites Barech Aleinu, he is to have in mind not only his own parnasah but the agricultural produce required for the mitzvos of the coming year. The specific items are three:

  • Good wheat for matzah on Pesach.
  • Fine minim for the arba minim on Sukkos.
  • Good wine from good gefanim (גפנים) for kiddush.

Three times each day, read with this kavvanah, the mispallel is davening for an esrog. The kavvanah is simple to plug in and sweet to carry; Rabbi Ephrati relates that he adopted it for himself from this source.

IX. The Baal Shem Tov’s Kashya

The Sugya of Mezonos Ketzuvim

The Gemara in Beitzah[14] teaches that mezonosav shel adam ketzuvin lo me’Rosh HaShanah ad Rosh HaShanah (מזונותיו של אדם קצובין לו מראש השנה ועד ראש השנה) — a person’s sustenance is decreed for him on Rosh HaShanah for the full year to follow. The Gemara notes one exception: Shabbos, Yom Tov, and the talmud Torah of the children (schar limud) are reimbursed above the quota. The kashya is immediate. If the yearly sum is fixed, what is the mispallel seeking when he davens, three times each day, for better parnasah?

The Baal Shem Tov’s Chiddush

The Baal Shem Tov’s resolution[15] is a chiddush of the first order and is widely preserved across the early chassidishe sefarim: the amount of parnasah is decreed on Rosh HaShanah, but the quality of the parnasah is not. Money arrives in different modes. It may be berucha (ברוכה) or arura (ארורה). It may be liquid or illiquid, accessible or locked in paper, sufficient to its purposes or accompanied by hidden costs that drain it. A person may be rich on paper and short of cash in his pocket; the yearly sum has arrived, but it has arrived in a form that does not nourish. The daily tefillah is a bakashah that the decreed parnasah arrive in a form that actually sustains — berucha, usable, accessible, sufficient, calm.

The Last Minchah of the Year

A chain of mesorah preserved in the court of Skver renders the weight of Barech Aleinu in the mode of the tzaddikim. Reb Yankel Shalom Unger, the father of the Unger family of Skverer chassidim, in his later years settled in Miami and davened at the small shul on 37th and Prairie among the elderly chassidim wintering or settled there.[16] On every Erev Rosh HaShanah, Reb Yankel Shalom wept through the silent Shemoneh Esrei of Minchah. Asked why he wept at that specific point — with all of Rosh HaShanah itself ahead, the Aseres Yemei Teshuvah, Yom Kippur — he answered that he had heard from the elder Skverer Rebbe that the weeping belonged at the words Barech aleinu Hashem Elokeinu es ha’shanah ha’zos — bless upon us, Hashem our God, this year. What year? the Skverer Rebbe would say through the tears. The year is finished. Five minutes remain of it. The last five minutes! — and in those last five minutes, the entire year can still be mesukan. Baruch Hashem, you have a last chance.

The practice establishes a kavvanah for every day. When the mispallel reaches es ha’shanah ha’zos, he asks himself what year is it? — and remembers that the brachah he is about to say may still draw down berachah upon the remainder of a year that has not yet closed.

X. Be’Heter Ve’Lo Be’Issur

The Yaaros Devash’s Unwritten Kavvanah

In his derashos on Birkas HaShanim, Rabbi Yonasan Eibschutz, the Yaaros Devash,[17] develops a kavvanah that does not appear in the printed text of the brachah but belongs to it in substance: the mispallel is to daven that his parnasah arrive be’heter ve’lo be’issur (בהיתר ולא באיסור) — earned within the boundaries of halachah, not through gezel or any of its proximities. There is no berachah in money that is not properly the earner’s. The tefillah’s outcome must be tied to its halachic foundation, or it will not land as a berachah at all.

Every generation, the Yaaros Devash warns, requires the explicit reminder. No generation of כלל ישראל has been fully clean of the hazard; and the specific shapes in which the hazard presents itself change with the economic conditions of each era.

The Arizal’s Kabbalistic Kavvanah

Parallel to the Yaaros Devash, the Arizal, as transmitted in Shaar HaKavanos by Rabbi Chaim Vital,[18] develops a kabbalistic kavvanah for Birkas HaShanim that reaches further still: beyond davening for the parnasah itself, the mispallel is to beg the Ribbono Shel Olam for the seichel (שכל) — the cognitive and ethical clarity — to recognize the boundary between heter and issur in business and earnings, and the siyyua min ha’shamayim to remain on the right side of the boundary. The bakashah ties the brachah’s outcome to its halachic foundation at the level of the mispallel’s own decisions.

XI. Koach HaPo’el BaNif’al — The Bnei Yissaschar

A Teaching Delivered as a Principle, Never Applied to a Person

The Bnei Yissaschar,[19] drawing on the Rishonim’s principle that an effect retains the koach of its cause — koach ha’po’el ba’nif’al (כח הפועל בנפעל) — develops a mussar-teaching of unusual sharpness. People came to him with the painful kashya of their generation: why were their children going off the derech? He gave an answer that is explicitly meant to be understood as a general principle and never as an attribution against a particular family. The principle is this: money generated through gezel — even partially, even at the edges, even in forms the earner did not himself regard as gezel — carries the imprint of the cheit in its very substance. When such money is used to buy the food and the clothing of the children — the pizza, the ice cream, the chicken, the day-school tuition, the shoes — the children ingest, in the Bnei Yissaschar’s formulation, the residue of the cheit embedded in the bills. The tikkun of those children, at the level the Bnei Yissaschar is describing, is undermined at the source.

Two cautions attend the teaching. First, the Bnei Yissaschar himself insists that the principle is never to be turned into an accusation against a particular family or a particular child; the mussar is for the earner about his own earnings, not for the observer about anyone else’s. Second, the mechanism of transmission is left by the Bnei Yissaschar at the level of principle, not reduced to a physicalist account; it operates in the register of middas ha’din and of chinuch, not as a claim about particles in bread. The teaching grounds the kabbalistic kavvanah of be’heter ve’lo be’issur: the brachah’s request is not merely for the sum but for the halachic shape of its earning.

XII. Gezel Akum — Principle and Contemporary Application

The Smag’s Pesak

The Smag[20] addresses the laxity of his generation on the question of gezel akum (גזל עכו"ם). In the Rishonim’s era, many yiddin considered the issur to apply only among Jews and treated taking from non-Jews as unregulated. The Smag rules sharply that the issur is operative in both registers: gezel is gezel regardless of the identity of the victim, both as a straightforward issur gezel and as a chilul Hashem. The sugya in Tosafos Bava Kamma on gezel goy develops the parameters at length — the distinct categories of outright gezel, of ta’us akum, and of dealings with non-Jewish governments.

The Historical Backdrop

The historical conditions of pre-war Europe shaped the minhag of entire communities on this question. In many places, in many eras, the yiddin had no legitimate access to the economy; the governments under which they lived neither protected their persons nor employed them lawfully; survival often required working around the law. In those conditions, the halachic judgment of the behavior of particular individuals is not simple, and the Rishonim and Acharonim of each place addressed it as it stood.

The complication of the present era is not the behavior of the pre-war generations but the inheritance of their mentality by generations whose actual circumstances are entirely different. A Jew who lives in a country whose laws protect him, whose economy employs him legitimately, and whose public programs are offered on stated terms, no longer has the pre-war halachic cover for working around the law. The Smag’s pesak — that gezel is gezel — applies with its full force.

The Halachic Criterion

The halachic question that a sefer-reader will want to carry away is: by what criterion does a given use of a public program cross the line into gezel akum-shaped territory? The principle that emerges from the Smag and Tosafos, applied to publicly offered programs, is this: a program is offered on stated terms; one who meets those terms and takes what is offered is within heter; one who does not meet the stated terms and nonetheless takes is within issur, both as gezel proper and as chilul Hashem. The salient question is not whether neighbors or friends are enrolled, nor whether taxes have been paid, nor whether the program is felt to be generous or deserved; it is whether the particular applicant genuinely meets the stated conditions.

Where the applicant does meet the stated conditions — a large family of modest earnings who satisfies the published thresholds for a given benefit — the program is within heter, and the mussar considerations of this section do not apply to him. Where the applicant does not meet the stated conditions — and takes nonetheless, on the reasoning that the money is there or that others are taking it — the Smag’s pesak operates. The mussar point that Rabbi Ephrati presses, in the register of this brachah, is that the question — am I in heter or in issur? — is one the mispallel owes himself honestly, and the Bnei Yissaschar’s mussar of be’heter ve’lo be’issur is the brachah’s own standing reminder.

Part Three  ·  וְתֵן טַל וּמָטָר

XIII. When the Request for Rain Begins

The Two Dates

Embedded within Birkas HaShanim is the seasonal request for rain — Ve’sein tal u’matar liverachah (ותן טל ומטר לברכה). The halachah fixes two distinct dates for its onset:

  • Eretz Yisrael: from the seventh of Cheshvan.
  • Chutz La’Aretz: from the fourth of December (and from the fifth of December in the civil year preceding a leap year).

The Mishnah in Ta’anis

The Mishnah in Ta’anis[21] explains the delay in Eretz Yisrael: the olei regel who ascended to Yerushalayim for Sukkos must have time to return home to the furthest reaches of the land — traditionally reckoned by the Mishnah as the Nahar Peras — without being caught in rain on the way. From the completion of Sukkos, a two-week interval is given, and from the seventh of Cheshvan the request is added to Birkas HaShanim.

The Kashya on Chutz La’Aretz

The Shulchan Aruch rules that all of Chutz La’Aretz follows the climate of Bavel. Bavel, lying slightly to the north of Eretz Yisrael, needed its rains later; the date for asking is correspondingly later, falling — by the arithmetic of sixty days from tekufas Tishrei — on December 4 of the civil calendar. The entire diaspora follows the date.

This is one of the strangest halachos in the Shulchan Aruch. Argentina lies in the southern hemisphere, where the seasonal rhythm is inverted. Australia the same. Panama has its own climate. On what principle does every kehillah on earth daven for rain according to the climate of a region none of them inhabits, and on which many of them inhabit a seasonal opposite?

XIV. The Rosh’s Lifelong Attempt

The Teshuvah

The Rosh, Rabbeinu Asher ben Yechiel, was bothered by exactly this question through the entirety of his life. His teshuvah on the matter[22] is among the most personally revealing of the thousand or so teshuvos in his collected She’eilos U’Teshuvos.

The Rosh’s Path

The Rosh was born in Ashkenaz around 1250. The great talmid of the Maharam MiRothenburg, he came of age in a world whose foundations were being pulled out from under it. When the Maharam was captured by Emperor Rudolf I in 1286 and imprisoned at Ensisheim in Alsace, the Jewish communities of Ashkenaz raised a staggering sum for his redemption — standardly reckoned as 23,000 marks of silver — and the Maharam himself refused to permit the ransom, citing the Mishnah in Gittin[23]: ein podin es ha’shevuyim yoser mi’kedei dameihen (אין פודין את השבויים יותר מכדי דמיהן) — captives are not redeemed above market value, lest the practice incentivize further captures and endanger the broader kehillah. The Maharam remained in prison for seven years and died there in 1293; his body was redeemed fourteen years later by Rabbi Alexander Zuskind Wimpfen, who bequeathed his entire fortune for the purpose and is buried alongside the Maharam in Worms.

In the aftermath of the Maharam’s death and in the growing shadow of the Rindfleisch massacres of 1298 — which killed tens of thousands of yiddin in Germany, among them the Mordechai ben Hillel Ashkenazi, his wife Zelda, and their children — the Rosh understood that the Ashkenaz he had inherited was no longer safe for its communities. He fled, traveling through France into Spain — which in his time was divided among the Christian kingdoms of Castile and Aragon.

The First and Last Ashkenazi Chief Rabbi of Spain

What followed was, historians note, unprecedented: an Ashkenazi rav accepted as rav of a major Sefaradi kehillah. As the Rosh traveled through Barcelona, the Rashba recognized his stature and told the local kehillah to honor him and seat him on a pedestal. The Rosh eventually took the position of rav of Toledo, where he integrated the Ashkenazi and Sefaradi traditions in his own pesakim. His son, the Tur, built the Arba’ah Turim on that Ashkenazi-Sefaradi foundation; the Beis Yosef built the Shulchan Aruch on the Tur. An entire architecture of unified pesak traces back to the Rosh’s displacement and his welcome in Toledo.

The Drought and the First Night of Pesach

The teshuvah recounts the ma’aseh on which the Rosh’s larger attempt turned. A severe drought struck Spain. The winter season passed without rain. In Eretz Yisrael, Pesach marks the close of the rainy season, and mashiv ha’ruach u’morid ha’geshem ceases from Shacharis of the first day; in Spain, rain continues well into the spring. The Rosh, sitting outside his home on the first night of Pesach before kiddush, learning with his talmidim, told them that the drought had made the standard minhag inappropriate. The next morning, he instructed, the shul would continue to say morid ha’geshem rather than switch to morid ha’tal. The talmidim agreed. Word went out to the kehal. The next morning, the chazan said geshem.

The Tumult and the Yielding

The shul erupted. Rebbi, are you a navi? Who are you to change the minhag? The chachomim and zekeinim of Toledo protested; nothing in the broader situation changed; it did not begin to rain. Uncharacteristically, the Rosh backed down on the spot.

The teshuvah was written on Chol HaMo’ed Pesach — the only teshuvah in the Rosh’s collected She’eilos U’Teshuvos written on Chol HaMo’ed; he apologizes in the opening lines for the writing itself, invoking davar ha’aveid and chilul Hashem as the heterim for the permission he took from himself. He then records the long history of his lifelong attempt:

From Ashkenaz through France, through every kehillah I passed, into Sefarad, in every place I asked: why are you not asking for rain in the season when you need it? Why follow the climate of Bavel, which is not your climate? Every kehillah acknowledged that I was correct. None would change the minhag. After the whole of my life trying — I lift my hands. I will not create machlokes in כלל ישראל.

The Rosh understood the principle better than almost any rishon of his era: agudah achas (אגודה אחת) — כלל ישראל as one bound community, davening together as a single body, even at the cost of climatic incoherence between the minhag’s date and the local field. The yielding was not a concession on the merits of the halachic argument but a judgment about the halachic weight of communal unity in the minhag of tefillah. The Rosh’s own halachic position persisted; he had simply decided that there was no legitimate way to realize it without breaking the body of כלל ישראל into regional minhagim, and he judged the cost too high.

XV. Closing — Two Brachos That Open the Mispallel’s Mouth

The two brachos studied in these pages share a feature that Chazal did not grant any other middle brachah of Shemoneh Esrei: the mispallel is invited to insert, within the brachah itself, a personal bakashah that fits its theme. Refaeinu and Birkas HaShanim — refuah and parnasah — are the two brachos of Shemoneh Esrei at which Chazal permit the standardized tefillah to open and admit the mispallel’s voice directly. The pairing is not accidental. These are the two universal registers of human need: the integrity of the body and nefesh, and the means by which to sustain that body and nefesh in dignity. On both, Chazal made room for the particular individual within the collective script.

And on both, the structure of the brachah presses the mispallel to a larger frame than his individual case. Refaeinu’s chasimah binds his personal yehi ratzon for a loved one to the cholei amo Yisrael at large. Birkas HaShanim’s be’heter ve’lo be’issur, carried through from the Yaaros Devash to the Bnei Yissaschar to the Arizal, binds his personal bakashah for parnasah to the halachic character of its earning. And embedded within the second brachah, the request for rain binds his personal climate — wherever on earth he stands — to the agudah achas of a כלל ישראל that davens together across every klima. The Rosh gave his life to the attempt to realign the minhag with the local field, and in the end he gave his life to the recognition that the agudah is worth more than the alignment.

May the Ribbono Shel Olam grant to כלל ישראל refuah shelemah — refuas ha’nefesh u’refuas ha’guf; parnasah berevach, be’shefa, u’be’heter; tal u’matar li’verachah in every klima; and the ultimate geulah bimheirah be’yameinu. Amen.
