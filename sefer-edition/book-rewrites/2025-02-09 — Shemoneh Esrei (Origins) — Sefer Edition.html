שְׁמוֹנֶה עֶשְׂרֵה — רֵאשִׁית וְתוֹלָדוֹת

The Origins and Development of Shemoneh Esrei
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Sunday Morning Series on Tefillah
Recorded 9 February 2025  ·  Sefer Edition · Prepared 23 August 2026

Opening chapter of the long study of Shemoneh Esrei (שמונה עשרה): its authorship, its shape, the number of its berachos, and the historical layers that have made it into the tefillah we recite three times each day.

An introduction to Shemoneh Esrei: the machlokes (מחלוקת) whether the Avos (אבות) established tefillah (תפילה) or whether the berachos (ברכות) were fixed k'neged ha'korbanos (כנגד הקרבנות); why the count is precisely eighteen; the addition of the nineteenth berachah by Shmuel HaKatan (שמואל הקטן) in Yavneh (יבנה); the Rambam (רמב״ם)'s historical reconstruction tracing the entire enterprise to Ezra HaSofer (עזרא הסופר); and the Avudraham (אבודרהם)'s lonely shitas yachid (שיטת יחיד) on the counting of words.

I. Beginning the Journey

Having closed Birkas Ga'al Yisrael (ברכת גאל ישראל), the final berachah of the Birchos Kri'as Shema (ברכות קריאת שמע) that precede Shemoneh Esrei, we now come to Shemoneh Esrei itself: who composed it, where it originated, whether the nusach (נוסח) we recite today was always the standard, and how it developed. Much of what follows is divrei peshutim (דברים פשוטים); but before we take up each berachah individually over the coming months, the foundations require laying. A Jew davens Shemoneh Esrei three times each day; he ought to know what he is saying.

The Names of the Tefillah

Among Ashkenazim (אשכנזים) the tefillah is called Shemoneh Esrei, or simply ha'lachash (הלחש) — “the silent one.” Among Sefaradim (ספרדים) it is Tefillas HaAmidah (תפילת העמידה), after the requirement to stand — a requirement whose source is Avraham Avinu (אברהם אבינו): “Va'yashkeim Avraham ba'boker el ha'makom asher amad sham” (וישכם אברהם בבקר אל המקום אשר עמד שם). When Chazal (חז״ל) speak of tefillah without qualification, they mean specifically the Shemoneh Esrei; the rest of the daily siddur (סידור) is called tefillah only by extension.

II. The Machlokes: Avos Tiknum or K'neged Korbanos Tiknum

The First Position — Avos Tiknum

The Gemara (גמרא) in Berachos (ברכות) 26b[1] records the famous machlokes. The first view holds that the Avos instituted the three daily tefillos:

  • Avraham Avinu established Shacharis (שחרית) — from “va'yashkeim... el ha'makom asher amad sham,” where amidah (עמידה) is tefillah.
  • Yitzchak Avinu (יצחק אבינו) established Minchah (מנחה) — from “lasu'ach ba'sadeh” (לשוח בשדה), where sichah (שיחה) is tefillah.
  • Yaakov Avinu (יעקב אבינו) established Ma'ariv (ערבית) — from “va'yifga ba'makom” (ויפגע במקום), where pegi'ah (פגיעה) is supplication.

What is meant by the claim that the Avos “instituted” these tefillos? The mefarshim (מפרשים) do not suppose that Avraham, Yitzchak, and Yaakov recited our precise Shemoneh Esrei. They fixed the three times of daily tefillah; the nusach is not from them. Each Av davened in his own words — the supplications and praises particular to his own avodah.

The Second Position — K'neged Temidim Tiknum

The other shitah (שיטה): the three daily tefillos were established k'neged ha'korbanos (כנגד הקרבנות) — corresponding to the Tamid shel Shachar (תמיד של שחר), the Tamid shel Bein Ha'Arbayim (תמיד של בין הערבים), and the haktar chalavim v'evarim (הקטר חלבים ואברים) of the night. After the Churban (חורבן), tefillah stands in the place of avodah (עבודה) — it is avodah she'ba'lev (עבודה שבלב). On this shitah, the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah (אנשי כנסת הגדולה) — the one hundred and twenty chachamim whose ranks included Mordechai, Ezra, and Shimon HaTzaddik (שמעון הצדיק) among the last of their remnants[2] — are the mesakenim (מתקנים) of tefillah.

The Gemara's Synthesis

Even on the first shitah, the question presses: what of Mussaf (מוסף) — recited every Rosh Chodesh, every Shabbos, every Yom Tov? The Gemara answers that those who hold Avos tiknum agree the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah later “asmichinhu a'korbanos” — anchored the three tefillos to the korbanos and added Mussaf as k'neged Korban Mussaf. Both shitos are absorbed into the received practice: the Avos set the times, and Chazal fixed the structure and the nusach.

III. The Threefold Structure of Shemoneh Esrei

Chazal arranged the berachos in three blocks:

  1. The first three berachos — shevach (שבח), praise: Avos, Gevuros, Kedushas HaShem.
  2. The middle twelve — bakashah (בקשה), petition: Refuah, Parnasah, and the remaining requests.
  3. The final three — hoda'ah (הודאה), thanksgiving: Avodah, Modim, Shalom.

This shevach–bakashah–hoda'ah triad is not an aesthetic ordering; it is, as will emerge in the Rambam's reconstruction below, the biblical minimum shape of tefillah itself. Every human address to HaKadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא), on that view, must contain these three elements in this order — and Chazal, in fixing the Shemoneh Esrei, expanded that biblical triad into eighteen articulated berachos.

IV. Why Eighteen? Three Sources in the Gemara

The Eighteen Mentions of Hashem in Havu La'Hashem

The mizmor (מזמור) “Havu la'Hashem bnei eilim” (הבו לה' בני אלים)[3] contains eighteen references to Hashem. The eighteen berachos correspond to those eighteen Sheimos (שמות).

The Eighteen Chuliyos HaShidrah

The Gemara teaches that eighteen berachos correspond to the eighteen chuliyos ha'shidrah (חוליות השדרה) — the vertebrae of the spine. Modern anatomy counts thirty-three vertebrae in the developing spine, which fuse into twenty-six distinct bones in the adult; the Gemara's count is by a different reckoning, functional rather than descriptive.[4]

The teaching that lies within: the Pnei Yehoshua (פני יהושע) and other Acharonim bring the pasuk “kol atzmosai tomarnah” (כל עצמותי תאמרנה).[5] Most mitzvos engage specific evarim (אברים) — the hand that binds tefillin, the foot that walks to a mitzvah, the mouth that speaks Torah, the eyes that see tzitzis. How does the entire body — all of the ramach evarim (רמ״ח אברים) — engage in avodas Hashem? Through Shemoneh Esrei. When a person stands and bows through eighteen berachos, the spine — the eighteen vertebrae — moves the whole body in avodah. It is uniquely a mitzvah of the entire person.

The Eighteen Sheimos in Kri'as Shema

A third shitah in the Gemara: there are eighteen mentions of the Name in Kri'as Shema, which immediately precedes Shemoneh Esrei. Each of the three derivations is understood by many Rishonim as an asmachta (אסמכתא) rather than a binding structural claim — a placement of Chazal's tekanah against the natural landmarks of Tanach. This will matter directly when we come to the addition of the nineteenth berachah.

V. The Nineteenth Berachah

For all the reasons the count is eighteen, the truth is that our Shemoneh Esrei today contains nineteen berachos. Almost since time immemorial it has been in fact tesha esrei (תשע עשרה); only the name Shemoneh Esrei persists.

Yavneh and the Aftermath of the Churban

The Gemara in Megillah[6] reports that the nineteenth berachah was added at Yavneh in the days of Rabban Gamliel (רבן גמליאל). The Rishonim dispute which Rabban Gamliel; the location itself suggests Rabban Gamliel the second. The historical setting is the aftermath of the Churban Bayis Sheni: Rabban Yochanan ben Zakkai (רבן יוחנן בן זכאי), in his celebrated request to Vespasian,[7] said “ten li Yavneh v'chachameha” (תן לי יבנה וחכמיה). He understood that Torah she'baal peh (תורה שבעל פה) and a central seat of chachamim would carry Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל) through the long galus (גלות) ahead. The Sanhedrin (סנהדרין) moved from Lishkas HaGazis (לשכת הגזית) to Yavneh, and many tekanos were established there.

The Early Minim

The earliest Christians were themselves Jewish — me'anshei brisenu (מאנשי בריתנו). They wore the same tzitzis, attended the same batei knesses, lived as friends and neighbors of their fellow Yehudim. Chazal call them minim (מינים). For several centuries it was extraordinarily difficult to distinguish them externally from authentic Yehudim, and their malshinim (מלשינים) — informers — operated from within the community, denouncing their fellow Yehudim to the malchuyos (מלכויות). The structural need for sharp differentiation was pressing; without it, the kehillah (קהילה) faced an existential threat. It is against this background that the nineteenth berachah must be read.[8]

Rabban Gamliel and Shmuel HaKatan

Rabban Gamliel stood before the assembled chachamim at Yavneh: We must have a berachah against the minim — who among you will compose it? A great tanna (תנא) arose. It was Shmuel HaKatan — called ha'katan not for stature but for the anavah (ענוה) that defined him. He composed Birkas HaMinim (ברכת המינים); Rabban Gamliel instituted it as a tekanah. From that day, Shemoneh Esrei contains nineteen berachos. The severity of the lashon (לשון) reflects what these malshinim were and what they did.

The addition raises a real lomdishe question. If the count of eighteen is anchored in three biblical landmarks — the Sheimos of Havu La'Hashem, the eighteen chuliyos, the Sheimos of Kri'as Shema — how did the beis din at Yavneh have the koach to add a nineteenth? The mainstream reading treats those three derivations as retrospective asmachtos rather than binding structural constraints; the tekanah of the count belongs to Chazal, and what Chazal established, Chazal could adjust when the hour required.

Why Specifically Shmuel HaKatan?

The Rishonim ask what seems the obvious question. The Rosh[9] writes that any ordinary Yehudi — even a woman — could compose effective kelalos (קללות) against minim. The Rosh himself concedes he has no satisfying answer; the Rashba, the Ritva, and Rabbeinu Yonah all wrestle with the same difficulty.

The Nefesh HaChaim (נפש החיים) in Shaar II[10] supplies the deepest answer. The berachos of Shemoneh Esrei are not merely human sentiments aimed at driving minim from the batei knesses. Each word and each letter of a berachah aligns with profound spiritual structures; the berachah functions in higher worlds. To compose a berachah tuned to those structures required a person whose kavvanah (כוונה) could align every word and letter precisely. Shmuel HaKatan's anavah made him precisely that kli. The answer also frames the whole study ahead: if that is what a berachah is, the word-by-word study to come is not pilpul; it is exegesis.

VI. The Forgetting

The Gemara preserves a remarkable epilogue. A year later, Shmuel HaKatan was honored to lead the tefillah as shliach tzibbur (שליח ציבור) before Rabban Gamliel. He reached “v'la'malshinim” (ולמלשינים) — the very berachah he himself had authored — and froze. He fumbled, hemmed, could not produce the nusach.

The immediate suspicion in the beis midrash (בית מדרש) was sharp: he must be a min. Only a min would forget the very berachah against minim. Remove him from the amud. The Gemara defends him: he composed the berachah; how could he be a min? The counter: perhaps he had become one in the interim — for we hold “al ta'amin b'atzmecha ad yom mos'cha” (אל תאמין בעצמך עד יום מותך), and the Gemara brings the case of a Kohen Gadol (כהן גדול) who served faithfully for eighty years and ended his life a tzedoki (צדוקי). The Gemara concludes: this was not what happened to Shmuel HaKatan. He remained an upright Yehudi to the end.

Why then did he forget? The Rishonim answer that Shmuel HaKatan was of the type the Gemara calls “toraso umnaso” (תורתו אומנתו) — his Torah is his craft — like Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai (רבי שמעון בר יוחאי). Such a person is fully obligated in Kri'as Shema but holds a different relationship to fixed daily tefillah. After composing Birkas HaMinim, Shmuel HaKatan returned to the beis midrash and had no occasion to recite his own berachah aloud. It had not entered muscle memory; standing at last as shliach tzibbur, he could not retrieve the words. After a time — some hours — he recalled them, and tefillah continued.

VII. The Older Midrash: The Berachos of the Malachim

Beneath the historical institution by the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah and by Rabban Gamliel lies a more ancient midrash (מדרש), brought (apparently) in Pirkei d'Rabi Eliezer (פרקי דרבי אליעזר) and cited at the opening of Hilchos Shemoneh Esrei in Siman 113 by the Beis Yosef, the Kol Bo, the Shibolei HaLeket, and the Shulchan Aruch[11] — notably absent from the Tur.

The midrash: these berachos are not new compositions. They are as ancient as creation itself. The malachei ha'shareis (מלאכי השרת) were already reciting them before any human throat gave them voice. When Chazal formalized our Shemoneh Esrei, they were channeling — drawing down into our human peh (פה) and our daily siddur — the tefillah of the malachim.

The midrash stands in evident tension with the historical account that the Gemara and the Rambam supply, in which particular chachamim at particular moments compose particular berachos. The Rishonim who cite it evidently read the two accounts as complementary rather than competing. The historical account describes the vessel: the tekanah of Ezra, the composition by Shmuel HaKatan, the fixing of the nusach. The midrash describes what was poured into the vessel: a pre-existing tefillah that had waited from creation for a mouth capable of receiving it. On this reading the Nefesh HaChaim's answer about Shmuel HaKatan is not an accident but the pattern of the whole enterprise — each of the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah was the kli through which one particular berachah of the malachim was drawn into speech.

VIII. The Rambam's History

The Rambam at the opening of Hilchos Tefillah[12] supplies the most complete historical account of the Shemoneh Esrei's development.

The Daily Mitzvah

The Rambam rules — against the Ramban (רמב״ן)[13] — that daily tefillah is a mitzvas aseh min ha'Torah (מצות עשה מן התורה). But only the fact of daily tefillah is d'oraisa. The specific number of tefillos and the specific nusach are d'rabbanan. Biblically, the obligation is discharged by speaking — at any time, in any words, in any language — three elements in order: praise HaKadosh Baruch Hu; ask Him for what one needs; thank Him. This is the biblical minimum. It is precisely the shevach–bakashah–hoda'ah triad that Chazal later expanded into the eighteen articulated berachos.

The machlokes between Rambam and Ramban is not a curiosity. On the Rambam's shitah, a person unable or unfit to daven the fixed nusach — a sick person, a person who has never learned the words — discharges his chiyuv d'oraisa the moment he articulates the three-part shape in his own words; on the Ramban's shitah, there is no chiyuv d'oraisa of daily tefillah at all, and the entire obligation is d'rabbanan. The two positions bear directly on safek tefillah, on women's obligation, and on the halakhic weight of any alteration to the fixed nusach.

The Era Before Ezra

The Rambam traces the early period. From Moshe Rabbeinu (משה רבינו) through the Shoftim, the Nevi'im, and the Melachim, every Yehudi davened in his own words — at his own times, with his own emphasis, in his own lashon ha'kodesh (לשון הקודש). Moshe Rabbeinu davened as he wished in the midbar. Yehoshua bin Nun the same. The kohanim of the Mishkan and the Bayis Rishon had no fixed daily Shemoneh Esrei; the record leaves no trace of such an institution before Ezra. Which answers a natural difficulty a thoughtful child once put to his rebbi — is it not strange to thank HaKadosh Baruch Hu by reading words from a siddur? Real gratitude is personal, passionate, one's own. The Rambam answers that kushya: for the greater part of Jewish history, that is precisely how tefillah was conducted.

The Turning Point — Ezra HaSofer

The Rambam writes:

כיון שגלו ישראל בימי נבוכדנצר הרשע, נתערבו בפרס וביון ובשאר האומות... ולא היו יכולין לדבר בלשון אחת עד שתערב בלשונם משאר לשונות... וכיון שראה עזרא ובית דינו כך, עמדו ותקנו להם שמנה עשרה ברכות על הסדר.
Rambam, Hilchos Tefillah 1:4

With the galus of Nevuchadnetzar, lashon ha'kodesh itself was lost. The next generation could not speak Hebrew properly — what the Rambam calls “lashon ilegess” (לשון עילגת); Sefer Nechemiah[14] documents the linguistic collapse directly. Without a unified lashon, ordinary Yehudim could no longer compose proper tefillos in their own words. Ezra and his beis din saw this and stood up: they composed shemoneh esrei berachos al ha'seder — eighteen fixed berachos, in a fixed order, which the chacham and the am ha'aretz (עם הארץ) would recite identically.

Tefillah as we know it is, in the Rambam's framing, a product of galus: a unifying instrument for a moment in which the unifying language had been lost. Rabban Gamliel at Yavneh later reasserted and reinforced it; the foundational tekanah is Ezra's. The three accounts of authorship — k'neged korbanos by the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah, the linguistic-response tekanah of Ezra, and the ancient malachim-tefillah of the older midrash — describe one composite reality from three angles. Ezra and his beis din are of the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah; the Rambam foregrounds the linguistic occasion, while the Gemara foregrounds the korbanos correspondence, and the midrash foregrounds the metaphysical content. It is one tefillah, seen from three sides.

IX. Counting Words — and the Avudraham's Skepticism

The Rishonim — and after them particularly the Chasidei Ashkenaz (חסידי אשכנז)[15]: Rabbeinu Yehudah HaChasid, the Rokeach, and their circle, roughly one to two centuries before the Tur — counted every word and every letter of every berachah. They identified corresponding pesukim in Torah with matching counts, and built extended edifices of kavvanos (כוונות) upon them. The Tur preserves these counts; the Arizal (האר״י ז״ל) and the kabbalah (קבלה) intensify them. On the mainstream mesorah, every word of every berachah is gold.

The Avudraham's Kushya

The Avudraham[16] registers a sharp kushya on this entire enterprise. He writes that he himself once counted the words; he came to regard the exercise as futile. Why? Because even after Chazal's formalization, communities have always maintained differing nuscha'os (נוסחאות). Nusach Ashkenaz and Nusach Sefard differ in their word counts; even in his own day the variations existed. So how can the precise count be load-bearing?

The Avudraham's conclusion: do as you will; what is truly binding is the prohibition “meshaneh mi'matbei'a she'tav'u chachamim” (משנה ממטבע שטבעו חכמים) — one may not alter the chasimah (חתימה) of a berachah or the body of the berachah in a meaningful way; one may not transform “Magen Avraham” (מגן אברהם) into something else. Within that frame, individuals historically added their own supplementary language; today we no longer possess that liberty.

The Avudraham's position is a shitas yachid. The mainstream of the Rishonim and Acharonim holds that the precise nusach matters and that the counts do their work; and as we move through the berachos in the shiurim to come, b'ezras Hashem (בעזרת השם) we shall see them counting and weighing every word.

X. Closing

We began with the observation that a Jew davens Shemoneh Esrei three times each day and ought to know what he is saying. What we have laid out is the foundation of that knowing. The Avos set the times; Chazal gave the structure; Ezra and his beis din, in a moment of linguistic collapse, composed the fixed nusach; Rabban Gamliel and Shmuel HaKatan at Yavneh, in a moment of communal peril, added the nineteenth berachah. Beneath every layer, on the reading of the Zohar-tradition and of the older midrash, the berachos themselves are ancient as creation; the Nefesh HaChaim teaches that every word aligns with structures in higher worlds.

With the historical spine now in place, the study proper begins. Over the coming months we shall take the berachos one by one — Avos, Gevuros, Kedushah, and onward through bakashah and hoda'ah — counting words where the mainstream counts them, weighing the chasimos, and asking what each berachah is doing. If the Nefesh HaChaim is right, this is the work of a lifetime; if the Avudraham is right, at least the matbei'a deserves our care. On either reading, three times each day we are placing on our lips the tefillah of the malachim, transposed into the human throat by Ezra and his beis din. It is worth knowing what we are saying.

Pirkei d'Rabi Eliezer. — The older midrash, brought (apparently) in Pirkei d'Rabi Eliezer and cited at the opening of Hilchos Shemoneh Esrei: the berachos are not new compositions but as ancient as creation itself; the malachei ha'shareis were already reciting them. Chazal's formalization channels the tefillah of the malachim into our daily peh. Notably absent from the Tur. ↩
  12. Rambam, Hilchos Tefillah ch. 1 (esp. halachos 1–4). — The most complete historical reconstruction. Daily tefillah as a mitzvas aseh min ha'Torah (against the Ramban); the number of tefillos and the specific nusach as d'rabbanan. The pre-Ezra period: every Yehudi davened in his own words. With galus Bavel, lashon ha'kodesh was lost; Ezra and his beis din stood up and composed shemoneh esrei berachos al ha'seder — a unifying instrument for a generation that could no longer compose proper tefillos. ↩
  13. Ramban, Hasagos al Sefer HaMitzvos, mitzvah 5. — The Ramban's machlokes against the Rambam: daily tefillah as d'rabbanan, not as a Pentateuchal mitzvah. The obligation, on the Ramban's reading, originates with the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah rather than with any biblical chiyuv. The dispute bears directly on safek tefillah, on women's obligation on various shitos, and on the halakhic weight of any alteration to the fixed nusach. ↩
  14. Sefer Nechemiah 13:23–24. — u'vneihem chatzi medaber ashdodis v'einam makirim l'daber Yehudis. Direct documentation of the linguistic crisis of the post-galus generation, and the textual anchor for the Rambam's account of lashon ilegess driving Ezra's formalization of Shemoneh Esrei. ↩
  15. Chasidei Ashkenaz: R' Yehudah HaChasid (Sefer Chasidim, Sefer HaKavod); the Rokeach (R' Elazar of Worms) and their circle. — The systematic counters of every word and letter in every berachah; identifying corresponding pesukim in Torah; building edifices of kavvanos upon them. The Tur preserves their counts; the Arizal and kabbalah intensify them. The mainstream mesorah against which the Avudraham's shitas yachid stands. ↩
  16. R' David ben Yosef Avudraham, Sefer Avudraham, section on the counting of words in Shemoneh Esrei. — The lonely shitas yachid: the Avudraham himself used to count words and concluded the exercise futile, given that communities have always maintained differing nuscha'os with differing word counts. What is binding is meshaneh mi'matbei'a she'tav'u chachamim — one may not alter the chasimah or the body of a berachah; word-by-word counts cannot be load-bearing when even Nusach Ashkenaz and Nusach Sefard disagree. The mainstream mesorah stands against this view. ↩
