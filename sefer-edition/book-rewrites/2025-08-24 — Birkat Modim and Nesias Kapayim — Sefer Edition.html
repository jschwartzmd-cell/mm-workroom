בִּרְכַּת מוֹדִים וּנְשִׂיאַת כַּפַּיִם

Birkat Modim and Nesias Kapayim — The Thanksgiving That Remains, Modim DeRabbanan, and the Puzzle of Daily Duchening in Chutz LaAretz
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Sunday Morning Series on Tefillah
Recorded ל׳ אב תשפ״ה  ·  24 August 2025  ·  Sefer Edition

Sponsored לעילוי נשמת הרב זצ״ל, ישועה בן רחמים.

A treatise on Birkat Modim (ברכת מודים), the penultimate berachah (ברכה) of Shemoneh Esrei (שמונה עשרה): the standing pause of the Chofetz Chaim (חפץ חיים) at Modim; the kavanah (כוונה) of thanks for every breath; the Midrash (מדרש) that of all korbanos (קרבנות) and all prayers only thanksgiving will remain in the time to come.

It treats Modim DeRabbanan (מודים דרבנן) as the gathered formulations of Rav Pappa (רב פפא)’s “limeimrinhu l’tarvaihu” (למימרינהו לתרווייהו); the learning of “thank you” in English by Rav Elyashiv (רב אלישיב) at the age of one hundred and two; and the Modim that the shaliach tzibbur (שליח ציבור) must not say silently.

It then takes up the long puzzle of nesias kapayim (נשיאת כפים) in chutz laaretz (חוץ לארץ): why Ashkenazim (אשכנזים) there do not duchen (דוכן) daily; the attempts of the Gra (גר״א), Reb Chaim of Volozhin and the Baal HaTanya (בעל התניא) to restore it; Rav Nosson HaKohen Adler (רבי נתן הכהן אדלר) in Frankfurt as the one who succeeded; chezkas kehunah (חזקת כהונה) in exile; and the regional custom of the Galil (גליל).

I. The Primacy of Modim (מודים)

The Body That Bows

The Gemara (גמרא) reflects a recognition of human limits: a Jew cannot sustain full kavanah (כוונה) through every berachah (ברכה) of Shemoneh Esrei (שמונה עשרה); it is too difficult. The Yerushalmi (ירושלמי)[1] records the candid word of one of the amoraim (אמוראים): “I am fortunate that my body bows down of itself (מאליו) at Modim and at Magen Avraham (מגן אברהם) — for were it not so, I would not even know to do it.” The body knows; the mind drifts.

The standard rule is that the one berachah that requires kavanah without exception is the first, Avos (אבות), which closes with Magen Avraham (מגן אברהם). A variant reading (גרסה) of the Gemara equates Modim with it. At the least, then, one must have kavanah at one of the two, and ideally at both.

The Chofetz Chaim (חפץ חיים)

It is told that someone once had the temerity (חוצפה) to ask the Chofetz Chaim: “Rebbe (רבי), why do you stand so long during Shemoneh Esrei? Say the words with kavanah and be done.”

The Chofetz Chaim answered: “You are mistaken. I daven Shemoneh Esrei very quickly. At Modim I stop and pause. I cannot get enough of this emotion — of expressing thanks and gratitude to Hakadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא) for every moment of my life. My Shemoneh Esrei is short; the Modim is long.”

What Kavanah in Modim Requires

The berachah declares thanks to the Holy One, blessed be He, for all that He does boker v’erev v’tzohorayim (בקר וערב וצהרים). The commentators (מפרשים) explain that the soul (נשמה) entrusted to Him each night with “beyadcha afkid ruchi” (בידך אפקיד רוחי) at krias shema al hamitah (קריאת שמע על המטה) returns whole each morning. We are alive.

The Toras Chaim (תורת חיים)[2] cites earlier authorities (קדמונים) that a person should thank the Holy One for every single breath. David HaMelech (דוד המלך) closes Tehillim (תהלים) with “kol haneshamah tehallel Kah” (כל הנשמה תהלל י־ה) — every breath should praise. Were we to function as we ought, we would give thanks for each inhalation and exhalation; but who can? Hence in Modim we say “al nishmoseinu hapekudos lach” (על נשמותינו הפקודות לך) with the kavanah: we thank You for every breath. To dwell upon this and have proper kavanah takes time; that is why the Chofetz Chaim stood long at this place.

The Bowing — Equal to Avos (אבות)

A further sign of the standing of Modim is that we bow in it. We bow at Avos and at Modim — the four bows of Shemoneh Esrei. Bowing is the body’s expression of complete subservience (השתעבדות): “We are Yours.” As at Avos, so at Modim, at the opening (פתיחה) and at the close (חתימה) of the berachah: the posture is identical.

II. The Eternal Todah (תודה)

A striking Midrash (מדרש)[3] teaches that in the time to come (לעתיד לבוא) all korbanos will be annulled (בטל) except the korban todah (קרבן תודה). (The plain sense (פשט) of the Rambam (רמב״ם) is that korbanos will be brought again when Mashiach (משיח) comes; the Midrash contemplates a still later moment, at which all but the todah fall away.) The Shulchan Aruch (שולחן ערוך) brings the idea into halachah (הלכה), in the form that all songs (שירות) will be annulled except Mizmor LeTodah (מזמור לתודה) — which is why it is said to a melody. It is unusual for the Shulchan Aruch to bring a midrash; this underlines the standing of todah.

The Four Who Must Bring the Korban Todah

The Torah (תורה) knows four categories, remembered by the mnemonic “Chayim” (חיים), from “Chayim yoducha” (חיים יודוך):

  • ח — cholah shenitrapei (חולה שנתרפא): one who has recovered from illness.
  • י — yam (ים): a sea traveler.
  • י — one released from prison or from great affliction (יסורים).
  • ם — midbar (מדבר): a desert traveler.

One who would have brought the korban todah, in the absence of the Beis HaMikdash (בית המקדש), recites Birkas HaGomel (ברכת הגומל) instead. When we recite Mizmor LeTodah daily we partially discharge our obligation of todah. We cannot know how many times in the past year a todah was owed — how many times our lives were saved without our knowing.

The same principle holds for tefillah (תפילה). Since tefillah stands in place of the korbanos (כנגד הקרבנות), the Midrash teaches: as all korbanos will be annulled but the todah, so all prayers will be annulled but the prayer of thanksgiving, which is received as “Modim anachnu lach” (מודים אנחנו לך). Modim is eternal.

III. Modim DeRabbanan (מודים דרבנן)

Its Origin

The Gemara in Sotah (סוטה) 40a[4] relates that when the shaliach tzibbur (שליח ציבור) says “Modim anachnu lach,” the congregation (ציבור) bows with him and says its own formulation of thanksgiving. Several amoraim proposed different wordings (גרסאות). Rav Pappa settled it, characteristically, at the end of the sugya (סוגיא): “limeimrinhu l’tarvaihu” (למימרינהו לתרווייהו) — say them all. Rav (רב) proposed “Modim anachnu lach Hashem Elokeinu al she’anu modim lach”; Shmuel (שמואל), “Elokei kol basar al she’anu modim lach”; and Rabbi Simai (רבי סימאי), the sages of Neharde’a in his name, and Rav Acha bar Yaakov (רב אחא בר יעקב) each contributed a further formulation. As Rav Pappa ruled, we incorporate them all. This composite is Modim DeRabbanan.

Why “DeRabbanan” (דרבנן)?

It is a fair question, since everything in tefillah is from the Sages (רבנן). The answer proposed is that this berachah is called Modim DeRabbanan because it is a combination of all the wordings (לשונות) that the various amoraim proposed; the Rabbis collected them, hence “DeRabbanan.” This is one explanation among several.

Why the Congregation Says It At All

A deeper question: why does the congregation recite its own Modim? Elsewhere we are discharged through the shaliach tzibbur. But thanks cannot be offered to the Holy One through an agent (שליח)[5]; todah demands the personal voice.

Rav Elyashiv at One Hundred and Two

Rav Elyashiv (רב אלישיב זצ״ל)[6] was the posek hador (פוסק הדור) in Yerushalayim (ירושלים), the gadol hador (גדול הדור), who did not leave Yerushalayim; he did not leave his four cubits (ארבע אמות), learning day and night (יומם ולילה) for nearly a hundred years. At the age of one hundred and two he underwent open-heart surgery. The best surgeon in America was flown to Eretz Yisrael (ארץ ישראל) rather than fly Rav Elyashiv abroad.

When he woke after the surgery he turned to his attendant and asked: “How do you say yasher koach (יישר כוח)? How do you say todah rabbah (תודה רבה) in English?” He did not know the words “thank you.” They taught him.

He called the doctor in. “Thank you very much.” Those around him said: “Rebbe, you could have told us; we would have thanked him in English on your behalf. You could have spoken in lashon hakodesh (לשון הקודש) and we would have translated.”

Rav Elyashiv answered: “That is exactly why we say Modim DeRabbanan! One does not send another to give thanks. When you have to thank, even if it is in a different language, learn that language yourself. Do not send a shaliach tzibbur to thank on your behalf.”

The congregation joins, bows, and thanks the Holy One personally. Thanks given through an agent is not a true thank-you.

“Al She’anu Modim Lach” (על שאנו מודים לך)

The closing line has been understood from childhood as follows: we thank the Holy One that we have the ability to thank Him — for the gift of gratitude itself. One of the Rishonim (ראשונים), in a responsum (תשובה)[7], reads it differently: “al she’anu modim lach” means that in addition to what we thanked You for in the silent prayer (תפילת לחש), we now add these formulations. The two readings are not mutually exclusive.

IV. The Shaliach Tzibbur and the Silent Modim

A common error: many a shaliach tzibbur (שליח ציבור) recites Modim silently during the repetition (חזרת הש״ץ), quietly while the congregation (קהל) says Modim DeRabbanan, reasoning that no one can hear him anyway; he raises his voice only at the conclusion.

This is wrong. The shaliach tzibbur must say every word of the repetition aloud, Modim included, even though the congregation will not hear it because it is saying its own Modim DeRabbanan. That was the ordinance (תקנה). We do not strictly need the repetition today, since each person davens individually; but once it was incorporated, there is no deviating from it.

Some authorities (פוסקים) require ten persons in the vicinity of the shaliach tzibbur who hear every word[8] — practically difficult, for “shelosha kolos lo yishtamu” (שלשה קולות לא ישתמעו), three voices cannot be heard at once; yet that is the ruling. It is a mitzvah (מצוה) to set this right (מתקן): whoever would conduct the repetition as it should be conducted must say every word aloud, Modim included.

V. Nesias Kapayim (נשיאת כפים) — A Long Puzzle

The Practice

Why is it that we in chutz laaretz (חוץ לארץ) do not duchen (דוכן) daily? In Eretz Yisrael they do so every day. Among Sephardim (ספרדים) in chutz laaretz, every day. Even in an Ashkenazi (אשכנזי) shul in Eretz Yisrael with an entirely Ashkenazi minyan (מנין), they duchen daily. Only the Ashkenazim of chutz laaretz reserve the practice for Yom Tov (יום טוב). No posek (פוסק) offers a fully satisfying explanation.

The Mitzvah

Birkas Kohanim (ברכת כהנים) is one of the 613 mitzvos (תרי״ג מצוות)[9]. The Rambam (רמב״ם), the Chinuch (חינוך) and the Rishonim (ראשונים): the kohanim (כהנים) are to bless Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל) daily. In the formulation of the Gemara, a kohen (כהן) who does not ascend the platform transgresses three positive commandments, corresponding to the three expressions of the verse: “koh sevarchu” (כה תברכו), “amor lahem” (אמור להם), “v’samu es shemi al bnei Yisrael va’ani avarchem” (ושמו את שמי על בני ישראל ואני אברכם). The Chinuch describes the mitzvah as practiced “b’chol yom uvchol zman” (בכל יום ובכל זמן), every day and at all times.

How is it that we in chutz laaretz stopped fulfilling this?

Not at Minchah (מנחה)

There is one exception even in Eretz Yisrael: no duchening at Minchah. (At Neilah (נעילה) on Yom Kippur (יום כיפור) there is variation; some shuls do, some do not.)

The Rambam’s explanation[10]: Minchah follows the day’s principal meal. In those generations that meal was taken in the afternoon, and three drinks were standard at meals: milk, water, and wine. Wine was far more common at meals than it is today, and the Sages were wary of intoxication (שכרות) at Minchah. Nesias kapayim has the status of service (עבודה) — like the service in the Beis HaMikdash — and a kohen may not perform service while intoxicated. Hence not at Minchah. Shacharis (שחרית) is untroubled: who drinks wine before Shacharis?

Nesias Kapayim as Avodah (עבודה)

The Torah records the first blessing of Aharon (אהרן): “Vayisa Aharon es yadav el ha’am vayevarcheim vayered me’asos hachatas vehaolah vehashelamim” (וישא אהרן את ידיו אל העם ויברכם וירד מעשת החטאת והעולה והשלמים) — the blessing is given in the midst of the service. The Mishnah (משנה)[11] records nesias kapayim in the Beis HaMikdash as part of the daily order of the Tamid (תמיד): the kohanim came and stood on the steps of the Ulam (אולם) and blessed the people. The blessing is avodah; that is why the rule of intoxication applies, and it is also why the matter is taken so seriously.

VI. The Old Minhag (מנהג) of Chutz LaAretz

The Antiquity of the Custom

Why, then, do we not duchen in chutz laaretz? An ancient minhag. The poskim report testimony from as far back as the Maharam of Rothenburg (מהר״ם מרוטנבורג) — some seven hundred years ago in Europe — that they did not duchen except a few times a year, on Yom Tov only, and only at Musaf (מוסף). The poskim and the historians struggle to explain why. When did the practice change, and how? They do not know.

The Rema’s Reason

The Rema (רמ״א)[12] brings the well-known reason: in exile (גלות), Jews in chutz laaretz were so consumed with the difficulty of earning a livelihood (פרנסה) that they could not have proper kavanah; and Birkas Kohanim requires love and joy (אהבה ושמחה) — not merely the absence of intoxication but real love and joy. With no joy (שמחה) in exile, the practice lapsed. On Yom Tov there is joy, there is no work, the day is free; the kohanim can duchen.

And Shabbos?

The Rema holds that most Jews on Shabbos (שבת) are mentally occupied with the week’s business. (Halachah (הלכה) permits thinking about business on Shabbos: it is speaking of business that is forbidden, under “v’daber davar” (ודבר דבר); if one cannot restrain oneself and thinks about deals, that is permitted.) So even on Shabbos the repose of mind (מנוחת הנפש) needed for the blessing is not assumed; only Yom Tov, when everyone is in a different frame of mind, suffices.

The Difficulty With This Reason

This is a difficult reason. Are we to say that Jews in Eretz Yisrael today have an easier time with parnasah? They too worry about the next bill, the deal that is to close, the stocks. The Rema’s reason has the air of a justification after the fact for a minhag whose origin is genuinely obscure; but it is what we have. The Aruch HaShulchan (ערוך השלחן) himself states that there is no correct reason for our custom of annulling this mitzvah all year, and that it has been called a bad custom (מנהג גרוע).

The Witness of Eretz Yisrael and Egypt

The Beis Yosef (בית יוסף)[13] contrasts the practice of the people of Eretz Yisrael, and of Egypt — the Sephardim who lived there for some four or five hundred years and did duchen every day — and praises them for acting correctly; it is a loss that the people of chutz laaretz do not. And the Mishnah Berurah (משנה ברורה) is cited as saying that one who cannot do it with his hands should not do it with his mouth; hence Sephardim today do so daily.

The practical conclusion drawn by many poskim is that an Ashkenazi whose custom has been, for eight hundred years, not to duchen daily in chutz laaretz should not change it; even though it is a mitzvah to hear Birkas Kohanim, one need not alter the minhag of the place.

VII. The Failed Attempts to Restore

Several great men (גדולים) sought to overturn the minhag and reinstate daily duchening in chutz laaretz. The pattern is striking.

The Gra (גר״א)

The Gra in Vilna determined that enough was enough. He gathered his students (תלמידים): “Tomorrow we begin daily duchening. We are changing the ancient minhag.”

The next day the Gra was put in jail. The dispute between Chassidim (חסידים) and Misnagdim (מתנגדים) was raging, and an informer reported him to the non-Jewish authorities. The Gra took it as a sign from Heaven (סימן מן השמים). On his release, which came at once, he retracted: “Forget it. No daily duchening.”

Reb Chaim of Volozhin

The Gra’s great disciple, Reb Chaim of Volozhin, did not take the lesson. The Netziv (נצי״ב), Reb Chaim’s grandson, records that he heard from his father, Rabbi Yitzchak of Volozhin (רבי יצחק מוולוז׳ין), son of Reb Chaim:

After the Gra’s passing, Reb Chaim in Volozhin — a city in Belarus, where the yeshivah (ישיבה) building still stands as a museum — resolved to restore daily nesias kapayim. He told his students: “Tomorrow we begin a new minhag in the beis midrash (בית מדרש). We start duchening daily.” It is related that that night half the city of Volozhin burned down: the buildings were of wood, one house caught fire, then the next, then the next. Reb Chaim said: “I wanted to break an ancient minhag. I do not know why Hakadosh Baruch Hu did this; apparently the shefa (שפע) in chutz laaretz flows differently from that in Eretz Yisrael. We should not do it.” He retracted.

The Aruch HaShulchan (ערוך השלחן)[14], recording a tradition that two great men of earlier generations each sought to institute daily nesias kapayim and were frustrated, says: “It is as if a bas kol (בת קול) went out from Heaven” — not to allow us to lift our hands all year.

The Baal HaTanya (בעל התניא)

Some say the Baal HaTanya also tried. The Lubavitcher Rebbe gave a sichah (שיחה)[15] on this. Shulchan Aruch HaRav (שולחן ערוך הרב) records no such attempt; but the sichah says that the Alter Zeide (אלטע זיידע), the Rebbe the Zakein Baal HaTanya (הזקן בעל התניא), also wished to restore it — and something happened, there was some confusion, and he retracted.

Rav Nosson HaKohen Adler — The One Who Succeeded

One Jew succeeded. Rav Nosson HaKohen Adler (רבי נתן הכהן אדלר)[16] was the teacher of the Chasam Sofer (חתם סופר), who was born in Frankfurt and became gadol hador (גדול הדור) in Pressburg. Rav Nosson Adler was a gaon (גאון), a tzaddik (צדיק) and a mekubal (מקובל) who did unusual things in Frankfurt — a יעקישע שטאט (a “Yekkish” German city) — among them changing the nusach (נוסח) from Nusach Ashkenaz (נוסח אשכנז), which was unheard of there. The rabbanim (רבנים) drove him from the city, and the Chasam Sofer left with him while still young and never returned to Frankfurt; he went on to Pressburg.

Rav Nosson Adler was a kohen. In his beis midrash he instituted daily nesias kapayim. He was the only one who succeeded; he always recited Birkas Kohanim, until he was forced from the city. No one else succeeded.

VIII. Chezkas Kehunah (חזקת כהונה) — Lost in Galus (גלות)

The Argument

Some poskim trace the minhag to a halachic concern: kohanim in chutz laaretz have lost their chezkas kehunah. The Sheilas Margolios (שאלת מרגליות)[17] is said to have an entire responsum on it. The point is not about any individual’s father: a given kohen’s father was a kohen, and his father before him; he knows it and believes it. The question is structural, across generations of exile.

The Story of the Kohen From Pinsk

A well-known story[18] tells of a man who emigrated from Pinsk in Belarus to America in the early 1900s — the Goldene Medina (the “golden land”). He came to a new congregation (קהילה) looking for a kohen. In the new world, he decided to pass himself off as a kohen: back in the shtetl (שטעטל) he had been a simple person sitting at the back of the shul, but here he could be a kohen. He bought a new suit, polished his shoes, and walked into a shul where no one knew him.

They needed a kohen for the first aliyah (עליה). “איך בין הכהן” (“I am the kohen!”), he called out. He received the aliyah and felt important. After davening he went to greet the rav (רב). The rav looked at him: “Shmerel! I know you. You are from Pinsk. I am from Pinsk too. Shmerel, I don’t understand: your father was not a kohen — I knew him. Your grandfather was not a kohen. How are you suddenly a kohen?”

Shmerel answered: “Rabbi, this is a new country. If you can be a rav, I can be a kohen.”

The story is humorous, and the point beneath it is real: it illustrates the loosening of chezkas kehunah, but it is not itself the halachic argument, which is structural and does not rest on cases of individual error or fraud. Rav Frank and Rabbi Efraim Zalman Margolios[19] are cited as holding that kohanim across galus have lost their chezkas kehunah. Many families have a yichus letter (יחוס בריף) at home — a documented family record; not all do. A family in Lakewood, until the mid-2000s, performed duchening and pidyon haben (פדיון הבן); on a family trip to the ancestral graves (קברי אבות) abroad they discovered, from the gravestones (מצבות) of their elders, that they were not kohanim at all. Those for whom they had performed pidyon haben had to find another kohen, so that the earlier ceremonies were thought to require repeating — a conclusion that is itself open to discussion.

Why Eretz Yisrael Differs

Why do the Sephardim of Eretz Yisrael duchen? The historical fact: the kohanim there never left the land continuously since the churban (חורבן), and therefore never lost their chezkah (חזקה). They were never scattered through exile as the kohanim of Europe were. The reasoning is admittedly hard to follow, and the tension was observed that a kohen is relied upon for pidyon haben while his presumption is held too weak for duchening.

DNA and Kehunah

Today there are DNA tests, and contemporary responsa (תשובות) on the subject. There is an island in Tunisia, Djerba, said to be known as the city of the kohanim (עיר הכהנים); as reported, DNA testing there indicated common descent from the seed of Aharon HaKohen (זרע אהרן הכהן)[20]. Whether to rely on DNA in halachah is its own question.

The first major halachic case on DNA arose after September 11, 2001. A special beis din (בית דין) was set up for the agunos (עגונות); many Jews had died, remains were processed for years, and the question was whether DNA matches sufficed for a heter (היתר). Three poskim are reported on that beis din: Rav Shmuel Fuerst of Chicago, Rav Avrohom Felder (rosh yeshivah (ראש ישיבה) in South Fallsburg), and Rav Eliyahu Levine of Lakewood (an expert (מומחה) in contemporary technical questions). They are said to have written an unpublished responsum of some four hundred pages, whose conclusion was not to rely on DNA testing. By extension: not relied upon for kehunah either.

The Documented Families

The Baal Shem Tov (בעל שם טוב)[21] is said to have declared that there are three families with established lineage (יחוס) as kohanim: Rapoport, Katz, and one other (some say Margolios, though not all Margolios are kohanim). The blessing (ברכה) of the Baal Shem Tov on these families is taken as substantiation.

IX. Regional Custom in Eretz Yisrael — The Galil (גליל)

One more layer. Even within Eretz Yisrael, Ashkenazim do not everywhere duchen daily. Sephardim do so everywhere. But Ashkenazim in the Galil — Haifa, Tiberias, Tzfas — duchen only on Shabbos and Yom Tov, not during the week. One may walk into an Ashkenazi shul in Tzfas, Tiberias, Haifa, all the way down to Hadera, and they do not duchen on weekdays. (At Meron they duchen daily, because so many come there from Yerushalayim; the custom of Yerushalayim governs.)

The Historical Explanation

The Galil was thinly populated for stretches of history. Tzfas was a major city in the time of the Beis Yosef (בית יוסף) and the Arizal (אריז״ל); then a famous earthquake in the eighteenth century emptied much of Tzfas, and the survivors went to Yerushalayim. Who came to resettle it? European Jews: the disciples of the Baal Shem Tov — Reb Mendel of Vitebsk among the first — and the disciples of the Gra (תלמידי הגר״א). They brought with them their chutz laaretz custom of not duchening daily, and chose not to reinstitute it. The compromise: on Shabbos and Yom Tov they duchen; on weekdays they do not.

So even today, large communities in the Galil do not duchen during the week. The disciples of the Gra sought to duchen daily like their master; the Chassidim chose not to. To this day some rabbis regard the chutz laaretz custom in the Galil as a mistaken custom (מנהג טעות) and try to reinstitute daily duchening; it has not taken hold.

Kfar Hasidim — One Exception

One place — the yeshivah at Kfar Hasidim, today called Rechasim — does duchen daily. It was instituted there by Rav Noach Shimonowitz[22], whose Chiddushei Rav Noach (חידושי רב נח) is a major work. The rabbis of the north still debate the underlying dispute (מחלוקת).

Rav Kook’s Stand

There are letters of Rav Kook (רב קוק)[23] on this. On one occasion he travelled from Yerushalayim to Haifa. He himself duchened daily; he was a kohen. In Haifa they told him: “Rav, here we don’t do that.” Rav Kook stood up: “I don’t care, I am duchening.” He blessed the congregation; nothing happened. But he duchened only while he was there; once he left, no one continued the practice.

X. Closing

[Editorial coda — not part of the delivered shiur.] From Modim, where the Chofetz Chaim stood pausing because he could not have enough of thanking the Holy One, and where Rav Elyashiv at one hundred and two learned to say “thank you” in English in his own voice, through to nesias kapayim, where attempt after attempt to restore the mitzvah in chutz laaretz was met with prison cells and city fires, the underlying theme is the same: tefillah moves through structures we did not invent and cannot dismantle, and through mitzvos we did not impose and cannot easily restore. We work within the structure as it has come down to us, with the pauses of the Chofetz Chaim and the English “thank you” of Rav Elyashiv as our models. B’ezras Hashem (בעזרת השם), may Mashiach bring us to a world in which the kohanim bless Klal Yisrael daily again. Nor is it a contradiction that, when every other prayer has fallen away, thanksgiving alone remains: Birkas Kohanim is avodah, as has been seen, and not tefillah in the ordinary sense; and the Modim that remains is the Modim of a Jew who thanks in his own voice.
