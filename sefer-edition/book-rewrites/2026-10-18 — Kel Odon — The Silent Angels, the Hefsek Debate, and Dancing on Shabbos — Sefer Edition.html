אֵל אָדוֹן — הַמַּלְאָכִים הַשּׁוֹתְקִים, מַחֲלֹקֶת הַהֶפְסֵק וְהָרִקּוּד בְּשַׁבָּת

Kel Odon: The Silent Angels, the Hefsek Debate, and Dancing on Shabbos
Studies in the Shabbos Tefillos (תפילות) — The Piyutim of Yotzer Or
7 Cheshvan 5787  ·  Sefer Edition

This discourse was delivered לעילוי נשמת the mother of one member of the congregation and the father of another, each of whom sponsored the morning. [Dedication wording and names to be confirmed with the Rav before printing; they are held with the source material and are not reproduced here.]

On Shabbos morning the first blessing before Keriyas Shema (קריאת שמע) grows from a few lines to a long poem. This discourse asks why, and answers in three stages. First, a tradition in the Tosafos (תוספות) gives the angels six wings, one song for each weekday, and none for Shabbos, so that the song of the day falls to Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל); on that reading הכל יודוך recalls the first Shabbos of Creation, when the angels, created on Monday, still had a wing to spare. Second, it sets out the sharp dispute among the Geonim and Rishonim over whether these additions may be said at all: Rav Saadia Gaon (רב סעדיה גאון) and the Rambam (הרמב״ם) do not include them, the Tur reports that Toledo did not say לאל אשר שבת, and Rabbeinu Yehuda ben Barzilai calls them an interruption, while Rashi (רש״י) holds that the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah themselves enacted them; and it follows the Or Zarua and the Magen Avraham to the practical question of one who missed them. Third, it turns from the legitimacy of the piyutim to their joy: the closing words of לאל אשר שבת as a cure for a verse that ends badly, the Zohar (זוהר)’s picture of the angels, Shabbos itself and Adam HaRishon singing and dancing together, the old quarrel between Chassidim and Misnagdim over dancing on Shabbos, and the niggun that Reb Moshe Wolfson introduced for הכל יודוך.

I. The Question: Why Is the Shabbos Yotzer Longer?

The discourse follows directly on the previous week’s inquiry into Nishmas (נשמת כל חי), which had ranged from the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah to Shimon ben Shetach, from the Rokeach’s claim that it was composed before Rabbeinu HaKadosh redacted the Mishnah, to the legend that it was written by Shimon Kefa, Peter.[1] The lecturer adds one point before moving on. On Shabbos tefillin are not worn, and Rav Chaim Vital is reported to say that reciting Nishmas is, as it were, putting on tefillin; the lecturer says he does not know where this is stated, and remarks that there are great secrets (סודות) in Nishmas.[2] As was said the week before, the practical rule is that Nishmas must be said, and one who missed it must say it.

After Nishmas and Yishtabach (ישתבח) come Barechu (ברכו) and the first blessing of Shema, Yotzer Or (יוצר אור ובורא חושך). On a weekday the blessing continues המאיר לארץ ולדרים עליה and then adds a short alphabetical poem in which each letter receives a single word. This is the poem by which the alphabet is learned:

אֵל בָּרוּךְ גְּדוֹל דֵּעָה, הֵכִין וּפָעַל …
The blessed God, great in knowledge, prepared and made …

On Shabbos the poem is far longer and describes the greatness of Hashem at length. Each letter of the alphabet receives a whole sentence, twenty-two lines in all:

אֵל אָדוֹן עַל כָּל הַמַּעֲשִׂים, בָּרוּךְ וּמְבֹרָךְ בְּפִי כָּל נְשָׁמָה …
God, Master of all works, blessed and blessed again by the mouth of every soul …

A Note on the Singing

The lecturer reports a recent complaint about the way the poem is sung in his congregation. In every synagogue in which he had prayed since childhood, the chazzan sang a line, the congregation repeated it, and so on, line by line. In this congregation, the chazzan and the congregation sing it together, without pausing. The saving of time was named as the reason, perhaps thirteen or fifteen seconds. Members of the congregation observed that the practice is common, including in many synagogues, and that it is the Sefardi custom. The lecturer’s own view is that there is no halachic issue and that either practice is acceptable. A member of the congregation proposed that the real reason is the women, who prefer the poem sung straight through; the lecturer took up the suggestion with evident pleasure, adding a joke about the number of women who are in the women’s section at that hour.

II. The Six Wings and the Silent Angels

The discourse now puts the larger question. The Shabbos Pesukei DeZimra is longer than the weekday one, and the Shabbos blessings of Shema are expanded; why? The lecturer says that he had raised this when the series studied Pesukei DeZimra some months earlier, and that the answer is one that every Jew should know.

The answer is given in the Tosafos on Sanhedrin (סנהדרין) 37b.[3] On each day of the week the angels sing shirah (שירה). The lecturer, saying that he does not know what this means, connects it with the verse that gives the angels six wings, שש כנפים לאחד: each wing sings on one day of the week, the first on Sunday, the second on Monday, and so on to Friday. On Shabbos no wing is left. At that point, as the lecturer reports the Tosafos, Hakadosh Baruch Hu tells the angels that they have sung throughout the six days of the week, silences them, and says that He wishes to hear the song of His children. The printed Tosafos, as far as it could be checked, says only that each wing sings one song a day on the six weekdays and that when Shabbos arrives the creatures (חיות) speak before Hamakom; the silencing of the angels is the lecturer’s report. On Shabbos, therefore, Klal Yisrael sings. This is why Pesukei DeZimra are lengthened, why the blessings of Shema are expanded, and why on Shabbos the congregation praises Hakadosh Baruch Hu at length: the angels are silent that day, and the song is ours.[4]

III. הכל יודוך and the First Shabbos

The Shabbos blessing of Yotzer Or opens with a verse of universal praise:

הַכֹּל יוֹדוּךָ, וְהַכֹּל יְשַׁבְּחוּךָ, וְהַכֹּל יֹאמְרוּ אֵין קָדוֹשׁ כַּה׳.
All shall thank You, and all shall praise You, and all shall say: there is none holy like Hashem.

The word הכל, “all,” is repeated several times in this section, by the lecturer’s count six, from ברוך אתה ה׳ יוצר to בורא את הכל. He says that he does not know the source of the explanation that follows, which “the world” gives, and that it depends on the answer to a question: when were the angels created?

A member of the congregation answers that it was on the second day, Monday. The lecturer confirms that he has said so before, in the study of the angels, and that Rashi implies it in Bereishis. The matter, however, is the subject of a major dispute in the Midrash, between Rabbi Chanina (רבי חנינא) and Rabbi Yochanan (רבי יוחנן): some say the second day of Creation, some say a later day (the Midrash as retrieved has the fifth), and some say that the angels were created before the world, being spiritual beings who precede the physical world.[5] Most follow the opinion that they were created on Monday. A proof is brought from Tehillim (תהלים) 104, ברכי נפשי: the verse המקרה במים עליותיו is followed at once by עושה מלאכיו רוחות משרתיו אש לוהט, which suggests that the angels were made on the day that follows.

From this the lecturer draws a calculation. In the first week of the world the angels, created on Monday, sang on Monday with the first wing, and so on through Friday, five wings in all. On the first Shabbos they had a wing left over. On that first Shabbos, he says, Klal Yisrael sang together with them, והכל ישבחוך. [“Klal Yisrael” is the lecturer’s phrase; in the account of the Zohar below it is Adam HaRishon who joins the angels and Shabbos. See Open Question 4.] The opening of the blessing, הכל יודוך, is therefore said in commemoration of that extra unison, the singing and praising of Hakadosh Baruch Hu together on the first Shabbos. From the second week onward the angels use all their wings between Sunday and Friday, and on every Shabbos the song belongs to Klal Yisrael alone; hence the length of the praises of Shabbos Kodesh.

A member of the congregation asked about Michael and Gavriel, with a reference to the Gemara in Berachos. The lecturer answered that the verse of the six wings is the general principle, and called the question a good one.[6]

IV. May These Piyutim Be Said? The Dispute of the Geonim and Rishonim

The lecturer now turns to a fact that the tradition of the long Shabbos Yotzer tends to hide. The additions to Yotzer Or, הכל יודוך, אל אדון על כל המעשים and לאל אשר שבת מכל המעשים, were not said by all.

Rav Saadia Gaon and the Rambam

Rav Saadia Gaon, in his siddur (סידור), a very ancient one, says that no addition is made on Shabbos morning: not הכל יודוך, not אל אדון, not לאל אשר שבת. In the lecturer’s words there is no source for them in Chazal, in either Talmud.[7] The Rambam, at the end of the Laws of Prayer in the Book of Ahavah of his Yad HaChazakah (יד החזקה), sets out the order of the prayers, and for Shabbos morning he gives Nishmas, then Yishtabach, then the ordinary blessing יוצר אור ובורא חושך, without any of the additions.[8] In their world, the lecturer observes, the weekday text was said, and the congregation saved several minutes every Shabbos morning. He asks what happened to the Ashkenazic communities, and asks the Sefardim in the room what they say; a member of the congregation answered that all the Sefardim say the additions, the Syrians among them. A short exchange about אל אדון on Yom Tov was not clear enough on the recording to be reported.

Toledo and the Tur

The Tur, Rabbeinu Yaakov ben Asher, was the son of the Rosh. The Rosh had been in Ashkenaz and, in unusual circumstances which the lecturer says he has described on another occasion, moved to Spain, and his son moved with him, so that the Tur knew both traditions. In the Laws of Shabbos, Orach Chaim 281, where he treats the text of Shabbos morning, the Tur states that in Toledo they did not say the addition. The lecturer reports it as covering both אל אדון and לאל אשר שבת and wonders about Morocco.[9]

Rabbeinu Yehuda ben Barzilai

One of the Rishonim goes further than omitting them. Rabbeinu Yehuda ben Barzilai of Barcelona, whom the lecturer places in the late eleventh century as a contemporary of the Rif, writes in his Sefer HaIttim (ספר העתים) that saying them is a mistake. The blessings of Keriyas Shema are a matbea shetavu chachamim (מטבע שטבעו חכמים), a fixed formula, written by the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah, and one may not interrupt it. These additions were added later; those who say them make an hefsek (הפסק), and the lecturer says the Rishon calls it an act of audacity. He writes in very sharp language of those places that were accustomed to add piyutim to Yotzer Or.[10] A member of the congregation observed that the Ashkenazim say many piyutim throughout the prayers; the lecturer agreed, and added that they are glad not to say them all, because the prayers would be much longer. He agreed that the Poskim discuss how it is permitted at all: the Beis Yosef brings views, and the question is how to add something that is not in the Talmud Bavli and was not instituted by the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah.

Rashi in the Sefer HaPardes

On the other side stands Rashi. In his Sefer HaPardes (ספר הפרדס), the lecturer reports, a collection of many laws and customs, Rashi writes that the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah did enact these additions. Ashkenaz, in other words, had a tradition that differed from that of Sefard, according to which they belong here, and for that reason they are no interruption and ought to be said.[11]

Who May Insert?

The lecturer asks the congregation whether they understand that things like ה׳ שפתי תפתח, placed before the Shemoneh Esrei, do not make an interruption, because they belong to the same subject. Only the Gemara, he says, has the right to say so; ה׳ שפתי תפתח is part of the Shemoneh Esrei and Hashkiveinu is part of the blessing of redemption.[12] It does not follow that any person may write a poem in praise of Hakadosh Baruch Hu and insert it. Here there is a real dispute: either it is forbidden, or it is the best thing in the world and ought to be done. Klal Yisrael loved the piyutim, and the lecturer is sure that the practice is correct; but it should be known that in many synagogues and study halls of the Rishonim they were not said, and that some thought it wrong to say them.

Why Not Elsewhere?

One may ask why the piyutim were not placed before the blessing, or after Yishtabach. The lecturer answers that this too would be an interruption, here between Yishtabach and Yotzer Or. He recalls the announcement made before battle, when the kohen (כהן) said that those with sins should go home, and says that one who interrupts at this point is treated as one who returns from the lines of battle.[13] If they are to be said after the prayers, they should be said after the prayers; but Klal Yisrael adopted the custom of Ashkenaz, and Rashi is holy to us. A congregant raised another addition, שיר המעלות, which the lecturer called a very big question and which was not further explained on the recording.

V. If One Missed Them: The Or Zarua and the Magen Avraham

[As the lecturer reports these two authorities; neither text was located, and the Mishnah Berurah limits the rule to לאל אשר שבת (note 14).]

The lecturer uses the dispute to show how seriously a disagreement among the Rishonim is taken in practice. The Magen Avraham (מגן אברהם) cites the Or Zarua (אור זרוע), one of the great Rishonim, frequently quoted by the Beis Yosef (בית יוסף). The Or Zarua writes that one who did not say לאל אשר שבת and אל אדון on Shabbos morning, having come late, for example, to a prayer in which everyone had already said them, צריך לחזור, must return and say Yotzer Or again.[14] No one, the lecturer notes, is ever seen to do this.

The Magen Avraham finds this impossible. A rule that came after the text of Yotzer Or was composed, whether by the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah or others, cannot invalidate the blessing if it is omitted. The Or Zarua must therefore mean that the worshipper has skipped the additional Shabbos piyutim, not that his blessing is defective. He continues with the congregation, says Shema and prays with them; but after the Shemoneh Esrei he should open the siddur and make up what he omitted. The lecturer remarks that one does not make up Ashrei, or Yishtabach, which must be said, while these piyutim, by this rule, must be. As reported by the lecturer, the Magen Avraham is thus innovating a rule on the basis of the Or Zarua: the piyutim are so much a part of the prayer that, if missed, they must be said, and he adds that the Acharonim agree.

A member of the congregation asked whether one should then skip the piyut deliberately in order to pray the Shemoneh Esrei with the congregation. The lecturer replied that on the Magen Avraham’s reading, yes, since the Or Zarua does not mean that the blessing is defective, only that the piyutim must be said at some time on Shabbos. But no one does this: no one skips אל אדון in order to pray with the congregation, and no one returns to it afterwards. He told the room that he was teaching a halachah that none of them had learned and that he himself had not known until now.

VI. Ending on a Davar Tov: לאשמה בה

The lecturer has described the piyutim as the subject of dispute. He now turns to the closing words of לאל אשר שבת, which solve a problem in the Torah reading.

לָאֵל אֲשֶׁר שָׁבַת מִכָּל הַמַּעֲשִׂים, בַּיּוֹם הַשְּׁבִיעִי נִתְעַלָּה וְיָשַׁב עַל כִּסֵּא כְבוֹדוֹ.
To the God who rested from all works; on the seventh day He ascended and sat upon His throne of glory.

There is a rule in the Shulchan Aruch that every aliyah, and every extra aliyah (hosafah, הוספה), should end on a good matter, a davar tov (דבר טוב).[15] The lecturer jokes with the congregation about the practice of adding aliyos, which in the heimishe shtiebel can reach fifteen extra, among the Sefardim in some places, and almost nowhere in the Ashkenazic synagogues of the strict kind. An aliyah that ends on a negative note is not good, and congregants are sometimes insulted when they notice where the reader ended theirs.

The lecturer asks for a Chumash and reads the end of Parashas Vayikra: וְכִפֶּר עָלָיו הַכֹּהֵן לִפְנֵי ה׳ וְנִסְלַח לוֹ עַל אַחַת מִכֹּל אֲשֶׁר יַעֲשֶׂה לְאַשְׁמָה בָהּ. It ends with guilt. The Maor VaShemesh (מאור ושמש) of Rav Kalonymus Kalman Epstein of Krakow, which the lecturer calls the Shulchan Aruch of Chassidus, says that this is not a way to end, and records a custom that no one now keeps, apart perhaps from Spinka: after the reader says לאשמה בה, the whole congregation says לאל אשר שבת מכל המעשים ביום השביעי. The first letters of those words are the letters of לאשמה בה. The lecturer says that the Maor VaShemesh brings it from the Apter Rav, and that the words are a beautiful and positive praise of the Creator who stopped the work of the six days and sits upon His throne.[16] Someone in the room suggested that the verse contains the Name before its last words and that the reader might raise his voice; the lecturer was not aware of such a custom, and held that ending on לאשמה בה is not good.

The lecturer gives a second example, from the second day of Rosh Hashanah, when the reading ends with וְאֶת תַּחַשׁ וְאֶת מַעֲכָה, names without any evident connection to the day. The Imrei Noam (אמרי נועם), a Chassidic work, reads תחש as the initials of תקעו בחודש שופר and מעכה as the initials of מלוך על כל העולם.[17] The lecturer adds a plea for Chassidic books: it is worth looking at them, because they give a spark of enthusiasm in the service of Hashem, and one need not remain dry and cold. A congregant raised a Gemara in Megillah that, as he put it, learns something from the seven eunuchs; the lecturer postponed it to the study of the Torah reading. The Gemara in question (Megillah 23a) derives the seven readers of Shabbos from the seven who saw the king’s face (Esther 1:14), not from eunuchs.[18]

VII. From Legitimacy to Joy: The Zohar’s Picture of the Angels

The discourse so far has asked whether one may say these piyutim. It now asks what one is doing when one says them, and the two questions are one. The angels fall silent on Shabbos and the song passes to Klal Yisrael; and yet the Zohar, as the lecturer relates it, has the angels singing and dancing on Shabbos too. These are two pictures of one day, and the lecturer does not reconcile them in so many words. On the first he builds the argument that the length of the Shabbos prayer is ours. On the second, in the passage that follows, he describes the moment at which the song of the angels and the song of Israel join.

“Gentlemen, just to give you an idea of what we say by לאל אשר שבת מכל המעשים: it is important for a Jew to know,” the lecturer says, what the Zohar HaKadosh describes.[19] Whenever Hakadosh Baruch Hu created the malachim (מלאכים), on whichever day it was, He did not yet give them their tasks. He gave them their missions on Shabbos. On Shabbos, in the Zohar’s words as the lecturer gives them, כינס את כל מלאכי השרת לפניו, He gathered all the ministering angels before Him and told them who would do what: Michael this, Gavriel that, Raphael another. A member of the congregation objected that assigning work is a weekday matter; the lecturer replied that he did not understand the Zohar fully, but that this much was certain. With their tasks given, all the angels were glad that they could now function as emissaries, and they began, in the Zohar’s language, to sing and dance before Hakadosh Baruch Hu where He sits on His throne. The lecturer adds that he does not know what this means, since angels are spiritual.

The whole week He had not sat down, being engaged, so to speak, in the work of Creation. Now, ביום השביעי נתעלה וישב על כסא כבודו: the world is complete, Adam HaRishon and Chava stand in it, and the angels have their tasks.

Then Shabbos itself sings. The lecturer says he does not know what kind of being Shabbos is, but the Zohar speaks of one: Shabbos was so moved that on its own day every angel does the will of Hashem, and none waits idle for assignment, that Shabbos began to sing before Hakadosh Baruch Hu, מזמור שיר ליום השבת. Adam HaRishon, the Zohar says, heard Shabbos sing. By then he had done teshuvah (תשובה) for the sin of the Tree of Knowledge, which the lecturer understands the Gemara in Avodah Zarah (עבודה זרה) to teach, and so he joined Shabbos and the angels, “and there was song and dance. It was like a chasunah (חתונה), it was Simchas Torah, it was Purim up in heaven.”[20]

That, in the lecturer’s account, is why on every Shabbos we say מזמור שיר ליום השבת, אל אדון על כל המעשים and לאל אשר שבת מכל המעשים, and why we sing them: that is the manner a Jew should be in on Shabbos, with the whole creation, Shabbos included, in song. When the text says ויום השביעי משבח ואומר: מזמור שיר ליום השבת, we answer, לפיכך יפארו ויברכו לאל כל יצוריו. Only when Shabbos itself opens its mouth, so to speak, do Jews have the strength to join Shabbos and the angels and sing before Hakadosh Baruch Hu.

Two Pictures, One Shabbos

In the Tosafos, the angels are silent and the song is Israel’s. In the Zohar, the angels are sent to their tasks, and then angels, Shabbos and Adam HaRishon sing together. The lecturer does not resolve this in so many words. It is possible to read the first as the reason for the length of the Shabbos prayer, and the second as its content: Israel fills the hour left empty by the angels, and in doing so enters the chorus that the angels, by their Shabbos tasks, are free to join. This reading is the editor’s, offered as a bridge, and is recorded again among the Open Questions.

VIII. Singing, Clapping, and Dancing on Shabbos: The Talner Rebbe’s Tish

The lecturer says that in the Chassidic synagogues in which he has prayed, when they say אל אדון not only do they clap; he has seen people rise and dance, and he suggests that we ought to do the same. This was the first Shabbos of his life, he says, that he went to a bris (ברית) and a Kiddush at a Chassidic shul in the neighbourhood; the singing and dancing there astonished him. “It is beautiful,” he says. “It is a fine thing to sing and dance on Shabbos Kodesh.”

He then cautions that the halachah is not so simple. In the Shulchan Aruch, it is forbidden to clap, slap the thigh or dance on Shabbos.[21] He says that he claps, and that some others do. This was one of the chief disputes between the Chassidim and the Misnagdim at the beginning of the second and third generations of the students of the Baal Shem Tov: singing and dancing on Shabbos, which the Misnagdim saw as contrary to halachah. The Noda BiYehuda, he says, came out against it; he was very upset.

A Grandson at a Tish

The lecturer tells of something he saw once, which he calls a secret. A grandson of the Noda BiYehuda, a man named Rav Elazar Halevi Landa, whom he describes as a great Torah scholar, writes in the introduction to his book that he was a rav in a Lithuanian town. The Talner Rebbe, one of the great Chernobyl dynasty, about two hundred years ago, came to the town, and a tish (טיש) was arranged. The Lithuanian rav, to whom it was not a matter of honour, came to the tish on Friday night. In the middle of it the Rebbe rose and began to dance, and the Chassidim, overjoyed, climbed onto the table, on the kugel and the herring, the lecturer says.[22]

As the lecturer paraphrases the introduction, the rav writes that he had never seen such a thing in his life, that his grandfather would have told him to leave, but that he enjoyed it so much that he felt Shabbos as he never had before. “So you see,” the lecturer says, “the Misnagdim came to terms with the Chassidim and their dancing and singing.” He adds that today we are comfortable with Chassidus, but that it began then. The words are the lecturer’s English rendering of the passage as he remembers it, not a quotation of the printed text.

He wonders why Rebbes do not come to his synagogue, and proposes to invite some and hold a tish on Friday night, to bring the congregation into it. A Skverer Rebbe, he recalls, was once there, long ago. The point of the account is, he repeats, that the Rishonim, quoting the Zohar HaKadosh, say that everyone sang: the angels, Adam HaRishon and Shabbos, all dancing in unison. “I think that is a very beautiful image to have when we say this.”

IX. Singing הכל יודוך: Reb Moshe Wolfson and the Rokeach

The lecturer closes with a point raised at the beginning of the morning by a member of the congregation: in some places not only is אל אדון sung, but הכל יודוך והכל ישבחוך is sung aloud as well.

The lecturer had the privilege, when he was the rabbi in Miami, of having Rav Moshe Wolfson of Emunas Yisrael, whom he mentioned the week before, come for a whole winter, for months, and pray in his shul every day and every Shabbos morning. The local congregants were unhappy, since it made the prayers much longer, but Rav Wolfson introduced that when they came to הכל יודוך, the shaliach tzibbur (שליח ציבור) would sing it, from הפותח בכל יום דלתות שערי מזרח ובוקע חלוני רקיע, in a long niggun, the cantor and the congregation together, back and forth.[23] He wondered, he says, where that custom comes from.

The Rokeach, he says, writes that this part of the prayers is said בנעימה ובשירה ובזמרה, with melody, song and music. For some reason Klal Yisrael does not do so, though certain Chassidim do.[24] The lecturer anticipates the objection that a certain large congregation sings everything; a member of the congregation replied that they do sing it there, as an introduction, and that they add אין ערוך לך ואין זולתך, אפס בלתך. The lecturer joked about how to bring such a person back for Shabbos morning, and concluded: “It is all right, let them sing it.” He wished the congregation a wonderful week.

The Course of the Discourse

The discourse begins with a question about the length of the Shabbos prayer, answers it with the silence of the angels, finds that the answer does not make the piyutim lawful, follows the dispute over their lawfulness down to the practical question of a missed piyut, and then leaves legitimacy for joy. It ends, as the closing sections of לאל אשר שבת teach, on a good matter: the angels, Shabbos and Adam HaRishon dancing together, and a congregation invited to join them.

The lecturer reports that, on Shabbos, when tefillin are not worn, Rav Chaim Vital says that saying Nishmas is as if one puts on tefillin, and that there are great secrets in it.   3. תוספות סנהדרין ל״ז ע״ב ד״ה מכנף הארץ זמירות שמענו; ישעיהו ו׳:ב׳. The lecturer cites the Tosafos in Sanhedrin 37b for the idea that each wing sings on one day of the week. On Sefaria (Vilna edition) the comment beginning מכנף הארץ זמירות שמענו reads, in the part retrieved, וכל כנף הוא אומר שירה אחת ביום בששת ימי החול (each wing says one song a day on the six weekdays), and then וכשיגיע שבת אומרים החיות לפני המקום (and when Shabbos arrives the creatures say before Hamakom …). Corrected: the lecturer says the six-wing verse is in Yechezkel; Yechezkel 1:6 gives each creature four wings, and the verse with six wings is Yeshayahu 6:2 (שש כנפים שש כנפים לאחד). The Tosafos speaks of the living creatures (חיות), not of angels generally. The lecturer’s further details, that Hakadosh Baruch Hu tells the angels they are finished and says that He wishes to hear His children, were not found in the part retrieved, and stand as his report of the Tosafos (uncertain). ↩
  4. חולין צ״א ע״ב (cross-reference, not cited by the lecturer). Verified on Sefaria. Its order (Israel first) differs from the lecturer’s picture of Shabbos, in which the angels fall silent; the two are not equated here. ↩
  5. בראשית רבה א׳:ג׳; תהלים ק״ד:ג׳–ד׳. On Sefaria, Bereishis Rabbah 1:3 has R. Corrected: the lecturer says “the fourth day”; the Midrash as retrieved gives the fifth. “Before the world was created” was not located in this passage. The lecturer’s “Rashi implies it in Bereishis” was not identified: Rashi on Bereishis 1:1 and 1:7 does not state the angels’ day (uncertain). ↩
  6. ברכות ד׳ ע״ב — Michael in one flight, Gavriel in two. That the question refers to this passage is inferred; the recording does not preserve the question itself. ↩
  7. Siddur of Rav Saadia Gaon: no Shabbos additions in the morning.   8. רמב״ם, נוסח התפילה (end of Sefer Ahavah) — Shabbos morning. Narrowed: the sentence located names only לאל אשר שבת for Toledo; the lecturer’s inclusion of אל אדון goes beyond it (uncertain). The lecturer’s contrast is between insertions the Gemara itself makes and a piyut composed by “me and you.” He named no daf. ה׳ שפתי תפתח is discussed in Berachos 4b and 9b (checked in the 2025-10-19 log entry).   13. שולחן ערוך אורח חיים נ״ד:ג׳ — between Yishtabach and Yotzer. On Sefaria, Shulchan Aruch Orach Chayim 54:3 states that speaking between Yishtabach and Yotzer is a sin, and that the speaker is among those who return from the battle lines. The siman is the editor’s identification (the lecturer said “the Gemara”). The kohen’s announcement is in Devarim 20:8; the reading of it as a man with sins is the lecturer’s. ↩
  14. אור זרוע; מגן אברהם; שו״ע אורח חיים רפ״א:א׳; משנה ברורה רפ״א:ג׳. The Or Zarua and the Magen Avraham themselves were not located; the lecturer’s account of them stands as his report (uncertain). On Sefaria, the Rema on Shulchan Aruch Orach Chayim 281:1 says that nothing is repeated for an omission, מלבד אם לא אמר לאל אשר שבת וכו׳ מחזירין אותו. The Mishnah Berurah (281:3) explains מחזירין אותו as not a return to the blessing but saying it after the Shemoneh Esrei without a blessing, and limits this to לאל אשר שבת: for the other zemiros there is no obligation to say them afterwards. Two points differ from the lecturer: the make-up obligation is limited to לאל אשר שבת, not אל אדון or the others; and the Chayei Adam (cited in the Mishnah Berurah) rules against skipping the piyut in order to pray with the congregation, which agrees with what everyone does and not with the lecturer’s “on the Magen Avraham, yes.” ↩
  15. שולחן ערוך אורח חיים קל״ח:א׳ (Rema) — to begin and end on a good matter. The siman is the editor’s; the lecturer gave none. That additional aliyos are made in some communities and not others is the lecturer’s report of practice. ↩
