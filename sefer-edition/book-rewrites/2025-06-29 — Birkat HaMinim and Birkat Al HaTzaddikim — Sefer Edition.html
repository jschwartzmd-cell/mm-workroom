בִּרְכַּת הַמִּינִים וּבִרְכַּת עַל הַצַּדִּיקִים

Birkas HaMinim and Birkas Al HaTzaddikim — The Nineteenth Berachah and the Six Categories of Tzaddikim
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Sunday Morning Series on Tefillah
Recorded כ"ג סיון תשפ"ה  ·  29 June 2025  ·  Sefer Edition

Continuing the exposition of ברכת המינים: the Rambam’s historical framing, the identity of the minim, the long shadow of Christian censorship, the six verbs of destruction, and the tanna who left Klal Yisrael as his only children. Then ברכת על הצדיקים and its six categories, closing with the Arizal’s kavvanah on “v’sim chelkenu imahem.”

I. The Rambam’s Framing — “Bimei Rabban Gamliel”

The Rambam, in Hilchos Tefillah 2:1 (רמב"ם, הלכות תפילה פ"ב ה"א),[1] opens the historical account of the nineteenth berachah with a single phrase: bimei Rabban Gamliel — in the days of Rabban Gamliel. The poskim record a minor machlokes over which Rabban Gamliel is meant: Rabban Gamliel HaZaken, who stood in the days of the Bayis Sheni, or his grandson Rabban Gamliel, son of Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, who presided after the churban. The second is the likelier referent, for the tekanah is identified with Yavneh, and Rabban Gamliel HaZaken stood while the Beis HaMikdash was still in place.

A note of language that recurs in the Rishonim: this berachah is not called by them “Birkas HaMinim” but “Killelas HaMinim” (קללת המינים). It is the only berachah in Shemoneh Esrei composed entirely of kelalos — three times each day the mispallel asks that destruction fall upon a defined class of enemies. And the uncomfortable truth is that most of those who recite the berachah do not fully know what its words mean.

II. The Minim in Real Time

Who were the minim whom the Rambam places at the center of the tekanah? They were Yoshke (ישו) and his talmidim, and — critically — they were part of our own community. The Rambam stresses the point. The minim were not an alien people whose beliefs could be dismissed from a distance; they were Yidden within the kehillah, davening in the same batei knesses, learning the same pesukim — and investing enormous effort to persuade Klal Yisrael of Yoshke’s messianic claim. They were sufficiently successful that the earliest Christian movement was, almost in its entirety, a Jewish movement. The transmission to the gentile world came through a later figure — Paul, whom some identify as the Yid Shaul — who carried the message to the Romans, until then pagan, whose intellectually inclined classes found the new doctrine more compelling than the older paganism. Christianity went global from there.[2]

The tekanah of Birkas HaMinim addressed the internal problem directly. When a min stood as shaliach tzibbur and arrived at the berachah of v’laMalshinim, he could not curse himself; he would skip the berachah; the kehillah identified him; he was removed from the amud. The berachah served, among its purposes, as a liturgical sieve.

One historical refinement deserves acknowledgment. The full doctrine of the trinity as a developed theology did not exist in Yoshke’s lifetime; it took shape over the first centuries of the Christian era, maturing only in the age of Constantine and the Church Councils. Rav Shimon Schwab, in his sefer, lays weight on the trinity as the central doctrine the minim labored to impose on Klal Yisrael;[3] the historical refinement does not undo his framing, for even in its earlier form the messianic claim of Yoshke and the missionary pressure of his talmidim were cause enough to call forth the tekanah.

III. The Origin of the Berachah — Kri’as Yam Suf

The midrashic tradition ties each of the berachos of Shemoneh Esrei to a moment in Tanach at which the malachei ha’shareis first uttered its substance.[4] For v’laMalshinim, that moment is Kri’as Yam Suf. When the Egyptians were drowned and Klal Yisrael stood on the far shore — before “Az Yashir Moshe,” before Shiras HaYam began — the malachim opened: “Baruch Atah Hashem, shover oyvim u’machnia zedim” (ברוך אתה ה’ שובר אויבים ומכניע זדים).

In this sense the berachah stood already in the mesorah. What Rabban Gamliel did was to reactivate it for a new historical crisis; Shmuel HaKatan stood and formalized its wording for the standing tefillah.[5]

IV. The Centuries of Censorship — Nicholas Donin and the Paris Disputation

Once the berachah was fixed by Chazal, its text was anything but fixed in the centuries that followed. No berachah in Shemoneh Esrei suffered the pressure that this one did. The Rishonim record variation upon variation of its opening word and its named targets: v’laMalshinim, v’laMinim, malchus zadon, malchus ha’rish’ah, kol ha’resha’im, resha’ah. The multiplicity of nuscha’os is itself the historical record of a berachah under siege.

Rashi’s Censored Peirush

Rashi, in many places in the Bavli, glosses “minim” as “talmidei Yoshke ha’Notzri” — students of Yoshke the Christian. These Rashis were absent from printed editions for centuries; only in recent generations have they been restored from manuscript. The agency of the suppression has a name, and a date.

Nicholas Donin and the Burning of the Shas (Paris, 1242)

The first Yid to put the Talmud under systematic Christian censorship was Nicholas Donin of La Rochelle,[6] a talmid of Rabbeinu Yechiel of Paris[7] — one of the great Baalei HaTosafos who presided over an enormous yeshivah in Paris in the thirteenth century and later made aliyah with his talmidim to Akko. Donin, by all accounts a brilliant bachur, drifted into apikorsus under his rebbi’s eye; Rabbeinu Yechiel placed him under cherem. Two years later Donin converted to Christianity, attached himself to the Franciscan order and ultimately to the Pope, and informed: in their Talmud, he said, the Jews mock Yoshke and his talmidim.

The Vikuach of Paris — the public disputation over the Talmud — followed, and in 1242 the Shas was burned. The verdict reduced twenty-four cartloads of manuscripts to ash in a public square of Paris.

Donin also furnished the Church with the substitution scheme that ran through our printed editions for the next six centuries: wherever the Gemara had written “minim,” replace with “akum” (oivdei kochavim u’mazalos), or with “goyim” or “kusim.” Because Donin was a talmid chacham who knew where to look, the Church accepted the substitution as authoritative. The scheme occasionally overshot. The word min appears already in Bereishis — “l’mineihu” — in the sense of kind, species. When Chazal wrote “kol minei kitniyos” (every variety of legume), the mechanical censor’s pen produced the absurdity “kol akum kitniyos.” The comic residue is a reminder of a long tragedy.

V. The Meaning of “Minim”

The Acronym

A widespread tradition — whose precise source is unclear but whose circulation among the poskim is wide — reads “minim” as an acronym: Ma’aminim b’Yoshke ha’Notzri (מאמינים בישו הנוצרי). The variant “v’laMinim al tehi tikvah”, preserved in several ancient nuscha’os, lies behind the reading.

The Aruch and Benjamin Mussafia

The Sefer HeAruch, by Rabbeinu Nasan of Rome[8] — talmid of Rabbeinu Chananel and standing in the orbit of Rabbeinu Gershom, author of the first Talmudic-Aramaic dictionary, cited constantly by Rashi and Tosafos — gives the standard peirush of the entry “min.” The Aruch itself was among the first Hebrew works printed after the Tanach, Talmud, and Rambam, and its circulation shaped the Talmudic lexicon of the Rishonim.

A later edition of the Aruch was prepared by Benjamin Mussafia[9] — a seventeenth-century Italian-trained scholar who lived most of his life in Holland, a formidable linguist and talmid chacham. (It should be noted that the honorific “Rav” is, for Mussafia, something of an overstatement.) His editorial additions to the Aruch include sharp and in places polemical glosses — comments to the effect that Abaye and Rava did not know Greek or Latin or old Italian and so misread the Mishnah’s diction — on which account many later talmidei chachamim distanced themselves from his editorial voice. The deeper disclosure came only centuries later: Mussafia was a Sabbatean. Gershom Scholem,[10] in his documentary research on the Sabbatean movement, published the original rabbinic letter accepting Shabsai Zvi as mashiach; Mussafia’s signature stands upon it. The sharp register of Mussafia’s Talmudic polemic is of a piece with that affiliation.

On the entry “min,” however, Mussafia preserves a striking and important chiddush. In the days of Yavneh, he explains, the chavurah of Yoshke had separated themselves from Klal Yisrael and taken for themselves the self-designation Ma’aminim — “the true believers,” they claimed, “we read the Tanach as it is meant to be read.” Chazal, in response, took the self-designation and removed from it a single letter — the alef of Ma’aminim — and named them Minim. The surgical cut turned the self-flattering label into a byword for the opposite of what the chavurah had claimed to be. Mussafia offers a parallel reading beside this one: minim because there are many kinds of kofrim. In the end both readings converge on the single referent, talmidei Yoshke.

VI. The Ma’aseh in Tzippori — Avodah Zarah 27b

The Gemara in Avodah Zarah 27b[11] records a ma’aseh from Tzippori that renders the minim-problem in concrete terms. A young man presenting himself as a talmid of Yoshke performed what appeared to be healings in Yoshke’s name, drawing Yidden toward the Christian claim. The sugya rules sharply: a Yid may not accept healing from a min in Yoshke’s name even where there is sakanas nefashos — precisely because the surrender of the neshamah at the moment of greatest vulnerability is a surrender that the halachah will not permit. This is one of the rare sugyos in which direct reference to Yoshke and his talmidim escaped the censors and remained in our printed editions.

A moment of personal hakaras ha’tov: when this ma’aseh was first raised in our beis midrash, the Rav had the honesty to note that he had not immediately placed its exact source in Shas. He used the moment to invoke the memory of Rav Kalish zt”l[12] — a talmid chacham whom one could not catch on a sugya in Avodah Zarah any more than one could catch him on Sanhedrin or on any other masechta. Even a century on, it is a mitzvah to thank Hashem for the generation that produced such a Yid.

VII. The Leshonos of the Berachah — Six Verbs of Destruction

The berachah’s central petition unfolds in six verbs, each a distinct mode of undoing:

תְּעַקֵּר  |  תְּשַׁבֵּר  |  תְּכַלֶּה  |  תַּשְׁפִּילֵם  |  תַּכְנִיעֵם  |  תְּמַגֵּר
Uproot  |  Shatter  |  Make an end of  |  Lay them low  |  Subdue them  |  Crumble (fragment)

The sixth verb — temager — is the least familiar of the six, and even those who have said the berachah for decades often cannot translate it precisely. It belongs to the family of liMager, to shatter into fragments, to crumble, to break into pieces too small to reassemble (cognate with the modern Hebrew limager, to extirpate). The six verbs are not synonyms; they are six registers of destruction, each targeted at a different mode of enemy presence.

A Ma’aseh from the Rebbe of Lubavitch

A story is preserved from the court of Lubavitch,[13] told of the Rebbe’s pedagogy with a drifting bachur. A chassid came in distress: his son, sixteen or seventeen, a good boy at heart, had begun to attach himself to chevra who were less than frum. The Rebbe — whose manner of addressing a young person was itself a mofes — called the bachur in, opened a siddur to Birkas HaMinim, and walked through the verbs one by one.

“What does te’aker mean?” — “To uproot.” “And teshaber?” — “To break.” “Techaleh?” — “To make an end of them.” “Tashpileim?” — “To humiliate.” “Tachni’eim?” — “To subdue.” “And temager?” The bachur did not know. The Rebbe answered: “I am not sure of the exact shade either. But I know that it stands in this list — alongside the five others — and that each of them is a separate mode of destruction we daven down upon the enemies of Klal Yisrael.”

Then came the point. “You are a good bachur. But if you attach yourself to those chaveirim, those same leshonos will land on them — and on whoever is standing next to them. The Chazal is unforgiving: oy la’rasha, oy l’sheicheino — woe to the rasha, and woe to his neighbor” (Rashi, Bamidbar 16, on Korach).

The six verbs are a tefillah for the undoing of the enemies of Klal Yisrael. The kavvanah is not frozen in the first century. The Rishonim expanded the berachah’s scope from talmidei Yoshke alone to the resha’im of every generation. In our own day the mispallel should direct the words toward the particular rasha whose name is in that morning’s news — whoever he happens to be.[14]

The Distinction Between Oyvim and Zedim

As was developed in the previous shiur, the closing formula — shover oyvim u’machnia zedim — distinguishes two categories:

  • Oyvim — those bearing malicious intent against Klal Yisrael who have not yet moved to the act.
  • Zedim — those actively engaged in the attempt to inflict harm. In our day, anyone in Azah actively seeking to kill a Yid stands squarely in the zedim category.

VIII. Shmuel HaKatan — Father of Klal Yisrael

The Gemara in Berachos 28b[15] records the moment: Rabban Gamliel asked his talmidim who among them could compose the new berachah; Shmuel HaKatan stood and composed it. The following year, when he stood before the amud once again, he forgot the wording and labored for hours to recover it, and the Chachamim permitted him to continue. The unique status of the nineteenth berachah — a berachah composed not by the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah but by a single tanna in response to a historical crisis — is grounded in this sugya.

Shmuel HaKatan was niftar childless. The hespedim preserved from his levayah carry the weight of the loss: the Chachamim wept, and said that a king dies and leaves his crown for the next generation — but Shmuel HaKatan, chemdaso shel olam v’halach lo, the beloved of the world has gone, and left nothing behind. The Chachamim were mistaken on the last point: he left Klal Yisrael. Every Yid who recites the berachah he composed, three times each day, is his inheritor. And he left one further mishnah: the pasuk in Mishlei[16] that bears his name in Pirkei Avos — “Binfol oyivcha al tismach” — when your enemy falls, do not rejoice. That the author of the most explicitly anti-enemy berachah in Shemoneh Esrei chose precisely this pasuk as his single mishnaic legacy is the kind of productive tension that Chazal favor. The tefillah for the enemy’s undoing is one avodah; the warning against gloating over his fall is another; both belong to the same tanna.

IX. Birkas Al HaTzaddikim — The Textual Continuity

The ordering of Shemoneh Esrei is not a pragmatic arrangement but a structure derived in the Gemara from pesukim.[17] On the question of why Birkas Al HaTzaddikim stands immediately after Birkas HaMinim, the Gemara in Megillah brings a pasuk in Tehillim:[18]

וְכָל קַרְנֵי רְשָׁעִים אֲגַדֵּעַ, תְּרוֹמַמְנָה קַרְנוֹת צַדִּיק.
And all the horns of the resha’im I will cut off; the horns of the tzaddik will be raised up.
Tehillim 75:11

The cutting-down of the resha’im and the raising-up of the tzaddikim are not two separate events but two sides of a single movement. The structural pairing of the two berachos in Shemoneh Esrei embeds the pasuk’s logic. Confirming the connection textually: Birkas Al HaTzaddikim opens with a vav — “al ha’tzaddikim” is grammatically continuous with the berachah that precedes it.

One further note — often missed and critical: the request here is not solely for le’asid lavo. The mispallel has the right, in the present, before the coming of Mashiach, to ask that the resha’im and the minim be uprooted today, so that the tzaddikim may rise today.

X. The Six Categories of Al HaTzaddikim

The berachah enumerates six distinct categories upon whom rachamim are requested:

  1. Tzaddikim
  2. Chasidim
  3. Zikne she’eiris amcha beis Yisrael
  4. Peleitas beis sofreihem
  5. Gerei ha’tzedek
  6. V’aleinu

1–2. Tzaddikim and Chasidim

The common reading divides the two: tzaddikim are the tzaddikim proper, those whose standing was never fractured; chasidim are the baalei teshuvah, who returned from an earlier fracture and now stand with the tzaddikim. On this reading each Yid davens for both classes. One difficulty: the Gemara in Berachos 34b[19] teaches that “b’makom she’baalei teshuvah omdim, tzaddikim gemurim einam yecholim la’amod” — in the place where baalei teshuvah stand, even the complete tzaddikim cannot stand. If chasidim here are baalei teshuvah, the order of the berachah should reverse: chasidim first, then tzaddikim. The order as we have it stands the other way. The question remains open; the reading is held, but held with the kushya.

3. Zikne she’eiris amcha beis Yisrael

At this clause, the practical kavvanah is to daven for the zikne ha’dor. The Rav would daven for Maran HaGaon Rav Moshe Feinstein zt”l; today, for HaGaon Rav Aharon Schechter shlit”a.[20] “Zaken” here is not merely the chronologically aged; the Gemara in Kiddushin 32b[21] reads “zaken” as “zeh she’kanah chochmah” — one who has acquired Torah. The berachah is for the talmidei chachamim who serve as the amudim and the she’arios of the generation. One who has a rebbi should daven for him here: that he live, that he be well, that his hashpa’ah continue.

A Ma’aseh — Reb Yitzchak Charif and the Yesod HaAvodah

Reb Yitzchak Charif (Rav Yitzchak Shapiro) was the Rav of Slonim — the appellation “Charif” after his name reflecting the sharpness of his lomdus. Slonim held a major chassidus presence; the Yesod HaAvodah,[22] founder of Slonimer chassidus, lived in the town. Reb Yitzchak, a misnaged, had his standing disputes with the chassidim.

Once, after the Yesod HaAvodah had been elevated to the position of Rebbe, Reb Yitzchak met him and asked, sharp as ever: “Yesterday you were a simple baalebos sitting in the shtiebel. What happened — you changed overnight?”

The Yesod HaAvodah answered with a kabbalah he traced to the Baal Shem Tov. Consider terumah: when a Yid lifts a single grain of wheat from a pile and declares, “This is terumah,” what has changed in the wheat? Nothing in its physical substance. And yet the moment it is set apart, any non-kohen who eats it is chayav misah bidei Shamayim. The status of the wheat has shifted entirely by the act of designation. So too, the Baal Shem Tov taught, when ten Yidden gather and elevate one of their own and designate him as Rebbe — even if he is an ordinary grain — the designation sets his prior standing aside as chulin and establishes him in a new status. “That,” he said, “is what happened to me.”

Reb Yitzchak Charif, true to form, answered on the spot. The Mishnah at the opening of Masseches Terumos begins “Chomer ba’Terumah” — which Reb Yitzchak parsed as a pun: “You are a chamor — a donkey — and you are a machmir besides!”

Both sides of the verbal jousting leave an emes intact: the chassidishe velt and the misnagdic velt alike hold that real Rebbe’im exist in Klal Yisrael. The berachah’s zikne she’eiris amcha davens for them both.

4. Peleitas beis sofreihem

Who are the sofrim? The Gemara in Kiddushin 30a[23] explains that Chazal, both Tannaim and Amoraim, were called sofrim because they would count (lispor) the letters of the Torah. The sugya on counting the pesukim, letters, and words of the Torah is a self-contained Talmudic genre, and the sofer here is the chacham who holds the chain of textual transmission in his hand. But Chazal also apply the term to the melamdei tinokos — the Rebbe’im in cheder and in the elementary classrooms who teach young children. The poskim highlight that the clause peleitas beis sofreihem should hold the melamdim of our children specifically in mind.

Rav Shimon Schwab on the Word “Peleitah”

Rav Shimon Schwab,[24] in his sefer on tefillah, draws the force of the single word peleitah — survivors, remnants. There are not many of them. Most ordinary baalei batim do not want the position; the pay is poor; the hours are long. A sixth-grade Rebbe in Lakewood is often a Yid who commutes to Brooklyn or the Five Towns on the side simply to make the household budget work. The tefillah davens that this peleitah survive.

The Rav spoke of his own younger brother, who had the choice between joining the family grandfather’s business and taking a sixth-grade Rebbe position. He took the Rebbe position. Teaching young children is not a career; it is a calling. One is loading boxes of chumashim into the trunk of the car at the start of the year. Those who do this with sincerity are moserei nefesh.

Not every generation’s melamdim have met the calling cleanly. The Rav spoke recently with a Yid in his mid-seventies who had learned in a particular cheder in Yerushalayim as a child; of twenty-seven or twenty-eight boys in that class, only three are frum today. The cheder Rebbe was a Holocaust survivor whose grief had turned to anger, and he hit the boys. The Yid’s own survival of Yiddishkeit, he told the Rav, was traceable to the fact that the Rebbe had rachmanus on him because he was an orphan, and so did not hit him. The anecdote carries a historical weight that no modern pedagogical complaint can match.

Today Klal Yisrael, baruch Hashem, has Rebbe’im — young people who have chosen the path of teaching the next generation. The chiyuv, per Chazal, is to thank them, to bless them, and to daven for them. If a grandfather has seven, eight, ten, twenty einiklach in chedarim across the world, let him pause and think of their melamdim: that they be well-rested and composed; that they teach with chochmah and with rachamim; that they not hit. The clause peleitas beis sofreihem, every morning, is the place for this tefillah.

5. Gerei ha’tzedek

The Yaaros Devash[25] — Rav Yonasan Eibeschutz of Prague — preserves in a derashah delivered in his shul in Prague a teaching the Rav takes as the practical register of this clause. The pasuk[26] commands: v’ahavtem es ha’ger — and you shall love the ger. For a Yid who does not encounter a ger in his day-to-day life, when was the last time he had any explicit kavvanah to fulfill the mitzvas asei of loving the ger? The Yaaros Devash’s answer: three times a day, in v’al gerei ha’tzedek, with proper kavvanah, the mitzvah is fulfilled. “V’ahavta l’rei’acha kamocha” directed by this clause toward the most marginalized member of Klal Yisrael.

The Rav shared the story of a soldier in Tzahal who was mid-conversion when October 7 came. He ran to the Beis Din of the Rabbanus: “Finish my giyur! Speed up my conversion!” They asked why. “Because I am about to deploy to Azah, to the battlefield. I want to die as a Yid.” Such gerei tzedek exist in our own day. They are worthy of the clause.

6. V’aleinu

Rav Nasan of Breslov, in Likutei Halachos,[27] presses the obvious question. We have davened for tzaddikim, for chasidim, for the zikne she’eiris, for the sofrim, for the baalei teshuvah, for the gerim — the great categories of Klal Yisrael. How then do we — who may belong to none of these clubs — end up at the close of the list? The answer: even if we have not yet entered those clubs, we have an hizdamnus here to daven that we be granted entry. “V’sim chelkenu imahem” — place our portion with them. Three words to dwell on.

Rav Yisrael Salanter to Reb Naftali Amsterdam

Reb Naftali Amsterdam — one of the three principal talmidim of Rav Yisrael Salanter,[28] alongside Rav Itzele of Peterburg and Rav Simcha Zissel of Kelm — once said to his rebbi: “I have neither the zechus of the Rema nor the limud of the Gra. Who am I? What am I?”

Rav Yisrael Salanter’s famous answer: “The Oibershter does not want you to be a gaon like the mekubal. He does not want you to be a tzaddik like the Baal Shem Tov. He does not want you to be a Rav like the Chasam Sofer. He wants you, Naftali — the gaon that Naftali can be, the tzaddik that Naftali can be.”

That is the peshat of v’sim chelkenu imahem. Not that we will become Baal Shem Tov or Chasam Sofer. That we maximize what the Ribbono Shel Olam deposited specifically in us. When we do, we have the right to stand in the berachah of tzaddikim and chasidim and ask — honestly — to be counted with them.

XI. The Arizal’s Pirush on “Chelkenu”

Beneath the straightforward reading of v’sim chelkenu imahem, the Arizal,[29] as transmitted by Rav Chaim Vital in Sha’ar HaKavanos, lays out a kavvanah of altogether different depth. The Rav offered it lidi’ah — as a chiddush to know — not necessarily as the kavvanah every mispallel will adopt from this morning onward.

The Mechanism of Mitzvos Transfer

The Gemara in Sotah 21a[30] takes up the interaction between mitzvos and aveiros: Torah shields; mitzvah protects; but the shielding has its limits. The Arizal builds on this structural ground and offers a kabbalistic chiddush: an aveirah does not extinguish earlier mitzvos. The spiritual kochos — the oros and the malachim — that those mitzvos generated are not destroyed; they are transferred, temporarily, to the tzaddikei ha’dor, until the Yid who originally generated them does teshuvah and reclaims them.

The parallel in the Chofetz Chaim[31] on the mechanics of lashon hara — the speaker’s zechuyos transfer to the victim — may rest on the same structural intuition. (The Rav himself was candid that he had not located the precise mekor for the Chofetz Chaim’s formulation; it is worth noting the candor rather than dressing the citation.) Children in cheder say playfully, “Don’t speak lashon hara on me — now I have all your mitzvos!” What sounds like a joke, the Chofetz Chaim writes as Torah.

Two Types of Tzaddikim

When the transferred mitzvos arrive at the tzaddikei ha’dor, the Arizal continues, the tzaddik faces a choice. Two kinds of tzaddikim respond in two different ways:

  • Some tzaddikim claim them. They say: “Wonderful — these oros, these malachim, are now mine.” The mitzvos are absorbed into the tzaddik’s own ledger.
  • Other tzaddikim refuse to claim them. They say: “I will do my own mitzvos; I do not need yours.” They hold the malachim and the oros in trust. When the Yid who originally generated them does teshuvah, those tzaddikim return the mitzvos to his hand.

The Kavvanah

The kavvanah for “chelkenu,” the Arizal’s teacher instructed him, is this. “Chelkenu” refers to the mitzvos we ourselves performed and which, through our later aveiros, were transferred away. We daven “v’sim chelkenu imahem” — let our portion be with the tzaddikim of the second kind, those who will return what is ours when we do teshuvah. Let the tzaddik currently holding our mitzvos in trust be a tzaddik who returns, not a tzaddik who keeps. The chiddush reframes the berachah from a request for proximity to tzaddikim into a request about the disposition of our own dispersed zechuyos.

XII. Bitachon — Real and Pretended

Beneath the berachah as a whole lies the desire to be numbered among the tzaddikim of real bitachon. Rabbeinu Avraham ben HaRambam, in HaMaspik L’Ovdei Hashem,[32] develops a sharp chiddush: everyone uses the words “emunah” and “bitachon” at every turn — they are easy to pronounce. True emunah and true bitachon, in the technical sense — actually living, through the real tzaros of life, in unwavering reliance on the Ribbono Shel Olam — are held by very few: yechidei segulah in each generation.

When we come up to Shamayim we will be asked “Tzipisa li’yeshuah?” — did you actually wait for the yeshuah, did you actually hold bitachon? The embarrassment of having spoken these words a thousand times and never actually held them is the bushah and cherpah the mispallel davens not to carry up. “V’sim chelkenu imahem”: place our portion with those whose bitachon was real, not the cheap surface bitachon of the mouth alone.

XIII. The Origin of the Berachah — Yaakov and Yosef

Where Birkas HaMinim’s first-utterance moment was located at Kri’as Yam Suf, the midrash[33] locates the first-utterance moment of Birkas Al HaTzaddikim at the deathbed of Yaakov Avinu. Years earlier, before Yaakov descended to Mitzrayim, the Ribbono Shel Olam had told him[34]: “V’Yosef yashis yado al einecha” — Yosef will outlive you and place his hands upon your eyes (closing them in death, by the minhag of a son for a father). At the close of Parshas Vayechi, Yosef stood beside Yaakov’s bed, placed his hands on his father’s eyes, kissed him, and wept. The havtachah made in Be’er Sheva on the eve of the descent to Mitzrayim had been fulfilled, exactly, decades later. In that moment, the midrash teaches, the malachim opened: baruch mish’an u’mivtach la’tzaddikim — Hashem is the support and the security of the tzaddikim; the havtachos He makes to them are kept.

In Birkas Al HaTzaddikim, the mispallel steps into the inheritance of Yaakov Avinu and asks for the same standing: that he be numbered among the tzaddikim for whom Hashem’s havtachos are, in due course, fulfilled.

XIV. Closing

Two berachos that at first glance seem opposite in mahus — one entirely kelalos upon the minim, the other entirely rachamim upon the tzaddikim — are revealed by the Gemara and by the midrash to be two sides of a single pasuk: the cutting off of the karnei resha’im and the raising up of the karnei tzaddik, in a single movement. The tanna who authored the first was niftar childless, and left Klal Yisrael as his inheritance; the Arizal teaches that when a Yid does teshuvah, the mitzvos he thought lost are returned to his hand from tzaddikim who held them in trust. The two together are the architecture of the mispallel’s standing before his King: destruction asked upon the enemies of Klal Yisrael, elevation asked upon the tzaddikim, and — in the single word chelkenu — a seat requested among those who have the real bitachon and whose havtachos are kept.

B’ezras Hashem, in the shiur to follow: “u’Vneh Yerushalayim ir ha’kodesh bimheirah b’yameinu.”
