עַל נַהֲרוֹת בָּבֶל — עִיּוּן בְּתְהִלִּים קל״ז

Al Naharos Bavel — A Study of Tehillim 137: Settled, Weeping, and the Harps upon the Willows
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Special Pre-Tishah B’Av Study
Recorded ו׳ אב תשפ״ה  ·  31 July 2025, three nights before תשעה באב  ·  Sefer Edition

Upon פרק קל״ז of Tehillim: the Malbim’s reading of a community settled yet weeping; the Radak on tears that prevent song; the Midrash’s account of the march to Bavel, of the harps hung upon the willows, and of the thumbs bitten off; the Alshich on the worst aspect of galus; the question of the malachim; and the closing pesukim reframed as gemul.

I. Why This Perek

It is fitting to gather before Tishah B’Av (תשעה באב) in order to enter the consciousness that the season requires. Among the chapters of Tehillim (תהלים), none is more suited to the task than פרק קל״ז, “Al Naharos Bavel” (על נהרות בבל), which is recited on weekdays before Birkas HaMazon (ברכת המזון). The Shulchan Aruch (שולחן ערוך) at the opening of אורח חיים establishes grief over the churban (חורבן) as a standing obligation, and the Mishnah Berurah (משנה ברורה), citing the Shelah (של״ה), records the practice of reciting this perek, with Shir HaMaalos (שיר המעלות) said in its place on Shabbos and on days when Tachanun is omitted.[1] To read the perek in depth, with what Chazal (חז״ל) and the mefarshim (מפרשים) have to say, is to set the perspective for what a Jew is meant to feel during Tishah B’Av and the days that surround it.

Dovid HaMelech (דוד המלך) composed this perek long before the churban of the Bayis Rishon, beholding it through ruach hakodesh (רוח הקודש).[2] The image he draws is one that Israel knows well — burned into the neshamos (נשמות) of every generation, even of those whose parents and grandparents were the ones who saw it directly. How many paintings of that scene have been made; how many photographs.

II. Pasuk Alef — Settled, and Still Weeping

עַל נַהֲרוֹת בָּבֶל שָׁם יָשַׁבְנוּ גַּם בָּכִינוּ בְּזָכְרֵנוּ אֶת צִיּוֹן.
By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat, and also wept, when we remembered Zion.
Tehillim 137:1

The Literal Reading

On the plain reading the verse paints a scene of mourners (aveilim, אבלים) walking the banks of the rivers of Bavel with sacks upon their backs, newly expelled from Yerushalayim, weeping as they remember Tzion (ציון).

The Malbim’s Reading — Settled and Weeping

The Malbim (מלבי״ם) proposes a different reading.[3] One must not suppose for a moment that in Bavel the exiles lacked material comfort (gashmius, גשמיות); they had it all — property, commerce, prosperity, whatever a community requires in order to endure in galus (גלות). It took time to break into the Babylonian network of business and wealth, but they broke in. The Malbim adduces the pesukim in Yirmiyahu (ירמיהו) which describe how, not long after their arrival, the Jews of Bavel already had homes, fields, and vineyards.[4]

Accordingly, “sham yashavnu” (שם ישבנו), on the Malbim’s reading, does not describe sitting on the floor in the manner of a mourner. It means that they settled; as the Yiddish idiom has it, “es iz geven in ordnung” (עס איז געווען אין ארדנונג) — it was in order; they had what they needed. And then: “gam bachinu” (גם בכינו) — even so they wept. Despite the comfort, despite the material ease, they retained the capacity to weep for what they had lost.

III. Pasuk Beis — The Harps upon the Willows

עַל עֲרָבִים בְּתוֹכָהּ תָּלִינוּ כִּנֹּרוֹתֵינוּ.
Upon the willows in its midst we hung our harps.
Tehillim 137:2

What the Kinnoros Signify

What does a person take with him when he is expelled from his homeland? On the eve of the Shoah, what did the generation of our grandparents take? The most precious, the most sentimental, the most valuable thing they owned. Dovid HaMelech says that when Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל) went forth from Yerushalayim, they took their harps — their kinnoros (כנורות), their violins.

The kinnoros represent the bond of Klal Yisrael with HaKadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא). When Jews ascended to the Beis HaMikdash (בית המקדש) for the avodah (עבודה), the Leviim (לוים) sang shir v’zimrah. That divine music — not music as it is known today, but holy niggunim, kedushah rendered in sound — awakened dveikus (דבקות) with HaKadosh Baruch Hu. Nevuah (נבואה) in ancient times came by way of music; the kinnoros were the instrument of that connection. That is what the exiles carried with them.

The Willows — Salix Babylonica

Dovid HaMelech describes the harps hung upon the aravim (ערבים) beside the Euphrates. In botanical taxonomy these trees are named Salix babylonica — the Babylonian willow, named in consequence of this very verse — and in English they are called weeping willows, for their drooping branches that appear to mourn. Klal Yisrael hung their harps upon trees that themselves seemed to weep.[5]

The Radak — When Tears Prevent Song

The Radak (רד״ק) explains that Klal Yisrael had taken the harps in the hope of a quiet interval — a moment of menuchas hanefesh (מנוחת הנפש) in which to take them out and play, and to hear again the melodies that had stirred dveikus in the Beis HaMikdash.[6] When that moment finally arrived, they began to play — and could not continue. The memory of Tzion, of the glory of the Beis HaMikdash, broke through; they wept and stopped. “We cannot play. It is the wrong time. We cannot play shir Hashem (שיר ה׳).” And so they hung the kinnoros upon the weeping willows: “You belong here now. We are too overcome. Your time will come; but this is a time to weep, not to play shir Hashem.”

The Malbim — The Sweetness of Bavel

The Malbim’s chiddush (חידוש) is sharper.[3] He reads aravim as a play on words: aravim as willows, and aravim in the sense of sweet things (mil’shon areivim, מלשון ערבים — נעימים). Because of the sweetness of Bavel they hung up their harps.

Bavel tempted them with the pleasures of olam hazeh (עולם הזה): “Come and join us; sing and dance with us; eat our food; enjoy our wealth; enjoy Bavel.” And precisely because of that sweetness — “al aravim b’socha taliynu kinnoroseinu” (על ערבים בתוכה תלינו כנרותינו) — “How can we play music that celebrates the materialism of Bavel? How can we celebrate their culture, their way of life, their olam hazeh, with our harps?”

The sweetness of olam hazeh — what does it represent? It represents a world that hates ruchnius (רוחניות), that hates yiddishkeit (יידישקייט), that hates Jews. And the reason for the hatred is that Israel possesses the Torah and a bond with HaKadosh Baruch Hu; Israel is the antithesis of the celebration of olam hazeh for its own sake. Israel employs olam hazeh in the service of avodah, and avodah is far higher.

When, therefore, Bavel called Israel to dance to its music, the Jews of that generation answered no: “Sham yashavnu” — though we settled here; “gam bachinu” — we still weep, because we still yearn for kedushah (קדושה); “taliynu kinnoroseinu” — we hung up our harps. “Forgive us, Nevuchadnetzar; forgive us, Bavel; forgive us, umos haolam (אומות העולם). We know what we have lost. We may not possess it now, but we yearn to be reconnected to it, and the two cannot be mixed.”

IV. Pesukim Gimmel–Daled — Bavel’s Demand and the Jewish Refusal

כִּי שָׁם שְׁאֵלוּנוּ שׁוֹבֵינוּ דִּבְרֵי שִׁיר וְתוֹלָלֵינוּ שִׂמְחָה שִׁירוּ לָנוּ מִשִּׁיר צִיּוֹן.
For there our captors asked of us words of song, and our tormentors asked for joy: “Sing for us from the songs of Zion.”
Tehillim 137:3

The word “toluleinu” (תוללינו) derives from the root t-l-l, the same root as the harps that were hung: Bavel demanded that they take down what they had hung up, and play. Bavel had seen Klal Yisrael hang the harps upon the willows and declared: “No! Ruchnius is your past. You are out of your land. The Jewish story of connection with HaKadosh Baruch Hu in the Beis HaMikdash is over. Olam hazeh is the present. You are a nation among the nations now.”

What Was Demanded

The Malbim reads the demand with precision. Every nation has an anthem — a song that boasts of its wealth, its resources, its power, its talents. Bavel asked: “What is yours?” Israel’s song was shir Hashem — the songs of the Beis HaMikdash, the shir shel yom (שיר של יום), the chapters of Tehillim (kapitlach Tehillim, קפיטלעך תהלים), the shirah of the hashraas haShechinah (השראת השכינה).

And Bavel sought something worse still. “Do not stop singing shir Hashem. We wish to hear it — the way you sang and danced to HaKadosh Baruch Hu in the Beis HaMikdash. Take that very song and change only the words. Cut and paste. Where you sang to HaKadosh Baruch Hu, sing to Bavel — the same intensity, the same passion, the same enthusiasm — for olam hazeh.”

Israel refused. At that moment Klal Yisrael preserved their dignity (dignitet, דיגניטעט): “We are not like the umos haolam. We will not dance to the tunes of the nations of the world. We will not sing their music.” The point cuts deep, and it is not narrowly about Jewish or non-Jewish music. It concerns taking shir Hashem and bending it into a celebration of olam hazeh. That is what Bavel wanted; that is what Klal Yisrael would not give.

אֵיךְ נָשִׁיר אֶת שִׁיר ה׳ עַל אַדְמַת נֵכָר.
How shall we sing the song of Hashem upon foreign soil?
Tehillim 137:4

Dovid HaMelech asks: how can one request of a Jew who has known kedushah in the Beis HaMikdash — who lived within the hashraas haShechinah that confirmed itself daily, who could approach a navi (נביא) and ask, “What is my task?” — to sing shir Hashem upon the soil of strangers? It is impossible. One cannot expect a Jew who knows what shir Hashem is, and what shir Tzion is, to celebrate the materialism of Bavel with the songs of Tzion. The two do not go together.

V. The Midrash — The Brutality of the March

A Jew must know this Midrash in order to know who he is.[7]

The Midrash on the first pasuk teaches that Bavel did not allow the exiles to sit for a moment. They were driven out of Yerushalayim and made to walk and walk — in chains, hungry, thirsty, beneath the burning Middle Eastern sun. It was Tishah B’Av. No food, no drink, metal chains, columns marched in single file. The echo of the Shoah is unmistakable: schnell, schnell — the guards’ shouted command to hurry — and dogs barking. Go. No time to think. No time for a moment of menuchas hanefesh.

Even the simplest human need — to weep, to begin to come to terms emotionally with the tragedy — the Babylonians would not permit. After losing so much, a person needs to weep. The first time they were permitted to sit was “al naharos Bavel”, at the Euphrates, after a tremendous distance. “Sham yashavnu” — there, finally, after so many days, they could sit; and for the first time they could weep.

The Alshich — The Worst Aspect of Galus

The Alshich HaKadosh (אלשיך הקדוש) asks: what is the worst aspect of galus Bavel? What is the worst aspect of the churban Beis HaMikdash?[8] It is not the destruction of the Beis HaMikdash itself, and it is not the geographic exile. The worst of it is that they did not let us weep.

To forbid a Jew to weep is the worst galus. And perhaps the Alshich intends more than the physical prevention. When a Jew engages too deeply with olam hazeh, when he dulls his sensitivity to kedushah — to yiddishkeit, to the Beis HaMikdash, to the Shechinah HaKedoshah (שכינה הקדושה) — when he becomes like Bavel rather than like the Jews who said no, then he can no longer weep. Not because anyone forces him, but because desensitization has set in. He knows that he is supposed to weep; he knows that there is something to weep about; but there is a numbness, and the tears do not come.

The Soldier at the Kosel

The well-known account of 1967 illustrates the point.[9] When the Israeli forces reached the Kosel (כותל), the cry went up: “Har HaBayis b’yadeinu! HaKosel b’yadeinu!” (הר הבית בידינו! הכותל בידינו!) Jews rushed from across Yerushalayim to embrace the stones — to pray, to dance, to weep. Among them stood a soldier from a kibbutz, a secular soldier from a non-religious upbringing, weeping. Beside him stood a religious soldier of his platoon — a young man with a kippah and tzitzis — also weeping. The religious soldier asked: “I understand why I am crying. Why are you?”

The secular soldier answered: “Do you know why I am crying? I am crying because I do not know what you are crying about. I do not understand what there is to cry about — and that itself makes me cry.”

Only a Jew could give that answer. The neshamah of a Jew, even when buried beneath decades of secular life, knows that there is something to weep about. The Jews of Bavel retained that capacity; many in galus, across our generations, have not. The peshat of the Alshich stands.

VI. Nevuchadnetzar’s Cruise and the Pride of the Exiles

The Midrash continues. As the Jews walked along the river, Nevuchadnetzar (נבוכדנצר) was on a ship sailing the Euphrates beside them, his officers and nobles (sarav v’gedolav, שריו וגדוליו) with him, kol minei zemer (כל מיני זמר) playing royal music, eating and drinking and carousing (ochlim v’shosim um’karkerin, אוכלים ושותים ומקרקרין). The contrast is complete: the oppressor in luxury; the oppressed in chains.

Heads Held High

Watching the Jews walk into galus, Nevuchadnetzar saw something extraordinary. Their heads were held high. Their bearing was dignified, though they were stripped, in shackles, bereft of everything, carrying a sack of clothing and a worn harp or violin. A Jew is a Jew. Even leaving Yerushalayim with smoke billowing behind them, even with everything taken from them, they did not bow their heads.

Nevuchadnetzar demanded of his servants: “What is this? How do these Jews walk into galus with their heads held high? Take loads of sand and place them upon their backs, that they should not walk with their heads erect!” The servants gathered sacks (the Midrash says they fashioned them from books), filled them with sand, and set them upon every Jew. Now they could no longer hold their heads high; bent and hunched, they walked like those who were truly going into galus. Nevuchadnetzar could not endure the sight of a Jew walking into galus with his dignity intact.

HaKadosh Baruch Hu’s Decision

At that moment, the Midrash teaches, when even the pride of Klal Yisrael was being stripped away — when Bavel declared, “You are no longer a Jew; that is your past; you are like everyone else” — HaKadosh Baruch Hu decided: “Enough (dayeinu, דיינו). If this is how My children are treated, let the world return to tohu vavohu (תוהו ובוהו); let us begin again. This is not the world I created.”

The Malachim’ Plea

The malachim (מלאכים) came before HaKadosh Baruch Hu: “Ribbono shel Olam (רבונו של עולם), how can You destroy the world? It is grievous enough that You have destroyed the Beis HaMikdash; must You now destroy the whole of creation?”

HaKadosh Baruch Hu answered — and the language of the Midrash is severe — “תנחומין הללו שאתם מנחמים אותי ניאוצין הן לפני — these consolations you offer Me are provocations before Me.” The Midrash adds the words of Yeshayahu (ישעיהו כ״ב:ד׳): “אל תאיצו לנחמני.” Comfort from the malachim is unwelcome at that hour. “Do you have a complaint? Go down to the world below. Go to those Jews walking ‘al naharos Bavel’ and lift the burdens from their backs.”

At once the malachim descended and removed the loads. Eventually Nevuchadnetzar himself restored some clothing to the exiles. For a moment their dignity was restored — because the malachim intervened, after HaKadosh Baruch Hu told them to remedy the matter themselves rather than to offer consolation.

VII. The Question, and Its Midrashic Answer

The Question

A question arises here — one that sounds sacrilegious, and yet one that must be asked.[10] The one who asks it is a grandson of survivors of the Shoah and a great-grandson of Jews who died al kiddush Hashem (על קידוש השם). Israel knows what it is to be killed and to be exiled; and it has seen what the evil ones (yemach shemam, ימח שמם) did on Simchas Torah (שמחת תורה) two years ago. None of this is foreign.

Where were the malachim seventy-five, eighty years ago, in Auschwitz? When Jews descended from the trains naked, carrying whatever they could salvage from their homes? When children were torn from their mothers and sent to the right and to the left? Where was HaKadosh Baruch Hu through every expulsion, every pogrom, every persecution across the centuries? Where were the malachim in Kfar Aza (כפר עזה), in Moshav Be’eri (מושב בארי)? If HaKadosh Baruch Hu acts thus when He sees His children leaving Yerushalayim for Bavel — declaring “dayeinu; I shall destroy the world; remove the loads from their backs” — where were the malachim in all the exiles since?

It is known that such questions are not to be asked, yet they trouble every one of us. The Midrash does not answer the question on its own terms; it reframes it as a question put to the questioner. What the Midrash says next is the beginning of that reframing.

The Demand to Sing

As the Jews sat for the first time at the rivers of Bavel — hungry, thirsty, exhausted — Nevuchadnetzar ordered them: “Rise and prepare yourselves. I am about to begin a feast upon this ship. Take your harps down from the willows and play for me.”

His own band possessed kol minei kli zemer, and it did not suffice. He wanted the Jew’s music. He wanted to take shir Hashem and uproot it from the heart, redirecting that intensity toward what Bavel represents: materialism.

The Midrash relates that Klal Yisrael looked at one another and said: “Is it not enough that we destroyed the Beis HaMikdash through our aveiros (עבירות)? Now we are to stand and play, upon those very same instruments, before this dwarf (hanenas hazeh, הננס הזה), before this wicked one, before his avodah zarah (עבודה זרה)?”

What This Teaches

One must pause and take in what the Midrash is teaching. Even hungry, thirsty, beneath heavy loads, even after losing his ruchnius and his world, the Jew can look at the man who did this to him, at the rasha (רשע), and say: “I should stand and play for this dwarf?”

That is a Jew. Nevuchadnetzar was victorious; he had burned and destroyed everything sacred — and now he wants the Jew to play for him? The Jews understood. They had the sensitivity to recognize that he was a dwarf in the larger scheme of things: physically, as Chazal say, Nevuchadnetzar was small,[11] but far more fundamentally, in the order of the universe. Shall one take shir Hashem and play it for one so small? Play it before his avodah zarah?

The Mashal of the Queen

The Midrash offers a mashal (משל). There was a queen married to a king, both of royal lineage. One day he said: “My queen, pour me a cup of wine.” She was in a rebellious mood and refused. He expelled her from his house for refusing to pour wine for the king.

She wandered, seeking a home, seeking someone to marry. At length she found a husband — but he was a mukeh shechin (מוכה שחין), covered in boils from head to foot, an awful sight. Yet that was who she found.

One day the mukeh shechin said to her: “My dear wife, pour me a cup of wine.”

What did she answer? The Midrash records: “ריקה! Empty-headed fool! I did not pour wine for the king when he asked. I erred; I sinned; I should have. But now you want me to pour wine for you — for a mukeh shechin?”

So Klal Yisrael: “I did not pour wine for HaKadosh Baruch Hu. I did not play music for HaKadosh Baruch Hu as I should have, and that is why the Beis HaMikdash was destroyed. Now you ask me to play music for the nations of the world, for Bavel, for this dwarf, for this wicked one, for this idolatry? Shall I pour wine for the mukeh shechin? No. I am still a Jew. I have my pride, my dignitet. We will not sell ourselves to the nations who would have us dance to their tunes.”

The Bitten Thumbs

The Midrash records — sparing the gory details — that the Jews saw no way out of the demand: he would force them. So they took their right thumbs, the fingers needed to play the strings, and bit them off. They maimed themselves, so that they would be unable to play the harps for Nevuchadnetzar, for his avodah zarah, for this dwarf.

And then they said the next pasuk — not “we will not” but “eich nashir” (איך נשיר), “how can we?” Our fingers are gone; we have no hands left; we cannot play. They held up their maimed hands to Nevuchadnetzar: “We cannot.”

Nevuchadnetzar’s Rage and the Secret of Galus

This enraged Nevuchadnetzar. Herein lies the secret of galus. He wished to tear the Beis HaMikdash from their hearts, to uproot ruchnius. And the Jews bit off their fingers rather than play the music of olam hazeh, the music of materialism, the music of the dwarf.

He was enraged because he understood, in that moment, what a Jew is. One may take the Beis HaMikdash from him; one may take Eretz Yisrael and Yerushalayim; one may take ruchnius from him — and he remains a Jew, who looks upon his captor as upon a dwarf. You are nothing.

One may take the Beis HaMikdash from him; one may take Eretz Yisrael and Yerushalayim; one may take ruchnius from him — and he is still a Jew.

At that moment Nevuchadnetzar became so enraged that he slaughtered Jews by the thousands upon thousands before they ever reached galus — killed brutally by Nevuchadnetzar and his soldiers. The Midrash reads “v’toluleinu simchah” (ותוללינו שמחה) in this light: “תלי תלים של הרוגים” — heaps upon heaps of the slain.

Yet the Midrash says that this is a mizmor (מזמור), a song of Dovid HaMelech: there was simchah. It was not any pleasure in the deaths themselves; it was the joy of restraint — of not having given in, of not having sold themselves to the music of the nations. When they turned and saw the heaps of corpses upon the ground, that very sight brought joy into their hearts, because they still knew that they were Jews, the nation of HaKadosh Baruch Hu.

The Answer to the Question

The moment a Jew goes out into galus, he is already re-establishing his connection with the Beis HaMikdash and with HaKadosh Baruch Hu. That is a Jew. The very same Jews through whose sin the Beis HaMikdash was destroyed, when they see how Nevuchadnetzar treats them, respond at once: “We are still Jews. We will not give you shir Tzion. How could the joy we had in singing in the Beis HaMikdash be equated with the songs of olam hazeh? We cannot. Our hands are maimed. The harps belong upon the weeping willows. That is where they belong.”

And the question returns, with the answer within it: Are we ready to bite off our fingers rather than dance and sing to the tunes of the nations of the world?

The Jews of that generation were ready — and they did so. The malachim descended for them. If we are not prepared to make even a small sacrifice in our olam hazeh for the sake of our yiddishkeit, then the calculus is different.

VIII. The Essence of Galus

Galus is not merely sitting on Tishah B’Av and reciting kinnos (קינות) for a few hours and calling it a day. Galus means the victory of olam hazeh — the triumph of Bavel, of materialism, of excessive luxury, of eating and drinking without restraint. It means living a coarse life (a grub life, in the Yiddish term), the life of a goy, instead of living as a Jew is meant to live: in the Beis HaMikdash, with kedushah and taharah (טהרה), elevating all materiality toward something transcendent.

The moment a Jew declares, “I no longer enjoy my Mikdash; I have no pleasure from it; at least let me enjoy life — eat, drink, be merry; let me enjoy the prosperity Bavel offers” — at that moment that Jew loses the ability to weep.

The Gemara in Bava Basra 60b

The Gemara (גמרא) records that at the time of the churban many Jews began to abstain from meat and wine.[12] Rabbi Yehoshua (רבי יהושע) approached them: “Why?” They answered: “How can we eat meat? How can we drink wine? Meat was brought just now as korbanos (קרבנות) in the Beis HaMikdash, drawing us close to HaKadosh Baruch Hu; wine was poured as nesachim (נסכים) upon the mizbeach (מזבח), bringing us to dveikus. Now? It has no taste. How can we?”

Rabbi Yehoshua replied: “If so, we should also not eat bread, for the menachos (מנחות) have ceased. We should not eat fruit, for the bikkurim (ביכורים) have ceased. We should not even drink water, for the water libation has ceased.” They were silent.

The Gemara concludes with the principle that a decree is not imposed upon the tzibbur (ציבור) unless the majority can abide by it (גזירה שאין הציבור יכול לעמוד בה). The Nine Days alone are difficult enough; every few seconds there is another siyum on Zoom or elsewhere. Universal abstention cannot be imposed l’maaseh (למעשה). Yet the sensitivity that the Midrash records — “How can I enjoy any olam hazeh when I do not have the Beis HaMikdash?” — is the spirit of the halachah (הלכה), even if it is not the letter.

IX. Pesukim Hei–Vav — “Im Eshkachaich Yerushalayim”

אִם אֶשְׁכָּחֵךְ יְרוּשָׁלִָם תִּשְׁכַּח יְמִינִי. תִּדְבַּק לְשׁוֹנִי לְחִכִּי אִם לֹא אֶזְכְּרֵכִי אִם לֹא אַעֲלֶה אֶת יְרוּשָׁלִַם עַל רֹאשׁ שִׂמְחָתִי.
If I forget you, O Jerusalem, may my right hand forget its skill. May my tongue cleave to my palate if I do not remember you, if I do not raise Jerusalem above my chiefest joy.
Tehillim 137:5–6

From this pasuk the Gemara derives a foundational principle: at every moment of joy — even the greatest simchah of one’s life, the night of a chasunah (חתונה) — ashes are placed upon the head of the chasan (חתן), as an expression of the phrase “al rosh simchasi” (על ראש שמחתי).

It is not merely a token. There is no real simchah any longer. We once had real simchah; perhaps our senses are too dulled to know what we are missing. But at the very least we should know how a Jew is supposed to think. The Jews of Bavel refused meat and wine because the meat in their mouths lacked the salt of kedushah. There is no true joy in pure materialism: pleasure to the senses, yes, but devoid of meaning. That is why a chasan wears ashes: “In truth, I do not have complete joy. This is not the wholeness of simchah we had in the days of the Beis HaMikdash.”

“Tidbak leshoni l’chikki” (תדבק לשוני לחכי) — let my tongue cleave to the roof of my mouth if I do not remember Yerushalayim above my highest joy. One may sing a beautiful song for Yerushalayim beneath the chuppah (חופה); what matters is the thought at that moment: that we are missing the real joy. We may be too deep in olam hazeh to yearn for it truly. But we should at least know what we are supposed to yearn for.

By the logic of the Malbim the pesukim fit precisely: the more one enjoys Bavel, the more one engages with the music and dancing of the nations — beckoning, “come and join us; you are no longer the nation of HaKadosh Baruch Hu; you are like all other nations” — the more one loses the ability to weep, the capacity to yearn for real things.

X. Pasuk Zayin — Edom on the Sidelines

זְכֹר ה׳ לִבְנֵי אֱדוֹם אֵת יוֹם יְרוּשָׁלִָם הָאֹמְרִים עֱרוּ עֱרוּ עַד הַיְסוֹד בָּהּ.
Remember, Hashem, against the children of Edom the day of Jerusalem, those who said: “Raze it, raze it, to its very foundation!”
Tehillim 137:7

An odd shift: the perek speaks of Bavel, the first galus; Edom (אדום) is the last, hundreds of years later. Why does Dovid HaMelech fast-forward to Edom?

Two Approaches

  • The Midrash: during the churban of the Bayis Rishon, the Babylonians destroyed, while the Edomites stood on the sidelines, cheering and jeering, urging Bavel: “Do not stop! Burn it to the ground! Aru, aru, ad hayesod bah!” Dovid HaMelech pleads: “HaKadosh Baruch Hu, remember that they laughed and encouraged Bavel to make it worse.” The Malbim likewise reads Edom as those who aided the destruction.
  • An alternate reading: Dovid HaMelech refers forward to the churban Bayis Sheni, when Edom acted similarly.

The Deeper Peshat

By an extended reading, which is not the Malbim’s own but which the shiur adopts in preference to the historical approaches: when a Jew lives immersed in olam hazeh, when he sells himself to play the music of the nations — though Yerushalayim still lies desolate, the moment a Jew declares “I do not care; let me enjoy olam hazeh” — at that moment “aru, aru, ad hayesod bah”: the very foundations of the Beis HaMikdash have been uprooted from within him.

And conversely: if a Jew can preserve his Jewish pride — “I do not have the Beis HaMikdash now, but I know what real joy is; I know what shiras Hashem is” — then Edom did not succeed in uprooting the foundations. The Jews of Bavel were zocheh (זוכה): though Edom screamed for the yesod (יסוד) to be torn out, the yesod remained deep in their hearts; the nations could not extract it.

XI. Pesukim Ches–Tes — The Harsh Conclusion

בַּת בָּבֶל הַשְּׁדוּדָה אַשְׁרֵי שֶׁיְשַׁלֶּם לָךְ אֶת גְּמוּלֵךְ שֶׁגָּמַלְתְּ לָּנוּ. אַשְׁרֵי שֶׁיֹּאחֵז וְנִפֵּץ אֶת עֹלָלַיִךְ אֶל הַסָּלַע.
Daughter of Babylon, the plundered one, happy is he who repays you according to your deed, which you did to us. Happy is he who seizes and dashes your infants against the rock.
Tehillim 137:8–9

The perek ends with two of the harshest pesukim in Tanach (תנ״ך). “Daughter of Bavel, the destroyed one, fortunate is the one who repays you for what you did to us. Fortunate is the one who seizes your children and smashes them against the rocks.”

What has become of us? Are we a brutal nation? Do we wish to take Babylonian babies and smash their heads against rocks? Is this the conclusion the perek wants — a searing image of murder? What an image, seared into the mind: people taking Babylonian babies — and here the speaker adds, explicitly offering it as a mashal, “Palestinian babies”[13] — and smashing them against the rocks. Is that what a Jew is?

As a child, the speaker confesses, he never understood this pasuk. The Malbim resolves it in one word.

The Meaning of “Gemul”

What does gemul (גמול) mean? When one pays for what was done to him, that is sachar (שכר) — reward. Gemul reflects something deeper: receiving back not only what one deserves, but in the very manner in which it was done. The Malbim defines gemul as an act arising from the passion of love or of hatred; what Bavel did to Israel came from hatred, and so it will be repaid with hatred.[3]

The manner in which the Babylonians made Israel suffer — the monstrosities, the unspeakable acts: what did they do? They took Israel’s babies and smashed them against the rocks. One may read Midrash Eichah for what was done to women and children then, and the same record across the generations of the enemies of Israel.

The pasuk does not say “we want this to happen to them.” The pasuk says: by the way, you should know that the only true justice for what you did, the only justice possible to repay a nation that committed such atrocities, is the very same. Gemul. When one brings such animal behavior into the world, in the end it turns back upon him. The perek does not glorify the smashing; it reports the metziyus (מציאות).

XII. The Lesson — What to Read

The Malbim, it may be, understands the harsh ending differently — not so harsh after all.

The next time one faces a nisayon (נסיון), when one struggles and wants more olam hazeh, when one is tempted to dance to the tunes of the goy — the next time one sits with him over a cocktail and he says, “Come, sit, relax, enjoy. You are an American citizen — perhaps a Jew second. Eretz Yisrael is the past; that is over” — at that moment, do this:

Open Perfidy.[14] Have you read Perfidy? Read it every year. If one lacks the patience, one might watch Schindler’s List — though, on reflection, perhaps not. Take out a book about the Shoah. Take out a book about Simchas Torah of two years ago.

Read what they did. Then think twice before eating from that hand — for that is what they represent, and Israel represents the exact opposite. We are kedushah, dveikus, the nation of HaKadosh Baruch Hu. We represent the Beis HaMikdash filled with Torah and avodah.

The Jew’s Portfolio

A Jew carries a portfolio: Torah, avodah, meaning, olam haba (עולם הבא), sachar v’onesh (שכר ועונש), Gan Eden (גן עדן), Gehinnom (גיהנום) — an entire world. The Babylonians, the nations, the enemies of Israel represent the antithesis, and they want to wrestle that portfolio out of Israel’s hands.

Remember: they smashed our babies against the rocks. Does one wish to join the people who smashed his babies against rocks, who vanquished ruchnius, who destroyed the Beis HaMikdash, who could not tolerate the very word “Jew,” because the existence of a Jew as ambassador of the Divine in this world drove them mad?

Perhaps that is why Dovid HaMelech closes the perek with that note. The next time one is tempted, let him remember “bas Bavel hashedudah” (בת בבל השדודה): at the end of days they will be repaid with the exact passion and hatred with which they punished Israel across galus. Let him think of what they did to Jews. And then ask: do I wish to go to that side, or do I wish to remain like the Jew in Bavel, gazing at the silent harp upon the willow, waiting for the day that Mashiach (משיח) comes and we play shir Hashem again?

XIII. Closing

May we be zocheh this Tishah B’Av — at the very least — to adopt this mindset. We are Jews. We are higher than olam hazeh. We are olam haba’dike Jews (עולם הבא’דיקע יהודים); we are Yerushalayim’dike Jews (ירושלים’דיקע יהודים); we are shir Hashem’dike Jews (שיר ה׳’דיקע יהודים) — that is, Jews of the world to come, of Yerushalayim, of shir Hashem. Before we run to olam hazeh, before we join the goy, let us think of that.

May HaKadosh Baruch Hu bring us the geulah shleimah (גאולה שלמה) speedily in our days. Amen.

  12. Gemara, Bava Basra 60b — abstaining from meat and wine after the churban; Rabbi Yehoshua’s reply; the ashes on the chasan. — Verified against Sefaria.
