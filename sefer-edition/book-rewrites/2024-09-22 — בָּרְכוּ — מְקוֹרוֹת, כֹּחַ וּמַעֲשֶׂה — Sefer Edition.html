בָּרְכוּ — מְקוֹרוֹת, כֹּחַ וּמַעֲשֶׂה

Barchu: Origins, Power, and Practice
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Sunday Morning Series on Tefillah
Recorded 22 September 2024  ·  Sefer Edition · Revised 23 August 2026

L’ilui nishmas Rachel bas Shloima — that her memory be bound up in the daily Barchu of every Jew who reads these pages and answers, from that day forward, with the full voice that Klal Yisrael has been trained for nineteen hundred years to give.

I. Where Barchu Belongs — And Why the Question Is Difficult

Every morning, immediately before Yotzer Or (יוֹצֵר אוֹר), the shliach tzibbur calls out Barchu es Hashem ha’mevorach, and the tzibbur answers Baruch Hashem ha’mevorach l’olam va’ed. The practice is universal — and, at first inspection, halakhically underdetermined. The Talmud’s discussion of Barchu in Maseches Berachos[1] is framed almost entirely around Birchas HaTorah: the call-and-response spoken when one receives an aliyah at Kriyas HaTorah. The use of Barchu as a preface to Yotzer Or is nowhere on the surface of the sugya.

This gap between Talmudic silence and universal practice is not incidental. It is the halakhic puzzle that shapes the entire discussion that follows. Two possibilities stand before us. Either Barchu at Kriyas HaTorah and Barchu before Yotzer Or constitute a single institution, in which case the Talmud’s treatment of one context is meant to govern the other by extension; or they are two distinct institutions that happen to share a formula, in which case the second requires its own foundation. The tradition’s answer is subtle: they are one institution in logic — both are communal divrei shebikedushah requiring a minyan, both invite the community to a brachah, both operate on the same halakhic mechanism — but the pre-Yotzer Or use requires an independent textual anchor because the Talmud does not supply one.

Maseches Sofrim as the Anchor

That anchor is Maseches Sofrim,[2] one of the masechtos ketanos appended to Maseches Avodah Zarah in the standard Vilna Shas. Though the compilation itself is Amoraic, the material it preserves is largely Tannaitic: Maseches Sofrim records explicitly that the shliach tzibbur calls out Barchu before beginning Yotzer Or u’Vorei Choshech. The masechta’s authority sufficed, from the era of the Geonim onward, to establish the practice across Klal Yisrael. Where the Talmud is silent, an extra-Talmudic Tannaitic source could speak — and did.

Once anchored, the dual function of Barchu discloses a common inner logic. Whether the call precedes an aliyah or the brachos of Kriyas Shema, its structural work is the same: the leader summons the community to a brachah; the community, through its response, ratifies the summons and enters the act of communal blessing. This is why both contexts require a minyan, and this is why both are governed by the halakhic architecture of shomea k’oneh.

II. The Zohar HaKadosh — The Neshamah and Its Five Levels

To the halakhic account the Zohar HaKadosh[3] adds a further dimension. When a Jew wakes in the morning and says Modeh Ani, the neshamah is restored to the body — but only partially. The Zohar teaches that the full integration of the neshamah unfolds by stages, and that these stages correspond to the structured progression of the morning tefillah.

The neshamah, in the language of the mekubalim, comprises five ascending levels:

  1. Nefesh (נֶפֶשׁ) — the vital soul, seated in the blood and the body’s animate life.
  2. Ruach (רוּחַ) — the spirit of moral and emotional character.
  3. Neshamah (נְשָׁמָה) proper — the intellectual soul, the seat of understanding.
  4. Chayah (חַיָּה) — the transcendent life-principle by which the soul reaches beyond the body.
  5. Yechidah (יְחִידָה) — the innermost point of union with the Divine.

Each level is drawn into the body by a progressive stage of davening. Nefesh is present already in Modeh Ani and the Birchos HaShachar; Ruach is engaged in the Korbanos; Neshamah enters through Pesukei d’Zimra. At Barchu, according to this teaching, the fourth level — Chayah — is drawn down; and by the time the tzibbur reaches Shemoneh Esrei, Yechidah stands revealed. The five words of the response — Baruch / Hashem / ha’mevorach / l’olam / va’ed — are read by the mekubalim as mirroring these five ascending strata, so that the moment of communal response completes an internal ascent that the individual has been undergoing since waking.

Whether or not one traces the correspondence word by word, the essential teaching is this: Barchu is the halakhic hinge on which the neshamah is fully seated within the body. It is not merely a communal call; it is a rite of integration. To answer Baruch Hashem ha’mevorach l’olam va’ed with kavvanah is, on the Zohar’s reading, to complete the daily work of becoming, in one’s own person, what a Jew is meant to be.

III. The Power of the Response — A Teaching of the Tanna D’Bei Eliyahu

That the response to Barchu carries spiritual force of the highest order is taught most vividly in the Tanna D’Bei Eliyahu[4] — a work traditionally attributed to the daily chavrusa of Rav Anan with Eliyahu HaNavi. There it is recorded that Rabbi Yochanan ben Zakkai, walking along the road, encountered a man gathering wood. He greeted him with Shalom aleichem; the man returned no greeting. After a moment he explained: “I cannot return your greeting — I am not among the living. I am from the dead.”

Rabbi Yochanan asked what use a dead man could have for wood. The man answered:

“My friend and I committed aveiros in our lifetimes. The beis din of Heaven decreed sereifah upon us. This is the arrangement: each day I gather wood for my own burning while my friend burns. The next day, my friend gathers wood for his own burning while I burn. So it continues, day after day, without end.”

Rabbi Yochanan asked whether any path of escape remained. The man answered that there was one, and that he required Rabbi Yochanan’s help to walk it:

“When I died, my wife was pregnant. I know — by the knowledge granted to those in the next world — that the child will be a boy. When he turns five, take him to cheder and teach him Chumash. Above all, train him that when the tzibbur says Barchu, he answer Baruch Hashem ha’mevorach l’olam va’ed aloud and clearly. At that moment — when his voice rises with the communal answer — the malachim who hold my punishment will release me.”

The teaching that emerges from this account is not incidental. Barchu, in this account, carries the same order of koach as Kaddish: the power of communal kedushah to elevate a neshamah even from the depths of Gehinnom. The Talmud in Chagigah and Rosh Hashanah[5] describes the twelve months of judgment appointed for serious resha’im; the tradition’s corollary is that within that period, communal divrei shebikedushah — Kaddish, Kedushah, and Barchu alike — retain the power to secure reprieve. What is widely known of Kaddish holds, quietly, of Barchu as well. There is scarcely a phrase a mourner utters, in the entire course of a year, whose power to elevate a departed neshamah exceeds the answer given clearly to the morning Barchu.

IV. The Halakhic Foundation — “Ki Sheim Hashem Ekra”

The biblical foundation of the entire structure of communal call-and-response is a verse in Devarim:[6]

כִּי שֵׁם ה' אֶקְרָא, הָבוּ גֹדֶל לֵא־לֹקֵינוּ.
“For I will call out the name of Hashem; ascribe greatness to our God.”
Devarim 32:3

Chazal read this verse as the Pentateuchal model of communal tefillah: Moshe Rabbeinu calls; the tzibbur ascribes greatness in response. From this model derives the halakhic character of every davar shebikedushah — Kaddish, Kedushah, and Barchu among them. All are communal by their essential structure, all require a minyan of ten, and all depend upon the double motion of call and response.

V. The Sefer Chasidim — The Robbery of Kedushah

Because Barchu is a communal davar shebikedushah, its structural integrity depends on a single condition: the tzibbur must actually hear the call. It is not enough for the summons to be uttered; the community whose response completes the act must be able to receive it.

The Sefer Chasidim[7] rebukes with unusual severity the widespread phenomenon of bar mitzvah boys and reserved adults who, receiving an aliyah for the first time, mumble Barchu es Hashem ha’mevorach too softly to be heard. The Sefer Chasidim assigns to this failure a striking legal name: gozel kedushah (גּוֹזֵל קְדוּשָׁה) — robbing the community of its kedushah. If the tzibbur cannot hear the call, they cannot answer; and if they cannot answer, the entire architecture of communal davar shebikedushah collapses. The mumbler has not merely failed to fulfill his own obligation; he has deprived every other member of the community of the response they came prepared to give.

The Mishnah Berurah and Biur Halacha[8] codify the Sefer Chasidim’s ruling and extend it: it is a mitzvah to actively guide and correct one who says Barchu too softly, even at the cost of embarrassment to the person being corrected. The Chofetz Chaim is unambiguous on this priority — the kedushah of the tzibbur takes precedence over the discomfort of the individual whose voice must be raised. Fathers and rebbeim bear a particular responsibility to prepare their sons and talmidim to declare the words audibly from the first aliyah of their lives.

Hearing the Initiator, Not the Crowd

The poskim further rule that to be yotzei the response to Barchu, one must actually hear the shliach tzibbur or the oleh — not merely the answering voices of the tzibbur from those closer to the bimah. The principle of shomea k’oneh requires shemiah from the originator; if the call itself was inaudible, hearing others’ responses does not permit one to join. One does not, in the pithy image, “jump aboard” the tzibbur’s answer.

VI. The Machlokes of R’ Akiva and R’ Yishmael

The Talmud[9] records a fundamental machlokes between the two great Tannaim of their era on the exact nusach of the call:

  • R’ Akiva: the leader calls only Barchu es Hashem, without the word ha’mevorach. The tzibbur then responds Baruch Hashem ha’mevorach l’olam va’ed.
  • R’ Yishmael: the leader says the full phrase, Barchu es Hashem ha’mevorach; the tzibbur responds identically.

R’ Akiva and R’ Yishmael are the canonical pair of Tannaim whose disputes span the whole of the Mishnah; despite close friendship, they diverged on nearly every halakhic point, and this machlokes concerning Barchu is characteristic of the deeper divergences in their approach.

Rav Acha bar Papa at Abu Jobar

The machlokes came to a memorable head in a scene the Talmud records with unusual dramatic detail. Rav Acha bar Papa — one of the ten sons of Rav Papa, all of them talmidei chachamim whose Yehi Ratzon is recited to this day at the conclusion of a masechta — arrived at the shul of Abu Jobar, on the outskirts of Damascus. He received an aliyah and called out Barchu es Hashem without the word ha’mevorach, following R’ Akiva.

The beis midrash erupted. Where is “ha’mevorach”?! The Talmud records that Rava was present and intervened, addressing Rav Acha bar Papa with a curious epithet: pas yochmo (פַּס יוּחְמָא). The Rishonim divide over its meaning.[10] Rashi and Tosfos advance competing readings, some hearing rebuke in the phrase, others hearing praise. The most striking of the positive readings takes pas yochmo as “the blackened bottom of a pot” — the image of a talmid chacham so long over the fire of Torah that his very skin has been darkened by it. Whatever the tone, the operative ruling was clear: do not insist on R’ Akiva’s nusach when the community is offended by its brevity. From that day the universal practice has followed R’ Yishmael, and the shliach tzibbur the world over calls out the full Barchu es Hashem ha’mevorach.

The Bach’s Grammatical Insight

The Bach[11] raises a subtle but consequential difficulty. The word ha’mevorach is grammatically a passive participle — “who is (or has been) blessed” — and can be read in a past-tense register. If the shliach tzibbur merely calls out and does not himself repeat the response, he risks standing outside the present-tense act of blessing that his own call has occasioned. To ensure his personal participation in the current brachah, the Bach rules that he repeat Baruch Hashem ha’mevorach l’olam va’ed together with the tzibbur. This practice is now universal; the chazzan’s repetition, sometimes puzzled over, is grounded in this precise grammatical consideration.

VII. The Beis Knesses of Abu Jobar

The shul in which Rav Acha bar Papa davened is itself among the most remarkable structures in the geography of Klal Yisrael.[12] Abu Jobar stood just beyond the walls of Damascus — today it lies within modern Damascus — and in its earlier centuries was a distinct Jewish village. It was active throughout the era of the Amoraim; the Talmud references it many times as Beis Kenishta d’Abu Jobar, and significant halakhic decisions were rendered within its walls. Rav Papa himself is known to have davened there, and a Syrian tradition holds that his kever lies nearby.

The Eliyahu HaNavi Tradition

Beyond what the Talmud itself attests, the site carries a body of mesorah reaching considerably further back — and here the register of the discussion shifts from documented halakhic history to received local tradition. Syrian Jewish tradition holds that a stone within the shul bore an inscription tracing the site to the days of Eliyahu HaNavi, roughly 2,700 years ago, predating the era of the Amoraim by centuries. On this tradition, the events described in Sefer Melachim — Eliyahu hiding from persecution, sustained by bread and water carried by ravens — are placed at this very location. A cave (מְעָרָה) on the property was identified as Eliyahu’s hiding place, and the same site is held to be where Eliyahu anointed Elisha ben Shafat to the office of navi. Syrian tradition further holds that the Tanna R’ Elazar ben Aroch — the disciple of Rabban Yochanan ben Zakkai — built or restored the shul. These claims belong to mesorah rather than to documented history; they are honored, and worth preserving, precisely as tradition preserves them. By the era of Rav Papa, on any reckoning, the building had already stood for many centuries.

The shul was destroyed by bombing during Syrian instability in recent decades. Its memory has been preserved in a body of documentation gathered from Damascene Jews before their flight — interviews, photographs, and architectural sketches from those who prayed within its walls in living memory.

The Abu Chatzeira Family and the Damascus Courtyard

The shul retained its spiritual significance for many centuries after the Amoraim. The Chida, in his Shem HaGedolim[13] — the encyclopedic biographical work covering gedolim from the Tannaim through his own generation — records the origin of the celebrated Abu Chatzeira family of Moroccan tzaddikim. The patriarch of the family, the Chida records, was not the Abir Yaakov (R’ Yaakov Abu Chatzeira), as popular attribution now assumes, but an earlier tzaddik: R’ Shmuel Elbaz, who lived some four centuries ago in Damascus and spent his days and nights in the chatzer — the courtyard — of the Abu Jobar shul, in continuous learning and meditation.

The Arabic Abu means “father”; chatzir derives from the courtyard in which R’ Shmuel Elbaz was to be found. Thus Abu-Chatzeira: “the father of the courtyard.” The family later relocated to Morocco, where its descendants became the great Moroccan dynasty that Sephardic Jewry continues to revere; the Abir Yaakov and, in our own day, R’ Dan Abuchatzeira and his brothers descend from this line. The etymology, restoring the true founder, links the entire family through eleven generations to the courtyard of the shul where R’ Acha bar Papa once received his aliyah.

VIII. Baruch Hu u’Baruch Shemo — The Ruling of the Maharam of Rothenburg

When the shliach tzibbur pronounces the Name of Hashem in the course of any brachah, the tzibbur customarily interjects Baruch Hu u’Baruch Shemo — “Blessed is He, and blessed is His Name.” The origin of this practice is preserved in the Tashbatz of R’ Shimshon ben Tzaddok,[14] the talmid who came each day to the prison window at Ensisheim Fortress to record the rulings and chiddushim of the Maharam of Rothenburg. The setting of the ruling is inseparable from its content.

In medieval Ashkenaz, very few Jews could daven independently. Siddurim were rare and costly, produced by hand; a great portion of the kehillah consisted of amei ha’aretz who could recite little more than fragments from memory. Communal structures emerged to make full tefillah available to all:

  • For Shemoneh Esrei, the institution of chazaras ha’shatz — the repetition aloud by the shliach tzibbur — enabled him to be motzi those who could not say the tefillah themselves.
  • For Birchos Kriyas Shema, no formal chazarah exists; the shliach tzibbur instead recited the brachos aloud so that the tzibbur could fulfill its obligation through shomea k’oneh.

For shomea k’oneh to function, the listener must actively receive the words of the speaker. The Maharam explained that when the shliach tzibbur was about to be motzi the tzibbur, he needed to alert them: listen — I am about to bless Hashem; be ready to be yotzei through my words. The response Baruch Hu u’Baruch Shemo was the tzibbur’s acknowledgment: we are with you; we are prepared to be yotzei. The interjection served as a live coordination mechanism, ensuring that speaker and listener were aligned at the very moment of the brachah.

In the present age, virtually every Jew possesses his own siddur and recites Birchos Kriyas Shema independently. The original functional purpose of Baruch Hu u’Baruch Shemo has largely lapsed. Yet the phrase has remained embedded in minhag, now serving as a communal affirmation of kavod shamayim at every mention of Hashem’s Name. That its origin has passed into disuse does not diminish its present function; on the contrary, it has become a kind of standing testimony to the era in which it was born.

Whether to Answer Amen After Baruch Hu u’Baruch Shemo

In certain Hasidic communities — Hungarian, Satmar, and others — the tzibbur answers amen after the chazzan’s repetition of Baruch Hu u’Baruch Shemo. The Sha’arei Ephraim[15] of R’ Ephraim Zalman Margolius — whose sefer remains the standard halakhic reference on the laws of Kriyas HaTorah in many kehillos — rules that there is no halakhic ground for such an amen; the tzibbur has already responded with the phrase itself, and the addition serves no further function. The Mishnah Berurah notes that those who do answer have authorities upon whom to rely, but records that most kehillos follow the Sha’arei Ephraim and refrain.

IX. Barchu After Maariv — The Machlokes of the Ritva and the Rema

A widespread minhag in many shuls: after Maariv, the chazzan calls out Barchu a second time, so that any who arrived after the original Barchu at the outset of Maariv may be yotzei.

The Ritva, in his commentary on Maseches Megillah,[16] objects with force, citing his own rebbe. Barchu, on the Ritva’s reading, is by its very nature an invitation to a brachah that follows immediately upon it. To call out Barchu es Hashem ha’mevorach when no brachah is to follow is halakhically incoherent: the tzibbur’s response is not actually functioning as the answer to a valid brachah-invitation, and the ritual gesture is emptied of its inner content.

The Rema defends the minhag, and his defense rests on a substantive theological ground. Baruch Hashem ha’mevorach l’olam va’ed, on the Rema’s reading, carries an independent kedushah — a status as a davar shebikedushah in its own right, comparable to Kedushah and to Kaddish. The Ritva has assumed that Barchu is only a preface, functioning by virtue of what follows; the Rema replies that Barchu is itself an act of communal declaration whose sanctity does not depend upon a subsequent brachah. On this reading, the post-Maariv Barchu is not a defective preface but a complete davar shebikedushah in miniature, and the tzibbur’s answer stands on its own.

The machlokes is not fully resolved. In many communities the practice is preserved universally; in others, only on Friday night, on the historical rationale that the tzibbur often arrived late from the mikveh; in still others, not at all. In each case one follows one’s mesorah; and in a shul where the minhag was never practiced, the Ritva’s objection provides sufficient ground not to introduce it.

X. The Maharam of Rothenburg — The Prison Cell and the Siddur

The threads that have run through the last several chapters converge upon a single figure. The Maharam of Rothenburg has now appeared three times in the shaping of the morning tefillah. He is the author of the closing Baruch Hashem L’olam cluster, instituted to seal Pesukei d’Zimra when forced interruptions during the German persecutions rendered the brachah-frame structurally vulnerable. He is the author of the halakhic explanation of Baruch Hu u’Baruch Shemo, dictated to the Tashbatz at the Ensisheim prison window and preserved in Klal Yisrael’s minhag to this day. And he is the central Rishon through whom the entire Ashkenazic tradition of the siddur has descended.

The setting of these rulings is inseparable from their weight. The Maharam was seized by Rudolf I of Habsburg in 1286 and confined at the fortress of Ensisheim in Alsace, where he remained for the last seven years of his life. He refused, from within the cell, to permit any Jewish community to pay the ransom his captors demanded — understanding that any such payment would establish a precedent for the perpetual extortion of Jewish leadership. He died in captivity in 1293. His body was held for a further fourteen years, until a Frankfurt Jew named Alexander Wimpen paid the ransom personally for the release of the body alone. The Maharam is buried in Vermayza (Worms), beside Alexander Wimpen, at Wimpen’s explicit request.

The Tashbatz visited him daily at the prison window throughout the years of his captivity. Significant portions of the siddur as we now know it were formulated in that cell — by a talmid chacham without sefarim, without books, without the ordinary tools of pesak, who refused his own freedom rather than let his person become a lever against Klal Yisrael. Every morning, when a Jew answers Baruch Hashem ha’mevorach l’olam va’ed and interjects Baruch Hu u’Baruch Shemo at the mention of the Name, he speaks words shaped by the ruling of a man whose kever in Vermayza marks the resting place of perhaps the single most influential figure in the construction of the Ashkenazic siddur.[17]

The two words Barchu es Hashem ha’mevorach compress into a single moment of communal tefillah the whole of what has been laid out in these chapters: a Tannaitic machlokes resolved by minhag at Abu Jobar; a Kabbalistic elevation of the neshamah through its five levels; a midrashic redemption of a neshamah from Gehinnom; a Geonic-era shul outside Damascus whose courtyard produced a Moroccan dynasty; the medieval German redifus that made a prison cell the workshop of the siddur; and a halakhic structure of shomea k’oneh that binds the shliach tzibbur in a live relation to every member of the tzibbur. None of it is to be mumbled. The call is the call; the response, said clearly and audibly with the full tzibbur, is the kedushah that Klal Yisrael has been trained for nineteen hundred years to deliver.

The shul was bombed in recent decades during Syrian instability and is documented in works based on interviews and photographs from Damascene Jews collected before their flight.

Chida (R’ Chaim Yosef David Azulai), Shem HaGedolim, entry on R’ Shmuel Elbaz. — The Chida records that the patriarch of the Abu Chatzeira family was R’ Shmuel Elbaz, who lived c. 400 years ago in Damascus and spent his days and nights in the chatzer — the courtyard — of the Abu Jobar shul. The Arabic Abu (“father”) + chatzir (from the courtyard) yields Abu-Chatzeira. The family later relocated to Morocco; the Abir Yaakov, popularly assumed to be the founder, is a later descendant of R’ Shmuel Elbaz. ↩
  14. R’ Shimshon ben Tzaddok, Tashbatz (also Tashbetz Kattan), siman on Baruch Hu u’Baruch Shemo. — The explanation of the Maharam of Rothenburg, dictated to the Tashbatz at the prison window at Ensisheim: in an era when most Jews could not daven independently, the shliach tzibbur was motzi the tzibbur through shomea k’oneh; Baruch Hu u’Baruch Shemo was their acknowledgment of readiness. In the present era, the underlying need has largely lapsed; the phrase persists as a communal affirmation of kavod shamayim at the mention of the Name. ↩
  15. R’ Ephraim Zalman Margolius, Sha’arei Ephraim; cf. Mishnah Berurah ad loc. — No halakhic ground for answering amen after the chazzan’s repetition of Baruch Hu u’Baruch Shemo: the tzibbur has already responded with the phrase itself. The Mishnah Berurah notes that those who do so have authorities on whom to rely, but records that most kehillos follow the Sha’arei Ephraim and refrain. R’ Ephraim Zalman Margolius (early nineteenth century) was an Acharon of major stature and one of the wealthiest Jews of his generation — the rare union of Torah u’gedulah b’makom echad. All of his sefarim bear his name in their titles: Sha’arei Ephraim, Beis Ephraim, Yad Ephraim. The Sha’arei Ephraim remains the standard halakhic reference for the laws of Kriyas HaTorah. ↩
  16. Ritva, Megillah 23b; Rema, Orach Chaim 133:1 and 236 (defense of the minhag). — The Ritva’s structural objection: Barchu is by nature an invitation to a following brachah; calling out without a following brachah is halakhically incoherent. The Rema’s substantive defense: Baruch Hashem ha’mevorach l’olam va’ed carries independent kedushah comparable to Kaddish and Kedushah, not solely a functional preface. On the Rema’s view, the post-Maariv Barchu is a complete davar shebikedushah in its own right. Many communities preserve the practice especially for Friday night, on the rationale that the tzibbur often arrived late from the mikveh. Practical: follow one’s mesorah. ↩
  17. Cross-reference and synthesis: The Maharam of Rothenburg recurs across multiple sections of Pesukei d’Zimra and Birchos Kriyas Shema: Baruch Hashem L’olam (sealing PDZ under persecution); Baruch Hu u’Baruch Shemo (the coordination mechanism for shomea k’oneh); and the broader transmission of Ashkenazic halakhah in the siddur. Significant portions of the siddur as we now use it were formulated in the Ensisheim prison cell, where the Tashbatz visited him daily. The Maharam died in captivity in 1293 after refusing to permit any ransom; his body was released only fourteen years later, at the personal expense of Alexander Wimpen of Frankfurt, who is buried beside him in Vermayza (Worms) at his own request. See the shiur of AUDIO-2024-09-01 for the full biographical treatment of the Maharam.
