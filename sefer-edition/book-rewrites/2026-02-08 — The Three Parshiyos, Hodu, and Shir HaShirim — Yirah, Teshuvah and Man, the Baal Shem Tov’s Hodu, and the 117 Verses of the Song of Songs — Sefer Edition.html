The Three Parshiyos, Hodu, and Shir HaShirim: Yirah, Teshuvah and Man, the Baal Shem Tov’s Hodu, and the 117 Verses of the Song of Songs
Studies in Tefillah (תפילה) — The Close of Shacharis (שחרית) and the Opening of Kabbalas Shabbos (קבלת שבת)
21 Shevat 5786 · 8 February 2026 · Sefer Edition
In memory of Reb Yosef Nachman ben Moshe Zelig (ר’ יוסף נחמן בן משה זליג), of blessed memory (ז״ל)

This discourse brings the long road through the weekday morning service to its quiet end and opens the study of the Shabbos prayers. It was given on Sunday morning, 8 February 2026, and was dedicated to the memory of Reb Yosef Nachman ben Moshe Zelig (ר’ יוסף נחמן בן משה זליג), a founding member of the congregation, whose funeral was to follow that morning.

It treats the three short passages that some say at the end of Shacharis (שחרית): Parshas HaYirah (פרשת היראה), Parshas HaTeshuvah (פרשת התשובה) and Parshas HaMan (פרשת המן), with the reasons given for them and the segulah (סגולה) for parnassah (פרנסה) attached to the last; then Hodu (הודו) (תהלים ק״ז), which opens Mincha (מנחה) of Erev Shabbos (ערב שבת) in Nusach Sefard (נוסח ספרד), and the tradition that traces it to the rescue of the Baal Shem Tov (בעל שם טוב) at sea; and finally the custom of saying Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים) before Shabbos (שבת), its standing as the Kodesh Kodashim (קודש קדשים), the view of the Alshich (אלשיך) that it is wholly sod (סוד), and the two calculations of Rabbi Eliezer Rokeach (רבי אלעזר רוקח) by which its 117 verses answer the 117 hours of Gehinnom (גהינום) in an ordinary week and the 117 lashes of King Shlomo (שלמה).

The edition follows the order of the discourse. What the lecturer reports from books that could not be checked is recorded as his report, and the items still to be settled are gathered under Open Questions at the end.

I. In Memory of a Founding Member

The Dedication

The learning of the morning was dedicated to the soul of Reb Yosef Nachman ben Moshe Zelig (ר’ יוסף נחמן בן משה זליג). The lecturer began by saying that all present had known Reb Yossi better than he had, and that his passing is a great loss for the shul. Reb Yossi was one of the founding members, together with Yossi Schwartz and Lloyd Kilsen, who hired the congregation’s Rav (רב), of blessed memory (זצ״ל). Any Torah (תורה) that anyone present had learned from the Rav (רב) over the past three decades, the lecturer said, was a zechus (זכות) for those who learned it and a zechus (זכות) for Reb Yossi.

He was faithful to this shiur (שיעור), sitting beside Rabbi Ari every Sunday morning when he was able, even through the last hard months of his illness; and on Shabbos (שבת) morning, at the Chumash (חומש) class with Rashi (רש״י), which only those who attended know of, he was the first one there. The lecturer said that he would not eulogize, since the Rebbetzin (רבנית) had asked that only the family do so, and added one word on how much he had valued him. Reb Yossi could have been his zeide (זיידע), or his elter zeide (עלטער זיידע), and yet he was so respectful that he would call with questions of halachah (הלכה) to which, as a fine talmid chacham (תלמיד חכם) and a good Jew, he often knew the answer himself, saying: “But I have to ask the Rav (רב).”

What Remains

The Gemara (גמרא) teaches that when one member of a chaburah (חבורה) dies, the whole chaburah (חבורה) should be concerned (אחד מבני חבורה שמת, ידאגו כל החבורה).[1] The lecturer drew the lesson that when Hakadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא) one day separates the neshamah (נשמה) from the body, all that remains are the mitzvos (מצוות), the Torah (תורה) and the chesed (חסד) that the person did. In going that morning to the funeral, the levayas hames (לויית המת), those present should have in mind the mitzvah (מצוה) of chesed shel emes (חסד של אמת). He prayed that Reb Yossi’s neshamah (נשמה) ascend and be a melitz yosher (מליץ יושר) for all of them, and said that he was sure there was a beautiful meeting in Gan Eden (גן עדן) between Reb Yossi and the Rav (רב), zatzal (זצ״ל).

II. Closing the Long Road of Shacharis

The lecturer observed that the congregation had come to the end of Shacharis (שחרית). Two years earlier the study had begun with Modeh Ani (מודה אני), and it had now reached the end of the service. He said that he was excited to embark, im yirtzeh Hashem (אם ירצה השם), on a new journey for as long as it should take: at the suggestion of the congregation, the study of the Shabbos prayers, their history and where they come from. He also offered a mazel tov (מזל טוב) to Reb Shoali, whose son Yoni had become engaged the week before.

First, however, he wished to finish the seder hatefillah (סדר התפילה) as it stands in the siddur (סידור), after what had been discussed the week before. At the end of the weekday morning service there are three parshiyos (פרשיות) that are printed in all siddurim (סידורים) today; some say them and some do not, and perhaps the study would inspire some to say them: Parshas HaTeshuvah (פרשת התשובה), Parshas HaYirah (פרשת היראה) and Parshas HaMan (פרשת המן). He took them one by one. (He remarked that in the siddurim (סידורים) the order is first Teshuvah (תשובה) and then Yirah (יראה), and that he had forgotten his siddur (סידור) that morning: “How can one give a shiur (שיעור) on the siddur (סידור) without bringing a siddur (סידור)?”)

III. Parshas HaYirah

The Source

Rabbeinu Yonah (רבינו יונה), the author of the great Shaarei Teshuvah (שערי תשובה), also wrote a small, tiny book called Sefer HaYirah (ספר היראה), in which he inspires a Jew to ascend the levels of yiras shamayim (יראת שמים).[2] He writes there that there is an inyan (ענין) in saying every single day Parshas HaYirah (פרשת היראה), which is printed in the back of the siddur (סידור). It is the passage in Sefer Devarim (ספר דברים) in which Hakadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא) asks what He requires of Israel, “And now, Israel, what does Hashem your God ask of you, but to fear Hashem your God” (ועתה ישראל מה ה’ אלהיך שואל מעמך כי אם ליראה), and then goes on to speak of the importance of yiras shamayim (יראת שמים). Rabbeinu Yonah (רבינו יונה) writes that saying it daily is a segulah (סגולה) for yiras shamayim (יראת שמים), and indeed some say it; many do so because it is in the siddur (סידור), saying whatever it says there.

The Practice of the Chacham Tzvi

The siddur (סידור) of the Yaavetz (יעב״ץ), Rabbi Yaakov Emden (רב יעקב עמדן), the son of the Chacham Tzvi (חכם צבי), records something the lecturer found beautiful.[3] Removing tallis (טלית) and tefillin (תפילין) and putting them away, a process that, done with care, can take a Jew a minute and a half, is a moment that many fill with something. The Yaavetz (יעב״ץ) writes that he used to watch his father to see what his holy father would do with that minute and a half, and saw him mumbling. He asked: “Tatte (טאטע), what are you saying?” and the Chacham Tzvi (חכם צבי) answered: “I say Parshas HaYirah (פרשת היראה), because Rabbeinu Yonah (רבינו יונה) says to say it every day.” The lecturer concluded that if the Chacham Tzvi (חכם צבי) did it, it must be a weighty matter, and that people say it because of that.

IV. Parshas HaTeshuvah

The Source

Parshas HaTeshuvah (פרשת התשובה) is also from Sefer Devarim (ספר דברים): “and you shall return to Hashem your God and heed His voice, just as I command you this day” (ושבת עד ה’ אלהיך ושמעת בקולו).[4] The lecturer explained that this parshah (פרשה) is the mitzvah (מצוה) of teshuvah (תשובה), and that it is something every Jew should say, drawing inspiration from the verses that speak of it.

In the sixteenth century there lived in Tzfas (צפת), in the time of the Arizal (אריז״ל), a Jew named Rabbi Moshe ben Machir (רבי משה בן מכיר), of whom the lecturer had spoken many times. He wrote a book called Seder HaYom (סדר היום), a guide to what a Jew does from morning until night, every single thing.[5] There he writes that one who says Parshas HaTeshuvah (פרשת התשובה) should have in mind (mechaven (מכוון)) that all the resha’im (רשעים) of Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל) should do teshuvah (תשובה).

Praying for Others When You Yourself Need It

The lecturer put the obvious question: one is suddenly worried about all the resha’im (רשעים); what about oneself? The answer is in the Gemara (גמרא): “Whoever prays for his fellow, and he needs the same thing himself, is answered first” (כל המתפלל בעד חברו והוא צריך לאותו דבר הוא נענה תחילה).[6] The lecturer said that, although he cited it from Berachos (ברכות), the principle is stated in the Gemara (גמרא) in Bava Kamma (בבא קמא). All of us need to do teshuvah (תשובה); when one prays that everyone do teshuvah (תשובה), and he needs it too, he will be answered first and enabled to do teshuvah (תשובה).

A Theological Question: Asking for Yiras Shamayim

A congregant raised a general theological question. If everything is in the hands of Heaven except yiras shamayim (יראת שמים) (הכל בידי שמים חוץ מיראת שמים), what does it mean to ask Hakadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא) for yiras shamayim (יראת שמים), which is in a person’s own hands?[7] The lecturer brought the answer of the Maharsha (מהרש״א) on that Gemara (גמרא) in Berachos (ברכות) 33, saying that the Maharsha (מהרש״א) reads the passage quite differently. Whatever a person asks of Hakadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא), such as parnassah (פרנסה), it is up to Heaven either to grant it or to reject it; but when a Jew says, “I want fear and awe of You” (אני רוצה יראה ואימה ממך), He has no choice, kaveyachol (כביכול), and is forced to answer; and for that reason we pray for it. The lecturer added that the question as a whole remains a good one and is outside the scope of the shiur (שיעור).

V. Parshas HaMan: A Segulah for Parnassah

The Once-a-Year Window

The last passage in the siddur (סידור), after which the siddur (סידור) is closed and one goes home, is Parshas HaMan (פרשת המן), which is much longer than the other two.[8] Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל), the lecturer said, “gets away with murder,” because it was learned from Rabbi Mendele of Rimanov (רבי מנדלי מרימנוב) that saying Parshas HaMan (פרשת המן) on the Tuesday of Parshas Beshalach (פרשת בשלח), which was two weeks earlier, is a segulah (סגולה) for parnassah (פרנסה); once a year, therefore, is good enough. On that day, he said, he had received some two hundred WhatsApp messages with the parshah (פרשה), from six in the morning until before shkiah (שקיעה). He recalled that the Satmar Rebbe (סאטמאר רב) used to say that this segulah (סגולה) helps only until nine o’clock: after that one goes to the office and makes one’s hishtadlus (השתדלות).

The Original Daily Custom

The original minhag (מנהג) was to say Parshas HaMan (פרשת המן) every day. The Shulchan Aruch (שלחן ערוך), in the very first siman (סימן), at the beginning of the laws of the morning prayer, lists it among the passages that it is good to say; and the lecturer reported that the Beis Yosef (בית יוסף) and, he believed, the Tur (טור) give the custom of saying it daily as a segulah (סגולה) for parnassah (פרנסה).[9] The source of that custom, he said, is a Yerushalmi (ירושלמי) that we no longer have, which he always finds curious. The Rishonim (ראשונים) often quote a Yerushalmi (ירושלמי) that cannot be found anywhere in the whole of it, from Berachos (ברכות) to Niddah (נדה); parts of the Yerushalmi (ירושלמי) are missing, but the Rishonim (ראשונים) saw a different version. Thus it comes from Chazal (חז״ל): whoever says Parshas HaMan (פרשת המן) every day has a segulah (סגולה). “Who turns down a segulah (סגולה) for parnassah (פרנסה)?” If it comes from the Rishonim (ראשונים) and from Chazal (חז״ל), one should certainly keep it.

The Tashbetz Katan and the Guarantee of the Maharam

There is a book called the Tashbetz Katan (תשב״ץ קטן). Why it is called “Katan” is too long to explain; Tashbetz (תשב״ץ) here is the acronym of Teshuvos Shimshon bar Tzadok (תשובות שמשון בר צדוק), one of the Rishonim (ראשונים), a disciple of the Maharam of Rothenburg (מהר״ם מרוטנבורג).[10] The lecturer recalled that the congregation had stood at the Maharam (מהר״ם)’s grave in Worms, and that he had spoken several times of his imprisonment in the tower at Ensisheim and of the story of the ransoming of captives (pidyon shevuyim (פדיון שבויים)) with the Rosh (רא״ש). For the six years that he was jailed, only one disciple was permitted to enter his cell, every day or once a week, to learn with him; some versions say that he stood outside, by the window, speaking with his rebbe (רבי) and learning from him. The Maharam (מהר״ם) was the rebbe (רבי) of the Rosh (רא״ש), of the Mordechai (מרדכי), and of the Hagahos Maimoniyos (הגהות מיימוניות): “Imagine, the gadol hador (גדול הדור) sitting in jail, and only one talmid (תלמיד) with whom he could share his Torah (תורה).” That talmid (תלמיד) wrote down everything that his rebbe (רבי) told him there, and recorded it in the book that we have today.

In it he writes in the name of the Maharam (מהר״ם): if you can take an extra minute or two out of your day, every day, to say Parshas HaMan (פרשת המן), it is a segulah (סגולה) for parnassah (פרנסה), and do not question it. He uses the words v’ani areiv (ואני ערב), “I am the guarantor”: the Maharam (מהר״ם) pledges that one who keeps this custom of saying Parshas HaMan (פרשת המן) every day will merit parnassah (פרנסה).

Shabbos, and Before or After Davening

A congregant asked whether it is said on Shabbos (שבת), and the lecturer answered that it is said without the yehi ratzon (יהי רצון). The siddurim (סידורים) say that the yehi ratzon (יהי רצון), which asks for parnassah (פרנסה), may not be said on Shabbos (שבת). He pointed out that the Beis Yosef (בית יוסף) and the Tur (טור), whom he had just quoted, say that it is to be said before davening (תפילה), and that the Acharonim (אחרונים) take strong issue with this: how can a Jew pray for his parnassah (פרנסה) before the davening (תפילה) itself? One wakes up in the morning, “parnassah (פרנסה), parnassah (פרנסה)!”; first one must daven (תפילה), say Krias Shema (קריאת שמע), pray Shemoneh Esrei (שמונה עשרה), and only then ask for parnassah (פרנסה). For that reason the passage was moved to the end of the siddur (סידור), at the end of Shacharis (שחרית), to be said after the davening (תפילה). The lecturer added, in his own voice, that he does not believe in segulos (סגולות) easily and does not know where this one comes from; all he knows is that holy Jews do it, so he supposes it has a source.

Why Every Day

For those who say it, the reason is plain. A person must beware of too much hishtadlus (השתדלות). The verses of Parshas HaMan (פרשת המן) teach “the one who gathered much had no excess, and the one who gathered little did not lack” (המרבה לא העדיף): however much effort they made, they received the same portion each day, and even one who made no effort received the same.[11] We become so carried away with hishtadlus (השתדלות), which is an obligation that Hakadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא) gave us, as is learned in the Chovos HaLevavos (חובות הלבבות), Shaar HaBitachon (שער הבטחון);[12] but we should not become overly excited, since it is He who provides. Saying Parshas HaMan (פרשת המן) establishes the emunah (אמונה) that Hakadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא) takes care of me. As we say in the prayers, in the passage that the lecturer places in Shomea Tefillah (שומע תפילה), “from the horns of the wild ox to the eggs of lice” (מקרני ראמים עד ביצי כינים), He provides for everyone.

The Gra’s Addition

The Vilna Gaon (וילנא גאון) adds something, in his characteristic style. The generation of the wilderness did nothing but learn Torah (תורה); they made no hishtadlus (השתדלות) for parnassah (פרנסה), and received manna (מן) from Heaven, lechem min hashamayim (לחם מן השמים), to teach that a Jew who spends his life learning Torah (תורה) will receive his parnassah (פרנסה). “Go tell that to the kollel (כולל) yungeleit (יונגעלייט) who come around collecting every Monday and Thursday: let them say Parshas HaMan (פרשת המן), and they will believe that Hakadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא) gives them their parnassah (פרנסה). But that is another inyan (ענין).” Some say the parshah (פרשה) as shnayim mikra v’echad targum (שנים מקרא ואחד תרגום), some with the taamim (טעמים), though not every day.

VI. The Transition to Shabbos Davening

The lecturer then turned from the prayers of the weekday. He acknowledged that the study had not covered Mincha (מנחה) and Maariv (מעריב) of the weekday, but said that there is not much to add, and that perhaps the congregation would return to Maariv (מעריב) later. He proposed to begin the prayers of Shabbos Kodesh (שבת קודש) from the beginning: “We are starting a new journey now.” A congregant asked whether talking is included in the Shema (שמע); the lecturer replied that the subject of talking during davening (תפילה) will be reached in its place.

VII. Hodu: The Opening of Kabbalas Shabbos

The Modern Opening

The lecturer asked the congregation to open a siddur (סידור) to Mincha (מנחה) of Kabbalas Shabbos (קבלת שבת) and asked what is the first thing one sees in every Nusach Sefard (נוסח ספרד) siddur (סידור) today. It is Hodu (הודו). The congregation has said Hodu (הודו) for the two years that he has been there; he noted that a bochur (בחור) who learns in a Lithuanian yeshivah (ישיבה) and davens (תפילה) Nusach Ashkenaz (נוסח אשכנז) had got up to lead Mincha (מנחה) without it, and that the Rav (רב) had not known, since the shul davens (תפילה) Nusach Sefard (נוסח ספרד), in which Hodu (הודו) is printed. Hodu (הודו), then, is the introduction to Mincha (מנחה) of Erev Shabbos (ערב שבת). What is it doing there?

Hodu (הודו) is a chapter of Tehillim (תהלים), the long chapter 107.[13] It is the source of the four who must give thanks: four people who pass through danger and are saved, and who must thank Hakadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא) and bring a korban todah (קרבן תודה). Since we have no korbanos (קרבנות) today, we say birkas HaGomel (ברכת הגומל), which comes entirely from this chapter: those who travel by sea, those who were ill and recovered, and the others, who “almost died.” Why, then, is it before Mincha (מנחה) of Erev Shabbos (ערב שבת)?

The Source: The Holy Baal Shem Tov

“You want to hear something crazy?” the lecturer said: it is unbelievable how much the influence of the chassidim (חסידים) shaped our siddur (סידור), even for those who are not chassidim (חסידים). The Baal Shem Tov (בעל שם טוב), in the 1700s, instituted this minhag (מנהג). The lecturer cited the book Maor Einayim (מאור עינים) of the holy Maggid of Chernobyl (טשרנובילער מגיד), one of the great disciples of the Baal Shem Tov (בעל שם טוב), who writes at the end of Parshas Beshalach (פרשת בשלח) that the Baal Shem Tov (בעל שם טוב) instituted the saying of Hodu (הודו).[14]

The Baal Shem Tov (בעל שם טוב) lived in a tiny village, Mezhibuzh, with a handful of disciples in the Carpathian Mountains; and slowly, over the century after his passing, his customs, what he introduced into the nusach hatefillah (נוסח התפילה) and his way in the service of God, spread like wildfire until they took over the world. The lecturer recalled that the Chofetz Chaim (חפץ חיים), before the Second World War, though he stood in the world of the misnagdim (מתנגדים), who could not stand the chassidim (חסידים), himself said that without the chassidim (חסידים) Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל) would have been lost to the Haskalah (השכלה), to Enlightenment and Reform, because the chassidim (חסידים) infused Judaism with fire, vitality, love and joy, so that even a simple Jew who did not know much could feel Hakadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא) through the teachings of the Baal Shem Tov (בעל שם טוב); and that this is true to this day. Everyone, he said, owes hakaras hatov (הכרת הטוב) to the Baal Shem Tov (בעל שם טוב), even the very Lithuanian; even the misnagdim (מתנגדים) understand today the power of the Baal Shem Tov (בעל שם טוב).

The Story of the Journey to Eretz Yisrael

What is the story behind the custom? The Baal Shem Tov (בעל שם טוב) wanted to go up to Eretz Yisrael (ארץ ישראל), as did every gadol (גדול) who lived in Europe. The Vilna Gaon (וילנא גאון) left his city and travelled toward Eretz Yisrael (ארץ ישראל), understood that Hakadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא) did not want him to go, and went back; many tzaddikim (צדיקים) wished to go up, and some succeeded and some did not. The Baal Shem Tov (בעל שם טוב) felt that he needed to go. He felt that if he were to meet the holy Or HaChaim (אור החיים), Rabbi Chaim ben Attar (רבי חיים בן עטר), who was his contemporary, Mashiach (משיח) would come. “It is easy for us to say such things,” said the lecturer, “but that was the legend among the chassidim (חסידים).”

He set out, taking his daughter, whom the lecturer named Adel (אדל), spelling it aloud, and noting that many girls in chassidic (חסידיש) circles bear the name, in English Adele; and his attendant, Reb Hirsh Mesharet (ר’ הירש משורת), who was his gabbai (גבאי). In those days, to get from Ukraine to Eretz Yisrael (ארץ ישראל) one had to pass through Istanbul. On the way to Istanbul there was a shipwreck and a storm, and the Baal Shem Tov (בעל שם טוב) went overboard. “Obviously it is all legends,” the lecturer said; “I do not know the exact details of the story.” But he was in danger of his life, and it was Erev Shabbos (ערב שבת). He got out somehow, and just before Shabbos (שבת) reached a shul somewhere near Istanbul, where he wished to thank Hakadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא) for the miracles.

At the same time he understood that it was not min hashamayim (מן השמים) that he should reach Eretz Yisrael (ארץ ישראל). Hakadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא) was placing in his way what we call meniyos (מניעות): blockages, obstacles. He was too holy, as it were, for the Baal Shem Tov (בעל שם טוב) to be in Eretz Yisrael (ארץ ישראל); the time had not come (לא הגיע הזמן). So he was sent back to Mezhibuzh. But on that Erev Shabbos (ערב שבת) the Baal Shem Tov (בעל שם טוב) told his disciple: “We must begin to say Hodu (הודו) to thank Hakadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא) for the miracles.” When he came home he told his disciples to say it together with him every Erev Shabbos (ערב שבת) before Mincha (מנחה), to commemorate the miracle that had happened to him.

That is why, said the lecturer, hundreds of thousands of Jews around the world (he first said millions, then corrected himself) say Hodu (הודו) before Mincha (מנחה) to this day. Whenever one sees someone in a short jacket saying Hodu (הודו), he suggested, one should ask him why; one might shock him by telling him that it memorializes a private miracle of the Baal Shem Tov (בעל שם טוב), which was in truth not private but a miracle for all of Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל), that the Baal Shem Tov (בעל שם טוב) survived. He found this fascinating, and noted that in a true chassidic (חסידיש) beis medrash (בית מדרש) Hodu (הודו) is said with fire.

VIII. Shir HaShirim: The Holy of Holies

The Custom Before Shabbos

At the request of a congregant, Reb Bernie, the lecturer turned to Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים), which most of those present, he assumed, do not say on Erev Shabbos (ערב שבת); the Sephardim do, he noted, and in some siddurim (סידורים) it is printed first, even before Hodu (הודו), verse by verse. Growing up, he said, his father had them ready before Shabbos (שבת), dressed, ten minutes early, to say Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים); he does not keep this himself, and his children, who are among the first in shul, he sends to say it, for it is said to be a segulah (סגולה) for a shidduch (שידוך). It makes sense because Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים) is all about the love between Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל) and Hakadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא), all in the form of a parable (derech mashal (דרך משל)). Why, then, is it said on Erev Shabbos (ערב שבת)?

The five megillos (מגילות) are read in public at other times: Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים) on the Shabbos (שבת) of Chol HaMoed Pesach (חול המועד פסח), Koheles (קהלת) on the Shabbos (שבת) of Chol HaMoed Sukkos (חול המועד סוכות), Rus (רות) on Shavuos (שבועות), Esther (אסתר) on Purim (פורים), and Eichah (איכה) on Tishah B’Av (תשעה באב). In shuls that follow the Vilna Gaon (וילנא גאון) they take a megillah (מגילה) written on klaf (קלף) and read from it; we have no such megillos (מגילות) except Esther (אסתר), the lecturer thought, but was told in the room that Koheles (קהלת) too is read from klaf (קלף), and that the Vilna Gaon (וילנא גאון) says to make a berachah (ברכה) on it. The lecturer, who had not known this, moved on.

Shlomo HaMelech and His Three Books

Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים) was written by Shlomo HaMelech (שלמה המלך), who wrote three books, Mishlei (משלי), Koheles (קהלת) and Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים); of Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים), tradition says that he wrote it when he was young. It is the song of songs, a song of love. If one reads it and translates it literally, which, the lecturer said, Rabbi Akiva (רבי עקיבא) tells us not to do, one finds it very graphic about the love between a lover and a beloved; but it is all parable of our love for Hakadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא). The Alshich HaKadosh (אלשיך הקדוש) explains in his introduction why Shlomo (שלמה) wrote it so (see below).

Tumas Kisvei HaKodesh and the Reason for the Decree

The lecturer turned to the Mishnah (משנה) in Maseches Yadayim (מסכת ידים), which concerns the tumah (טומאה) of the holy writings. Why do we not touch a Sefer Torah (ספר תורה) directly? We use a yad (יד), a silver pointer, and at the rolling (gelilah (גלילה)), if something needs to be adjusted, we take the mantle or the belt (gartel (גארטל)) and are careful not to touch it. Chazal (חז״ל) decreed that if one touches any part of Tanach (תנ״ך), whether Bereishis (בראשית), or the Neviim (נביאים), or the Kesuvim (כתובים), or certain megillos (מגילות) (not Megillas Esther (מגילת אסתר), which everyone touches), the hands become tamei (טמא).

The Gemara (גמרא) in Shabbos (שבת) 14 explains, in the name of Rav Mesharshiya (רב משרשיא).[15] In the old days there were no printed books; there were scrolls, of the Torah (תורה), Chumashim (חומשים), Neviim (נביאים) and Kesuvim (כתובים), and at home they kept them on a shelf next to the terumah (תרומה). The pantry then looked different, with a shelf for terumah (תרומה) and a shelf for chullin (חולין), and they kept the holy scrolls beside the terumah (תרומה) because both have kedushah (קדושה). The lecturer then told what happened: rats came into the pantry at night to eat the food, and while eating the terumah (תרומה) they damaged the scrolls beside it, with holes, or with droppings. (The Gemara (גמרא) itself speaks only of loss, pesedah (פסידא); the rats are the traditional picture of it.) So Chazal (חז״ל) decided to separate the terumah (תרומה) from the holy writings, and decreed a very light tumah (טומאה), among the lightest of the levels of tumah (טומאה), on the hands that touch them. The lecturer added that other reasons are given in the Gemara (גמרא) there. (Strictly, the Gemara (גמרא) attributes the tumah (טומאה) of the scroll itself to this storage, and gives a separate reason for the tumah (טומאה) of the hands: “because hands are busy.”)

The Dispute on Shir HaShirim

Maseches Yadayim (מסכת ידים), at the end of Seder Taharos (סדר טהרות), treats tumas yadayim (טומאת ידים). There the Mishnah (משנה) records a dispute whether Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים) was canonized, to use a word that is not the proper term: was it incorporated into Tanach (תנ״ך), with the different halachic level of kedushah (קדושה) that belongs to the holy scriptures? Rabbi Akiva (רבי עקיבא) answers: “Chas v’shalom (חס ושלום)!”[16] The lecturer said he did not know when the words chas v’shalom (חס ושלום) last appeared in a Mishnah (משנה); in the Gemara (גמרא) they are frequent, in the Mishnah (משנה) rare. No one in Israel ever thought that Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים) is not among the holy writings. Of course the hands become tamei (טמא); of course Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים) is holy. And then he adds: “for all the Writings are holy, and Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים) is the Kodesh Kodashim (קודש קדשים)” (שכל הכתובים קודש, ושיר השירים קודש קדשים). Just as there is a difference between the kedushah (קדושה) of the Beis HaMikdash (בית המקדש) and the Mishkan (משכן) and the Kodesh HaKodashim (קודש הקדשים), Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים) is the equivalent of the Kodesh HaKodashim (קודש הקדשים).

The Alshich: It Is All Sod

Why is Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים) the Kodesh HaKodashim (קודש הקדשים)? The holy Alshich (אלשיך), in his introduction to Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים), says something the lecturer called very interesting.[17] Every verse and every word in all of Tanach (תנ״ך) has pshat (פשט) and sod (סוד): a simple, literal meaning, and secrets upon secrets. In Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים) there is no pshat (פשט); if one reads it in its literal translation, it is all sod (סוד). It is therefore all Kodesh Kodashim (קודש קדשים): not kodesh (קודש) mixed with chullin (חולין), not pshat (פשט) with sod (סוד) above it, but entirely sod (סוד).

The Right Time: Just Before Shabbos

The only time, the lecturer reported, when a Jew can become a vessel to take in Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים), to contain the kedushah (קדושה) of this Kodesh HaKodashim (קודש הקדשים), is the time just before Shabbos (שבת). When a Jew goes to the mikveh (מקוה) on Erev Shabbos (ערב שבת), takes off his weekday clothes and puts on Shabbos (שבת) clothes, as those chassidim (חסידים) do who come out with wet peyos (פאות) still dripping, there is a loftiness, an external kedushah (קדושה), in bringing upon oneself the kedushas Shabbos (קדושת שבת) before Shabbos (שבת) has begun. It is not yet shkiah (שקיעה), but he is preparing. Those moments, said the lecturer, are the holiest of the week. This was a chiddush (חידוש) for him: they are holier than Shabbos (שבת), even than Seudah Shlishis (סעודה שלישית), since the holiest moments are those in which a Jew transforms from chol (חול) into a Jew of Shabbos (שבת). That is the proper time to say Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים); hence the ancient minhag (מנהג), which comes from the Arizal (אריז״ל) and his disciples, to say it on Erev Shabbos (ערב שבת).[18]

IX. Rabbi Eliezer Rokeach and the Amsterdam Medal

Who He Was

There is a hint (remez (רמז)), the lecturer said, and a secret in this, and it is worth knowing. There was a Jew named Rabbi Eliezer Rokeach (רב אליעזר רוקח); “it is a zechus (זכות) to speak of our heroes.” He was a gaon (גאון) and a tzaddik (צדיק), Chief Rabbi of Amsterdam, and wrote a book called Maaseh Rokeach (מעשה רוקח).[19] The Belzer rebbes (בעלזער רביס) today, he noted, are all Rokeachs, direct descendants, and there are many Rokeachs in many communities, not all chassidic (חסידיש). One must distinguish: “the Rokeach (רוקח)” without qualification means the first, Rabbi Elazar of Worms (רבי אלעזר מגרמייזא), in Germany; this is Rabbi Eliezer Rokeach (רב אליעזר רוקח) of the 1700s, who died, the lecturer said, in 1741, having been born, as someone in the room recalled, in the late 1600s.

The Medal

Something unprecedented in Jewish history, the lecturer said, happened with him. He was invited to become Chief Rabbi of Amsterdam, a massive Jewish community in the 1600s and 1700s made up of several kehillos (קהילות), Ashkenazim, Sephardim, Portuguese and Spanish, living under a government that was welcoming to Jews. The community wished to celebrate the coming of so special a rav (רב), the crowning (hachtarah (הכתרה)) of a new rav (רב), and a Jewish artist minted a medallion bearing his image, his bust, that is, the face and not the back. It had never happened before in history. A major controversy broke out. The medal bore an inscription, a blessing of welcome, baruchim haba’im (ברוכים הבאים) to our teacher Eliezer son of so-and-so, and a wish that his coming to Amsterdam be for blessing and success; the other side bears, the lecturer was told in the room, a verse from Tehillim (תהלים). One or two such coins survive in the world, in a museum in Europe whose name the lecturer had read and forgotten, and historians study it, since never before had a Jew, a member of the community, made such a coin in honor of a rav (רב).

The Responsum of the Yaavetz

The Yaavetz (יעב״ץ), the son of the Chacham Tzvi (חכם צבי), a contemporary of the Rokeach (רוקח), with whom he disagreed in matters, wrote about it in his Sheilas Yaavetz (שאלת יעב״ץ).[20] Someone had brought him the coin, from his father’s city, which had accepted a new rav (רב); on it was a picture of the rav (רב). He writes: I know this rav (רב); he is a tzaddik (צדיק), a gaon (גאון) and holy; I assure you that he does not know that this was done, that a medallion and a coin were minted in his honor. Why can it not be permitted? Because the Torah (תורה) says, “You shall not make an image or any likeness” (לא תעשה לך פסל וכל תמונה), which, the lecturer noted, had just been read in the parshah (פרשה).[21] He added, as an aside, that in earlier times rabbanim (רבנים) were very upset even at being photographed, citing the Yaaros Devash (יערות דבש) of Rabbi Yehonasan Eybeschutz (רב יהונתן אייבשיץ), who writes that it is a terrible thing to allow oneself to be photographed; and that the day before, in the shul office, a child had come to him with cards of gedolim (גדולים).

The Yaavetz (יעב״ץ) writes that when the coin was brought to him he was shocked that a Jewish image, of a rav (רב) and tzaddik (צדיק), should be directly against a lav (לאו) of the Torah (תורה), a piece of avodah zarah (עבודה זרה). He then analyzes the matter. There is a view among the Rishonim (ראשונים) that if it is only the face, the bust and not the rest of the body, the prohibition of “You shall not make an image or any likeness” does not apply; according to them it is only a matter of drash (דרש). The Yaavetz (יעב״ץ) brings that all the other Rishonim (ראשונים) disagree. He continues: I looked and saw (although I could not make out the letters) the outline of a nose, of a mouth, of eyes, and it is not engraved but raised, in the phrase of the Gemara (גמרא) the opposite of engraved, embossed. If it is embossed, if it sticks out, then it is assur (אסור) as avodah zarah (עבודה זרה). And at the end of the responsum he writes: Baruch Hashem (ברוך השם), that is why we have no such coins today, only one or two in the world; the government, the lecturer thought, never made it currency, but took it out of circulation; it was on a piece of copper, and it was withdrawn or hidden away (genizah (גניזה)), so that only one or two remain in museums and with collectors, all of which, said the lecturer, only shows the importance of this rav (רב) of Amsterdam.

Departure and Legacy

He left the city after six years because of a great dispute: Amsterdam was full of Sabbateans, whose stronghold there endured long after the death of Shabsai Tzvi (שבתי צבי), and he was a tzaddik (צדיק) and a zealot (kanoi (קנאי)). He went to Eretz Yisrael (ארץ ישראל) and died there; no one knows today where he is buried, perhaps in Tzfas (צפת), perhaps elsewhere. But he left a book called Maaseh Rokeach (מעשה רוקח), in which he writes a wonderful thing.

X. The Calculation of the 117 Verses

The Arithmetic

The lecturer said that the Maaseh Rokeach (מעשה רוקח) explains why Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים) is said every week; he had never seen it before and had come upon it the previous night, which was why he was so excited to share it. How many verses are there in Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים)? He did not expect anyone to know, and he had not known: 117 (קי״ז). Now the calculation. How many hours are there in a week besides Shabbos (שבת)? Six times twenty-four: 144 (קמ״ד). During the week the resha’im (רשעים) burn in Gehinnom (גהינום); on Shabbos (שבת) Gehinnom (גהינום) is closed. We are forbidden to light a fire here (“You shall not kindle a fire in any of your dwellings on the day of Shabbos (שבת),” לא תבערו אש בכל משבתיכם ביום השבת), and Hakadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא) does not light a fire there above; all the flames are lowered. The resha’im (רשעים) have an oneg Shabbos (עונג שבת) on Shabbos (שבת). We shall not burn on Shabbos (שבת), im yirtzeh Hashem (אם ירצה השם), and hopefully not during the week either; but during the week they could burn for 144 hours.

The Rokeach (רוקח) found in the holy Zohar (זוהר), in Parshas Noach (פרשת נח), that three times a day, when Jews say Amen Yehei Shmei Rabba (אמן יהא שמיה רבא), Hakadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא) shuts down Gehinnom (גהינום).[22] He is so moved by it that, the lecturer explained, it is not every Amen Yehei Shmei Rabba (אמן יהא שמיה רבא), since we say Kaddish (קדיש) countless times a day, but once at Shacharis (שחרית), once at Mincha (מנחה) and once at Maariv (מעריב). How long is Gehinnom (גהינום) closed? In the Zohar (זוהר), the lecturer believes, an hour and a half. Three times an hour and a half is four and a half hours a day; four and a half times six is twenty-seven. “I do not know what the calculation is,” he said, “I do not know what an hour and a half is, but that is what it says.” So for twenty-seven hours a week there is no Gehinnom (גהינום) in addition to Shabbos (שבת); 144 minus 27 is 117. For 117 hours a week Gehinnom (גהינום) is burning.

The Segulah

Shlomo HaMelech (שלמה המלך) wrote Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים) with 117 verses, which correspond to the 117 hours of the week. The Maaseh Rokeach (מעשה רוקח) writes that when we say it at the end of each week, and it takes no more than ten minutes (some say it slowly, with enthusiasm; at a regular pace the 117 verses take seven or six minutes), and we say it with kavanah (כוונה), “I promise you” that one will be able to diminish and take away the Gehinnom (גהינום) that one would have deserved for the 117 hours of the past week. Every week the slate is wiped clean: a wonderful segulah (סגולה) for a Jew to be saved from the judgment of Gehinnom (גהינום). That is why we say it on Erev Shabbos (ערב שבת), right before Shabbos (שבת): to compensate for, and to repair, the 117 hours that otherwise we would have burned.

For the Latecomer: Four VersesThe lecturer noted that at the end of Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים) in all the siddurim (סידורים), “ArtScroll” among them, there is a note that one who cannot say all of it, who comes running to shul late, should say four verses, which the lecturer gave as spelling Yaakov (יעקב) (יעקב): Yishakeini (ישקני), Shechorah (שחורה), Kumu (קומו), and a fourth that he did not complete. “I do not know if that segulah (סגולה) works, but it is certainly a big inyan (ענין). It is only four verses, but listen: knocking down four hours of Gehinnom (גהינום) is also a big thing. So perhaps we say those four.”

XI. The 117 of Shlomo HaMelech’s Atonement

The lecturer gave one more calculation of the Rokeach (רוקח) on the 117 verses. He prefaced it by saying that one cannot speak of Shlomo HaMelech (שלמה המלך) in this way, but that Chazal (חז״ל) tell us that Shlomo (שלמה), our hero, transgressed three major lavin (לאוין).[23] A king may not have too many horses (לא ירבה לו סוסים), may not have too many wives (לא ירבה לו נשים), and may not have too much silver and gold (וכסף וזהב לא ירבה לו מאד), which, the lecturer said, Shlomo (שלמה) had in bulk. What does one receive for transgressing a lav (לאו)? Malkus (מלקות). And how many malkus (מלקות) are there? Thirty-nine. Three transgressions at thirty-nine each is 117.

Shlomo (שלמה) wrote Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים) with 117 verses to atone for the three lavin (לאוין) and the 117 lashes that he would have received. At the end of the week, chas v’shalom (חס ושלום), if we deserve malkus (מלקות), we say it and take hold of the coattails of Shlomo HaMelech (שלמה המלך), asking: Hakadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא), as You forgive Shlomo (שלמה) and do not give him lashes in Gehinnom (גהינום), please do the same for us.

XII. Conclusion

The lecturer ended by urging that anyone who has the opportunity to say Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים) should say it, as a zechus (זכות), and that the week, though it began on a very hard and harsh note, should be full of simchah (שמחה); he asked those present to have in mind the mitzvah (מצוה) of “Serve Hashem with joy” (עבדו את ה’ בשמחה). In the three short parshiyos (פרשיות) at the end of Shacharis (שחרית) the service turns toward the world, to yirah (יראה), to teshuvah (תשובה) and to parnassah (פרנסה); Hodu (הודו) carries the memory of the Baal Shem Tov (בעל שם טוב) at sea; and Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים), said in the holy moments of transition into Shabbos (שבת), repairs the 117 hours of the week that is ending.

Before concluding, the lecturer acknowledged a guest who had come specially, Rabbi Mendy Koenig, who was sitting in the back. He did not usually make appeals on anyone’s behalf, he said, and was not permitted to, but this was something special for our brothers and sisters in Eretz Yisrael (ארץ ישראל): Rabbi Koenig does a great deal for the hostages and their families and for bereaved soldiers, and had shown him a book of photographs of the Shabbatonim (שבתונים) he holds, in which he strengthens people who are not observant and shows families what Shabbos (שבת) is and what ahavas Yisrael (אהבת ישראל) is. The lecturer said it is worth getting to know him, and that he wished the congregation to know that he was there and to take part. The congregation then went to the funeral.

As the lecturer reports: the work is a guide to the whole day, and in it the one who says Parshas HaTeshuvah (פרשת התשובה) is to have in mind that all the resha’im (רשעים) of Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל) do teshuvah (תשובה). The earlier text had instead that saying it “strengthens resolve for teshuvah (תשובה) through the year”; the recording says the former. The passage was not located (Open Questions, no. 4). ↩
  6. בבא קמא צ״ב ע״א. Confirmed on Sefaria: Rava (רבא) asks Rabba bar Mari (רבה בר מרי) for the source of the principle that one who prays for another, while needing the same thing, is answered first, and the verse given is Job 42:10, “And the Lord changed the fortune of Job when he prayed for his friends.” The lecturer placed the saying in Berachos (ברכות). The earlier text’s reference to Bereishis Rabbah (בראשית רבה) 52:5 did not match the passage retrieved there and is dropped. ↩
  7. ברכות ל״ג ע״ב; מהרש״א. Confirmed on Sefaria: Rabbi Chanina (רבי חנינא) says “everything is in the hands of Heaven except fear of Heaven,” and the Gemara (גמרא) there ties it to Deuteronomy 10:12. The Maharsha (מהרש״א)’s resolution is as the lecturer gives it; the passage itself was not retrieved (Open Questions, no. 5). ↩
  8. פרשת המן — שמות ט״ז. The passage of the manna in Parshas Beshalach (פרשת בשלח). The segulah (סגולה) of saying it on the Tuesday of Beshalach (בשלח) is attributed to Rabbi Menachem Mendel of Rimanov (רבי מנחם מנדל מרימנוב) (1745–1815); the remark of the Satmar Rebbe (סאטמאר רב) about nine o’clock is as the lecturer reports it. ↩
  9. שלחן ערוך, אורח חיים סימן א׳ סעיף ה׳. Confirmed on Sefaria: the Mechaber (מחבר) writes “it is good to say the portion of the binding of Yitzchak, the portion of the manna, the Ten Commandments” and the portions of the offerings; the text retrieved does not itself give the segulah (סגולה) for parnassah (פרנסה). That the Beis Yosef (בית יוסף) and the Tur (טור) give the daily recital as a segulah (סגולה), and that its source is a Yerushalmi (ירושלמי) not preserved in our editions, is as the lecturer reports (Open Questions, no. 6). The earlier text’s remark that the Rishonim (ראשונים) had fuller manuscripts of the Yerushalmi (ירושלמי) is the lecturer’s. ↩
  10. תשב״ץ קטן, רבי שמשון בר צדוק. The acronym is given in the recording as Teshuvos Shimshon bar Tzadok (תשובות שמשון בר צדוק); the earlier text had “talmid (תלמיד) Shimshon bar Tzadok,” a summary error. The work should not be confused with the later Tashbetz (תשב״ץ) of Rabbi Shimon ben Tzemach Duran (רבי שמעון בן צמח דוראן). The Maharam (מהר״ם)’s imprisonment in Ensisheim (from 1286) and the ransom story with the Rosh (רא״ש) are as the lecturer recalls them. The earlier text listed the Maharam (מהר״ם)’s students as the Rosh (רא״ש), the Mordechai (מרדכי) and the Agudah (אגודה); the recording has the Hagahos Maimoniyos (הגהות מיימוניות). The passage with v’ani areiv (ואני ערב) was not located (Open Questions, no. 7). ↩
  11. שמות ט״ז:י״ח. Confirmed on Sefaria: “But when they measured it by the omer, anyone who had gathered much had no excess, and anyone who had gathered little had no deficiency.” The lecturer’s wording, hamarbeh lo he’diph (המרבה לא העדיף), is a paraphrase of the order of the verse. ↩
  12. חובות הלבבות, שער הבטחון, רבינו בחיי אבן פקודה. As the lecturer reports; the classic work on bitachon (בטחון) and hishtadlus (השתדלות). The passage was not retrieved. ↩
  13. תהלים ק״ז; ברכות נ״ד ע״ב. Confirmed on Sefaria (Berachos (ברכות) 54b, Rav Yehuda (רב יהודה) in the name of Rav (רב)): four must give thanks, the seafarers, the travellers of the wilderness, one who recovered from illness and one released from prison, all found in this psalm (verses 23–31, 4–8, 17–21 and 10–14). The lecturer said that Hodu (הודו) is printed in almost every Nusach Sefard (נוסח ספרד) siddur (סידור) before Mincha (מנחה) of Erev Shabbos (ערב שבת). ↩
  14. מאור עינים, רבי מנחם נחום מטשרנוביל (1730–1797). As the lecturer reports: at the end of Parshas Beshalach (פרשת בשלח) the work states that the Baal Shem Tov (בעל שם טוב) instituted the saying of Hodu (הודו). The passage was not retrieved, and the lecturer himself said that the story of the journey, the shipwreck and the rescue is legend and that he does not know the details; whether it is told in the Maor Einayim (מאור עינים) is not shown (Open Questions, no. 8). The names Adel (אדל) and Reb Hirsh Mesharet (ר’ הירש משורת) are as the recording has them; the earlier text had Odel (עודל) and Avraham HaShamash (אברהם השמש), from the summary (Open Questions, no. 9). ↩
  15. שבת י״ד ע״א. Confirmed on Sefaria, in the name of Rav Mesharshiya (רב משרשיא): at first they stored terumah (תרומה) foods next to a Sefer Torah (ספר תורה) and said “this is holy and that is holy” (האי קדש והאי קדש); when they saw it was leading to loss (פסידא), the Sages decreed tumah (טומאה). The Gemara (גמרא) does not mention mice; the rats are the lecturer’s telling and probably the traditional explanation (Open Questions, no. 10). For the hands, the same page gives another reason: “because hands are busy” (מפני שהידים עסקניות הן). The earlier text’s Hebrew quotation and its description of this as one of the Eighteen Decrees did not match the page and are dropped. ↩
  16. משנה ידים ג׳:ה׳. Confirmed on Sefaria: Rabbi Akiva (רבי עקיבא) says “Far be it! No man in Israel disputed that the Song of Songs defiles the hands; for the whole world is not as worthy as the day on which the Song of Songs was given to Israel; for all the writings are holy but the Song of Songs is the holy of holies” (the Hebrew opens אמר רבי עקיבא, חס ושלום and ends ושיר השירים קדש קדשים; the middle was not retrieved in Hebrew). The lecturer’s paraphrase had “all the holy writings” (כתבי הקודש); the Mishnah (משנה) has the Writings (הכתובים). ↩
  17. אלשיך הקדוש על שיר השירים, הקדמה (רבי משה אלשיך, Tzfas, 1508–1593). As the lecturer reports; the passage was not retrieved (Open Questions, no. 11). The recording has the Alshich (אלשיך) explaining in his introduction why Shlomo (שלמה) wrote it in this way; the earlier text and the summary had the Ibn Ezra (אבן עזרא) here, which is not what the recording says. ↩
  18. מנהג האר״י ז״ל — שיר השירים בערב שבת. As the lecturer reports, that the custom comes from the Arizal (אריז״ל) and his disciples. The earlier text cited the Shaar HaKavanos (שער הכוונות) of Rabbi Chaim Vital (רבי חיים ויטאל); the lecturer did not (Open Questions, no. 12). He attributes the statement about the proper moment to the Rishonim (ראשונים) (and, in another place, to the books), which does not fit the date of the Arizal (אריז״ל). ↩
  19. מעשה רוקח, רבי אלעזר רוקח (d. 5501 / 1741). A commentary on the Mishneh Torah (משנה תורה) of the Rambam (רמב״ם); the lecturer places the calculation of the 117 verses in it, which was not retrieved (Open Questions, no. 13). The lecturer gives his death as 1741 and his birth as the late 1600s; the earlier text had “c. 1665” and an Amsterdam tenure of 1735–1740, which the recording does not give (he says about six years). The lecturer says that no one knows where he is buried. ↩
  20. שאלת יעב״ץ. As the lecturer reports; the responsum was not retrieved (the earlier text gave חלק א׳ סימן ק״ע, not verified). The lecturer has the Yaavetz (יעב״ץ) saying that the coin came from “my father’s city,” where a new rav (רב) had been accepted, and also says that the two men both lived in Amsterdam; how these fit is unresolved (Open Questions, no. 14). ↩
  21. שמות כ׳:ד׳; עבודה זרה מ״ג ע״ב. Exodus 20:4 (“You shall not make for yourself a sculptured image, or any likeness of what is in the heavens above…”) is confirmed on Sefaria. On Avodah Zarah (עבודה זרה) 43b (confirmed): the human face is forbidden because man is made in God’s image, and a protruding figure on a seal ring is the concern, while a sunken one may be worn. The lecturer’s remark that rabbis disliked being photographed, which he attributes to the Yaaros Devash (יערות דבש), presumably concerns portraits, since Rabbi Yehonasan Eybeschutz (רבי יהונתן אייבשיץ) died in 1764, before photography (Open Questions, no. 15). ↩
  22. זוהר, פרשת נח. As the lecturer reports; he named only Parshas Noach (פרשת נח) and said “I believe” of the hour and a half. The folio in the earlier text (ס״ב ע״ב) and the reference to the Tikkunei Zohar (תיקוני זוהר) were not checked (Open Questions, no. 16). ↩
  23. סנהדרין כ״א ע״ב; דברים י״ז:ט״ז–י״ז. Confirmed on Sefaria: Deuteronomy 17:16–17, “he shall not keep many horses … he shall not have many wives … nor shall he amass silver and gold to excess.” In Sanhedrin (סנהדרין) 21b Shlomo (שלמה) says “I will multiply and not turn away” (אני ארבה ולא אסור, of wives) and “I will multiply and not return” (אני ארבה ולא אשיב, of horses); on silver and gold the page quotes the verses and the days of Shlomo (שלמה) but records no such statement, so the third lav (לאו) in the count is the lecturer’s and the Rokeach (רוקח)’s. That each carries thirty-nine lashes is the lecturer’s and is not examined here (Open Questions, no. 17). ↩

Editorial Notes  ·  Open Questions

Items unresolved at the time of writing, to be settled with the lecturer or from the sources before printing. Reports of the lecturer are recorded as his and are not asserted in the text.

  1. The Gemara (גמרא) on the chaburah (חבורה). The continuation of the passage in Shabbos (שבת) 105b; the cross-reference in Moed Katan (מועד קטן).
  2. Sefer HaYirah (ספר היראה). The passage in which Rabbeinu Yonah (רבינו יונה) calls the daily recital of the passage a segulah (סגולה); the attribution of the book and the dates of the author.
  3. The Siddur HaYaavetz (סידור היעב״ץ). The place of the story of the Chacham Tzvi (חכם צבי) and his tefillin (תפילין).
  4. The Seder HaYom (סדר היום). The passage on Parshas HaTeshuvah (פרשת התשובה) and the intention for the resha’im (רשעים).
  5. The Maharsha (מהרש״א) on Berachos (ברכות) 33b. The passage; and the lecturer’s reading of it.
  6. The segulah (סגולה) of Parshas HaMan (פרשת המן). The Beis Yosef (בית יוסף) and the Tur (טור) on the daily recital; the Yerushalmi (ירושלמי); the source of the Tuesday of Beshalach (בשלח) and of the remark of the Satmar Rebbe (סאטמאר רב).
  7. The Tashbetz Katan (תשב״ץ קטן). The passage with v’ani areiv (ואני ערב); the title and the author; the detail of the single disciple at the cell or the window.
  8. The Maor Einayim (מאור עינים) on Hodu (הודו). The passage at the end of Beshalach (בשלח), and the source of the story of the journey, the shipwreck and the rescue.
  9. The names in the story. Adel (אדל) and Reb Hirsh Mesharet (ר’ הירש משורת), as the recording has them; the legend of a meeting with the Or HaChaim (אור החיים); the lecturer’s account of the Vilna Gaon (וילנא גאון)’s attempt.
  10. Rashi (רש״י) and the rats. Whether the account of rats in Shabbos (שבת) 14a is from Rashi (רש״י), and its wording; the “other reasons” in the Gemara (גמרא) that the lecturer mentions.
  11. The Alshich (אלשיך). The passage in the introduction to Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים), both on why Shlomo (שלמה) wrote as he did and on the absence of pshat (פשט).
  12. The custom of Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים) on Erev Shabbos (ערב שבת). The source for the “moments of transformation” and for the claim that they are holier than Shabbos (שבת); the Arizal (אריז״ל)’s custom.
  13. The Maaseh Rokeach (מעשה רוקח). Where the two calculations are found; the dates of the author (the lecturer: died 1741, born late 1600s); his Amsterdam years; his burial.
  14. The Amsterdam medal. The inscription and the verse on the reverse; the museum; the date; the Yaavetz (יעב״ץ)’s responsum and its text; how “my father’s city” fits the lecturer’s statement that both lived in Amsterdam; the view of the Rishonim (ראשונים) (the lecturer says “the Rosh”) that the face alone is only drash (דרש); the Gemara (גמרא)’s terms for engraved and raised.
  15. The Yaaros Devash (יערות דבש). The passage on being photographed (see the note on the dates).
  16. The Zohar (זוהר) on Amen Yehei Shmei Rabba (אמן יהא שמיה רבא). The passage; the figure of an hour and a half; the count of the hours of Shabbos (שבת).
  17. Shlomo (שלמה)’s three lavin (לאוין). The silver and gold; the lashes for each; and where the Rokeach (רוקח) gives the count.
  18. The four verses. The list that spells Yaakov (יעקב) (the lecturer gave Yishakeini (ישקני), Shechorah (שחורה), Kumu (קומו) and did not complete it), and its place in the siddurim (סידורים).
  19. Names in the room. “Rabbi Ari” (the bilingual transcript; the other has “Avi”); the name of the guest, Rabbi Mendy Koenig.
  20. Last week’s subject. The bilingual transcript has the lecturer referring to Ani Maamin (אני מאמין) as last week’s subject; the earlier text had Anenu (ענני).
  21. The Hebrew date. 21 Shevat 5786, computed for Sunday 8 February 2026 (15 Shevat, Tu BiShvat (טו בשבט), was Monday 2 February); the earlier text gave only the civil date.

The edition follows the order of the discourse, records the lecturer’s reports as his, and leaves unresolved items to the Open Questions.
