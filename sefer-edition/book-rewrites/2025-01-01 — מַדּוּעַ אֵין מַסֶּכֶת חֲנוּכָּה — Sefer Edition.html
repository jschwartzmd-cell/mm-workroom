מַדּוּעַ אֵין מַסֶּכֶת חֲנוּכָּה?

Why Is There No Maseches Chanukah?
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Erev Zos Chanukah 5785
Delivered 1 January 2025 (Erev Zos Chanukah)  ·  Sefer Edition · Revised August 2026

A study of one silence in the Mishnah — and of the political, historical, and mystical currents that give that silence its shape — delivered on Erev Zos Chanukah, the day the sages named for the last outstretched hand of the year.

I. Erev Zos Chanukah: The Last Outstretched Hand

The Plain Meaning of the Name

The eighth day of Chanukah bears an ancient name: Zos Chanukah (זֹאת חֲנֻכָּה). Its plain sense is drawn from the Krias HaTorah (קְרִיאַת הַתּוֹרָה) of that day, when the offerings of the twelve nesi’im are summed up in the closing verse: זֹאת חֲנֻכַּת הַמִּזְבֵּחַ בְּיוֹם הִמָּשַׁח אֹתוֹ — “This is the dedication of the altar on the day it was anointed.”[1] The name is textual: it belongs to the parshas ha’yom.

The Bnei Yissaschar: A Gate That Remains Open

The Bnei Yissaschar, however — as transmitted by his son the Sameach David (שָׂמֵחַ דָּוִד) — reads a deeper stratum into the day.[2] On Zos Chanukah:

כִּי יְמִינְךָ פְּשׁוּטָה לְקַבֵּל שָׁבִים.
“For Your right hand is outstretched to receive those who return.”

The right hand of HaKadosh Baruch Hu (הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא) remains outstretched even now to receive any Jew whose teshuvah (תְּשׁוּבָה) from the Yamim Nora’im has not yet been completed. In the framing of the Chassidishe sefarim (חֲסִידִישֶׁע סְפָרִים), we have been granted an additional stretch of weeks past the sealing of the year — past Rosh HaShanah (רֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה), past Yom Kippur (יוֹם כִּפּוּר), past Hoshana Rabbah (הוֹשַׁעְנָא רַבָּה) — and Zos Chanukah stands as the last of the gates through which the returning soul may enter.

The Apter Rav on the Secular New Year

To this the Chassidishe tradition adds a striking supplementary teaching, preserved in the name of the Apter Rav, the Ohev Yisrael (אוֹהֵב יִשְׂרָאֵל) of Apta[3] — that one ought to greet a fellow Jew with “A gutt yohr” even on the first of January. The vort answers its own obvious objection. Our Rosh HaShanah falls in Tishrei (תִּשְׁרֵי); why mark the calendar of the nations at all?

The answer runs to the theological core of the matter. On the night of Rosh HaShanah the Jewish people stood in tefillah (תְּפִלָּה) and cheshbon ha’nefesh. On the night that marks the turning of the secular year, much of the surrounding world stands in the opposite posture. When these two nights are set beside one another before the Throne of Judgment, the contrast itself becomes an advocate. Whatever tefillos (תְּפִלּוֹת) may have been obstructed by mekatregim (מְקַטְרְגִים) during Aseres Yemei Teshuvah (עֲשֶׂרֶת יְמֵי תְּשׁוּבָה), on this day — on the strength of the contrast — they are retroactively received.

Rav Yaakov Kamenetsky and the New Year’s Resolution

There is a well-preserved anecdote about Rav Yaakov Kamenetsky[4] that gives the same teaching a practical face. He walked into a yeshiva classroom on the morning of January the first — a regular day of learning in the beis midrash — and posed a question to the assembled bachurim (בַּחוּרִים): “What is your New Year’s resolution?” The students, taken aback, replied that such a thing was not properly a Jewish practice. Rav Yaakov corrected them. An entire country was spending the day in cheshbon ha’nefesh and kabbalos tovos (קַבָּלוֹת טוֹבוֹת). Ought Klal Yisrael (כְּלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל) to remain silent? If a whole nation had set aside a day to accept upon itself something good, then a ben Torah (בֶּן תּוֹרָה) should accept upon himself something good as well — something worthy of his stature. Where the surrounding world resolves to read two books, a Jew ought to resolve to finish two masechtos (מַסֶּכְתּוֹת).

The theme common to all three teachings — the Bnei Yissaschar’s hand, the Apter Rav’s vort, Rav Yaakov’s resolution — is that history is read backward. What appears final has not yet been sealed; what was blocked one day ascends the next; what appears secular becomes, through the contrast, an advocate for the sacred. This is the register in which the central kushya (קֻשְׁיָא) of this study must be heard, for it too concerns a silence that turns out to speak.

II. The Kushya: The Silence of the Mishnah

Nearly every yom tov (יוֹם טוֹב) in the Jewish calendar has received a maseches (מַסֶּכֶת) of its own from the hand of Rabbeinu HaKadosh (רַבֵּינוּ הַקָּדוֹשׁ). Yoma (יוֹמָא) for the fast; Sukkah (סֻכָּה) for the booth; Rosh HaShanah (רֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה), Megillah (מְגִלָּה), Beitzah (בֵּיצָה), Pesachim (פְּסָחִים), Ta’anis. Each of these tractates is the definitive halakhic codification of its day.

For Chanukah there is no such tractate. The halakhos (הֲלָכוֹת) of the eight-day festival — the lighting of the menorah (מְנוֹרָה), the placement at the doorway, the times of kindling, the order of the blessings — are transmitted in scattered mishnayos (מִשְׁנָיוֹת) across roughly seven tractates and receive their sustained treatment only in the beraisa (בְּרַיְתָא) preserved in Shabbos (שַׁבָּת).[5] No Maseches Chanukah (מַסֶּכֶת חֲנֻכָּה) appears in the six orders. The absence is conspicuous; it has troubled every generation of learners; and it demands accounting.

The classical tradition offers a series of answers. What follows arrays these answers in ascending order — each addressing a weakness in the one before it — and then draws them together at the end.

III. The First Answer: The Vilna Gaon — A Lost Maseches

The tradition of the Vilna Gaon (הַגְּרָ"א), preserved by his son R’ Avraham in the introduction to Aderes Eliyahu (אַדֶּרֶת אֵלִיָּהוּ),[6] is that there once did exist a Maseches Chanukah (מַסֶּכֶת חֲנֻכָּה), and that it was lost along with several other short masechtos (מַסֶּכְתּוֹת) devoted to specific inyanim (עִנְיָנִים) — among them a tractate on tefillin (תְּפִלִּין). The Gaon’s tradition draws on the well-known account of Rav Hai Gaon, who is said to have encountered a mysterious zaken v’chacham in possession of an unknown maseches (מַסֶּכֶת), and who departed before the text could be transcribed.

A serious difficulty accompanies this position. When one maseches exists within Shas (שַׁ"ס), the others typically cross-reference it in the familiar formula “k’di’amrinan b’maseches ploni.” No such reference to “b’maseches Chanukah” appears anywhere in the Bavli (בַּבְלִי) or the Yerushalmi (יְרוּשַׁלְמִי). This absence is not decisive — it is possible that a very short maseches of the type the Gaon envisioned would have generated no cross-references, and it is equally possible that the loss came sufficiently early that references were themselves excised or forgotten — but the difficulty remains substantial enough that the position cannot stand as the sole account. It is best treated as a genuine tzurech iyun (צָרִיךְ עִיּוּן): a mesorah (מְסוֹרָה) that resists ordinary evidentiary confirmation and that later authors have engaged rather than dismissed.

IV. The Second Answer: The Reported Vendetta and the Sforno’s Response

The Reported Chasam Sofer

A second answer is preserved in the name of the Chasam Sofer, transmitted by his grandson in Chut HaMeshulash (חוּט הַמְשֻׁלָּשׁ).[7] Rabbeinu HaKadosh (רַבֵּינוּ הַקָּדוֹשׁ) was a descendant of Beis David (בֵּית דָּוִד). The Chashmonaim (חַשְׁמוֹנָאִים) were kohanim (כֹּהֲנִים) of the tribe of Levi (לֵוִי). When they assumed the crown alongside their priestly service, they occupied a station reserved by prophetic promise for the descendants of David. On this reading, the composer of the Mishnah (מִשְׁנָה) could not bring himself to devote a full tractate to the yom tov (יוֹם טוֹב) of a dynasty that had, in his eyes, encroached upon the inheritance of his own royal line.

The position is not without internal support within Chazal (חֲזַ"ל)’s own literary conventions. The Gemara (גְּמָרָא) in Horayos (הוֹרָיוֹת)[8] records that Rabbeinu HaKadosh instructed his son not to cite Rabi Meir (רַבִּי מֵאִיר) by name but as “acherim,” and not to cite Rabi Nasan (רַבִּי נָתָן) by name but as “yesh omrim” — because these two great tanna’im had once attempted to unseat Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel (רַבָּן שִׁמְעוֹן בֶּן גַּמְלִיאֵל), Rabbeinu HaKadosh’s father, and thus (on the reading of the tradition) to uproot the honor of Malchus Beis David (מַלְכוּת בֵּית דָּוִד). Whoever attempts to displace the kavod (כָּבוֹד) of the Davidic line, in Rabbeinu HaKadosh’s editorial discipline, is not to be named. By extension, one might reason, the Chashmonaim’s yom tov would meet a corresponding silence.

The internal re’ayah is real, but a difficulty presses upon the answer from without. The halakhos (הֲלָכוֹת) of Chanukah are binding upon every Jew every year; they require transmission whatever a personal disposition might dictate. It is true that a distinction remains available — one may transmit the halakhos in scattered mishnayos (מִשְׁנָיוֹת) (as in fact happens) while declining to grant the Chashmonaim a full tractate of their own — but this refinement is not the position as originally presented, and it leaves open a still deeper question.

The Sforno’s Objection: Was There a Cheit at All?

The premise of the vendetta answer is that the Chashmonaim’s assumption of the throne was itself a transgression of Yaakov Avinu’s blessing, לֹא יָסוּר שֵׁבֶט מִיהוּדָה. The Ramban (רַמְבַּ"ן), in the previous year’s study, was cited for the view that it was.[9] The Sforno,[10] however — R’ Ovadyah Sforno of Bologna, one of the leading Italian poskim (פּוֹסְקִים) of the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries and among the finest Hebraists of the Renaissance — reads the verse in the opposite direction and thereby dismantles the premise of the previous answer.

“Lo yasur” in the Sforno’s reading is a prohibition on removing the royal scepter from Shevet Yehudah (שֵׁבֶט יְהוּדָה). So long as a Davidic king sits upon the throne, no other tribe may usurp him. But once the crown has been removed — by the churban (חֻרְבָּן) of the First Temple, by the exile of Tzidkiyahu (צִדְקִיָּהוּ), by the centuries in which no Davidic king reigned at all — the prohibition falls silent. There is no issur (אִסּוּר) upon a member of another tribe assuming a throne that has already stood empty. The Sforno’s objection to the Ramban’s reading is sharp: on his view it is a serious error to indict the Chashmonaim for taking up a crown that had lain vacant for centuries.

If the Sforno is correct, then the second answer loses its footing. There was no cheit (חֵטְא) for Rabbeinu HaKadosh to hold against the Hasmonean line. The Chashmonaim restored what had been removed; they neither trespassed upon Yehudah’s prerogative nor displaced a rightful heir. The kushya (קֻשְׁיָא) of the missing maseches (מַסֶּכֶת) must be answered on other grounds.

The Rambam’s Praise and His Historical Introduction

That the Sforno’s reading enjoys strong internal support may be seen from the Rambam’s own posture toward the Chashmonaim. In Hilchos Chanukah (הִלְכוֹת חֲנֻכָּה)[11] the Rambam does not condemn them; he praises them. The Chashmonaim were chazakim v’gibborim; through them malchus (מַלְכוּת) was restored to Klal Yisrael (כְּלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל) for more than two hundred years.

More striking still is the literary form the Rambam chooses for these halakhos. Uniquely in the entire Mishneh Torah (מִשְׁנֵה תּוֹרָה), the opening of Hilchos Chanukah is not halakhah (הֲלָכָה) at all but three full halakhos of historical narrative: who the Chashmonaim were, what oppression they overthrew, how the Temple was reconsecrated. The Mishneh Torah is, by design, a dry codex of law — not a storybook. What compelled the Rambam to open here with history?

The answer lies in the Rambam’s own hakdamah (הַקְדָּמָה).[12] He composed the Mishneh Torah, he tells us, so that a Jew need have at home only a Tanach (תַּנַּ"ךְ) and this book, and from the two possess the complete corpus of the written and the oral Torah. This hakdamah was the source of the great opposition that arose against him: the Geonim (גְּאוֹנִים) of Babylonia argued that his stated aim would render the study of Mishnah and Gemara obsolete, and roughly forty years after his passing his books were burned. For a century the Jewish world split between pro-Rambam and anti-Rambam parties. The Rambam’s vision ultimately prevailed.

But if the Rambam’s intended reader possessed only the Tanach and the Mishneh Torah, then a difficulty arises at Chanukah alone. Chanukah is nowhere in Tanach. Without a historical introduction, the reader would open Hilchos Chanukah and encounter obligations concerning a festival whose origin he could not name. The Rambam therefore prefixes a hakdamah historit (הַקְדָּמָה הִיסְטוֹרִית) to the halakhos. The comparison with Hilchos Megillah confirms the diagnosis: there the Rambam supplies no historical introduction, because the reader already possesses Megillas Esther (מְגִלַּת אֶסְתֵּר) in his Tanach.

V. The Third Answer: Only What the Olam Does Not Know

A third answer, likewise attributed to the Chasam Sofer (חֲתַם סוֹפֵר) in the tradition of his drashos,[13] rests on a general principle of the Mishnah (מִשְׁנָה)’s editorial method. Rabbeinu HaKadosh (רַבֵּינוּ הַקָּדוֹשׁ)’s task was l’chadeish — to record what was not already universally known. The halakhos (הֲלָכוֹת) that lived on every Jewish tongue required no tikun b’kesav; the Mishnah committed to writing only what was in danger of being forgotten.

The principle is not stated by Rabbeinu HaKadosh in the Mishnah itself. It is drawn, rather, from the Rambam (רַמְבַּ"ם)’s remarkable observation in his Pirush HaMishnayos (פֵּירוּשׁ הַמִּשְׁנָיוֹת) on Menachos (מְנָחוֹת).[14] The Rambam notes that there is no mishnah (מִשְׁנָה) anywhere that mandates the twice-daily recitation of Krias Shema (קְרִיאַת שְׁמַע). Maseches Berachos opens with the times and postures of the recitation but never establishes the underlying chiyuv (חִיּוּב). Nor does any mishnah mandate the affixing of a mezuzah (מְזוּזָה) to the doorpost or the daily wearing of tefillin (תְּפִלִּין). These were universal practices; every Jewish home observed them; no formal codification was required.

Applied to Chanukah: by the time of the Mishnah’s redaction, approximately three and a half centuries had elapsed since the Maccabean revolt. The chronology is precise. The Maccabean uprising is dated to 167 BCE; Rabi Yehudah (יְהוּדָה) HaNasi was born, as the Gemara (גְּמָרָא) in Kiddushin (קִדּוּשִׁין) records,[15] on the day Rabi Akiva (רַבִּי עֲקִיבָא) died, roughly 135 CE; the Mishnah was redacted approximately half a century after that. Over roughly three hundred and fifty years the practices of Chanukah — the eight days, the kindling of lights, the recitation of Hallel (הַלֵּל), the reading of the nesi’im — had settled so deeply into the fabric of Jewish domestic life that no household required a written codification.

The l’chadeish principle raises a converse question that its defender must be prepared to meet: if Rabbeinu HaKadosh declined to record what was universally known, why do Maseches Sukkah, Maseches Pesachim, and Maseches Megillah contain material equally universal? The distinction, on the closest reading of the principle, is that these tractates address halakhos whose fine details vary sufficiently in application, or whose calendrical or agricultural conditions require sufficient adjudication, that comprehensive treatment is necessary. Chanukah’s core practice — kindle the lights, on eight consecutive nights, at the doorway — admits of less variation. The l’chadeish principle is genuine, but it operates on a spectrum rather than as a binary; it explains what is possible about Chanukah’s treatment without exhausting what needs explanation.

VI. The Fourth Answer: Megillas Taanis Already Existed

A fourth answer removes the pressure of the kushya (קֻשְׁיָא) by pointing to what already existed in written form when Rabbeinu HaKadosh (רַבֵּינוּ הַקָּדוֹשׁ) sat to compose the Mishnah (מִשְׁנָה). Megillas Taanis (מְגִלַּת תַּעֲנִית)[16] is a short work in leshon ha’kodesh that lists the days of the year on which hesped (הֶסְפֵּד) and ta’anis are forbidden — the calendar of the yamim tovim ketanim (יָמִים טוֹבִים קְטַנִּים). It is quoted throughout both Talmuds; it is, by scholarly consensus, the earliest written composition of Torah she’b’al peh, predating the Mishnah.

The majority of the material in Megillas Taanis concerns the victories of the Chashmonaim (חַשְׁמוֹנָאִים) over the Greek forces. The chronology of the eight days, the sequence of the miracle, the identification of the days that must be treated as yom tov (יוֹם טוֹב) — the essential historical framework of Chanukah is already there. By the time Rabbeinu HaKadosh sat down to compose the Mishnah, the historical and halakhic corpus of Chanukah was already accessible b’kesav. He had no obligation to duplicate what the umah (אֻמָּה) already possessed.

This answer, unlike the two before it, does not depend on any claim about Rabbeinu HaKadosh’s personal disposition or editorial method. It rests on a simple bibliographic observation about the state of the written tradition in his day. As such it stands independently of the difficulties that press upon the earlier answers, and it may well remain a partial contributor to the true account even if it is not the whole of it.

VII. The Fifth Answer: Rav Reuven Margaliyos and the Political Silence

The Author and His Question

The most historically ambitious of the classical answers — and the one toward which the argument of this study is directed — is developed by Rav Reuven Margaliyos.[17] Rav Margaliyos was among the most striking figures of the twentieth century’s Torah scholarship: a talmid chacham (תַּלְמִיד חָכָם) of the first rank, a historian who read the Roman sources in the original, and by trade a shopkeeper in Eretz Yisrael (אֶרֶץ יִשְׂרָאֵל). He emigrated from Galicia in 1946 or 1947 and supported himself by running a small grocery; the dozens of sefarim (סְפָרִים) he produced were composed in the hours he could steal from his shop. His Margaliyos HaYam (מַרְגָּלִיּוֹת הַיָּם) on Sanhedrin (סַנְהֶדְרִין) stands as a required companion to that tractate for anyone learning it seriously; his other works include a sustained study of what motivated Rabbeinu HaKadosh (רַבֵּינוּ הַקָּדוֹשׁ) to write the Mishnah (מִשְׁנָה) at all. It is in that study that the fifth answer is developed.

The Tumultus Iudaicus

The three-and-a-half centuries between the Maccabean revolt and the redaction of the Mishnah were named by Roman historians the Tumultus Iudaicus — “the Judean tumult.” The period was defined by three great Jewish revolts against Roman rule:

  • The Great Revolt (66–73 CE), culminating in the destruction of the Second Temple and the fall of Masada.
  • The Diaspora Revolt, or Kitos War (פּוּלְמוֹס קִיטוֹס) (115–117 CE), which erupted simultaneously in the Jewish communities of Egypt, Cyprus, and Cyrene in Libya.
  • The Bar Kochva Revolt (132–135 CE), in Eretz Yisrael, led by Shimon Bar Kochva whom Rabi Akiva (רַבִּי עֲקִיבָא) proclaimed to be Mashiach (מָשִׁיחַ), and ending after roughly three and a half years in the catastrophe from which Rabi Akiva’s generation did not recover.

Across all three revolts, and in the decades of Roman consolidation that followed each of them, the pattern was the same. Jewish rebels — the biryonim (בִּרְיוֹנִים), of whom the Sicarii of the First Revolt were the most feared — adopted a militant nationalism grounded in the memory of Hasmonean triumph. When their revolts collapsed at home, the survivors dispersed into the great Jewish population centers of the Diaspora: Alexandria in Egypt, the cities of Cyprus, and Cyrene in Libya. Most Jews at this time already lived outside Eretz Yisrael; many families had never returned after the destruction of the First Temple. Into these established communities the failed rebels brought their nationalist ru’ach, and in more than one place they succeeded in kindling a further round of revolt.

Dio Cassius and the Roman Record

The principal non-Talmudic source for this period is the Roman History of Cassius Dio,[18] a Roman historian of Greek origin and a near-contemporary of Rabbeinu HaKadosh. Dio composed eighty books of Roman history; his account of the Diaspora Revolt is inevitably tilted against its Jewish subjects, but no substantive account of the period can be constructed without him.

The Great Synagogue of Alexandria

The magnificence of the Jewish community of Alexandria before its destruction is preserved in the Yerushalmi (יְרוּשַׁלְמִי) and the Bavli (בַּבְלִי).[19] The Great Synagogue of Alexandria was one of the wonders of the ancient Jewish world:

Whoever did not see the synagogue of Alexandria has never seen the glory of Israel.
Sukkah (סֻכָּה) 51b; Yerushalmi Sukkah 5:1

Along the walls stood seventy kateidros shel zahav (קָתֶדְרוֹת שֶׁל זָהָב) — seventy chairs of gold, arranged in memory of the seventy zekenim (זְקֵנִים) of the Sanhedrin. The building was so vast that the assembly could not hear the chazan (חַזָּן)’s voice, and a shamash (שַׁמָּשׁ) would raise a flag at each amen (אָמֵן) so that the tzibbur (צִבּוּר) knew when to respond.

The Massacre at Alexandria

Into this community the Judean rebels of the diaspora period arrived and stirred a great revolt. According to Dio Cassius, the Jews of Alexandria, Cyprus, and Cyrene, once roused, killed some 120,000 gentiles — a figure whose Roman source and Roman bias must be weighed but which points, at the least, to a conflict of catastrophic scale. The Roman response was proportionate to Roman terror: the emperor Trajan dispatched his general Lusius Quietus, from whose name the war acquired its Talmudic designation — the Kitos War — to reduce the diaspora communities.[20] The Gemara (גְּמָרָא)’s narrative names Trajan personally as the agent of destruction; the identification is faithful to the Talmudic register even where Roman sources place the direct action in the hands of his general.

The Gemara’s account of the emperor’s motive is preserved in the Yerushalmi. Trajan had a son who was born on Tisha B’Av; on that day the Jews mourned. He had a daughter who died on Chanukah; on those nights the Jews kindled lights. His wife pressed him: “Before you march out against the barbarians, come first to Alexandria. The Jews mourn what we celebrate; they celebrate what we mourn.”

The Yeshiva of Alexandria: Ki Savo

He came to Alexandria and entered the yeshiva. He asked the assembled scholars what they were learning. They were learning Parshas Ki Savo (פָּרָשַׁת כִּי תָבוֹא):

יִשָּׂא ה' עָלֶיךָ גּוֹי מֵרָחוֹק מִקְצֵה הָאָרֶץ... גּוֹי אֲשֶׁר לֹא תִשְׁמַע לְשׁוֹנוֹ... וְהֵצַר לְךָ בְּכָל שְׁעָרֶיךָ עַד רֶדֶת חוֹמוֹתֶיךָ הַגְּבֹהוֹת וְהַבְּצֻרוֹת.
“Hashem will raise up against you a nation from afar, from the ends of the earth… a nation whose tongue you will not understand… and they will besiege you in all your gates until your high and fortified walls fall.”[21]

To this Trajan replied: “Here I am. That is me.” The entire yeshiva was put to death. The Gemara describes what followed: the slaughter of the men, then the slaughter of their wives when they refused to be taken as spoils. So great was the loss that the sea between Egypt and Cyprus turned red with Jewish blood. The Gemara closes:

At that hour, the horn of Israel was cut off — and it will not be restored until Mashiach ben David, speedily in our days, amen.
Sidebar — A Twentieth-Century Reader of Ki Savo

On September 11, 2001, which fell that year in the week of Parshas Ki Savo, a rebbe in a Brooklyn yeshiva stood on the roof of his beis midrash and watched the smoke rise from lower Manhattan. He returned to his classroom, opened a Chumash (חֻמָּשׁ), and read to his students the tochachah (תּוֹכָחָה): “A nation from afar… a nation whose tongue you will not understand… the high and fortified walls.” He offered no interpretation; only the verses. The impulse to reach for these verses at that moment is itself a testimony to how the yeshiva of Alexandria heard them — the fixed vocabulary in which the Jewish people has always named what has come for it from afar.

Roman Surveillance of Jewish Texts

Two corroborating pieces of evidence support the political reading Rav Margaliyos builds on this history.

The first is the Targum (תַּרְגּוּם) of the Shivim Zekenim (שִׁבְעִים זְקֵנִים) — the Septuagint. The Gemara in Megillah (מְגִלָּה)[22] records that Ptolemy assembled seventy sages in seventy separate chambers and required each independently to translate the Torah into Greek. Eleven identical shinuyim (שִׁנּוּיִים) emerged from all seventy pens, guided by ru’ach ha’kodesh. Among the alterations was the change of “v’es ha’arneves” to “tze’iras raglayim,” because a member of Ptolemy’s family bore the name Arnevet (אַרְנֶבֶת). The translation, once produced, passed to the Roman successors of the Ptolemaic world and served for centuries as the Roman authorities’ window onto Jewish sacred texts.

The second is the striking narrative in Bava Kama (בָּבָא קַמָּא).[23] The malchus (מַלְכוּת) of Rome dispatched shnei sardiotin (שְׁנֵי סַרְדְּיוֹטִין) — two high officers — to sit before the Chachmei Yisrael (חַכְמֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל) and copy out the laws of Bava Kama. The scene is unambiguous: the imperial authorities monitored the halakhic corpus of the Jewish people, and they did so with sufficient interest to send trained officers for the purpose. Whatever Rabbeinu HaKadosh committed to writing would find its way into Roman hands.

The Self-Censorship of the Mishnah

Against this backdrop the fifth answer receives its full force. Chanukah is not, in its historical substance, a religious matter alone. It is the great national festival of the Jewish people. Its narrative arc is the arc of a subjected nation rising against a foreign empire, defeating that empire, and restoring an independent Jewish crown to Eretz Yisrael for more than two centuries. Even in modern times secular Israelis have adopted Chanukah as the foundational national holiday of the state, and they do so accurately: the historical Chanukah is a story of successful national revolt.

For Rabbeinu HaKadosh, composing the Mishnah in the immediate aftermath of the Bar Kochva catastrophe and against the fresh memory of the Kitos War, to grant this festival its own tractate would have been to place, in the hands of an imperial censor already known to monitor Jewish halakhic writing, an authorized codification of the last successful Jewish revolt against a foreign occupier. He refrained. He preserved the halakhos (הֲלָכוֹת) of the festival — scattered across seven tractates, embedded in mishnayos (מִשְׁנָיוֹת) whose primary subject was other — and declined to grant the Chashmonaim (חַשְׁמוֹנָאִים) the structural prominence of a tractate of their own.

Why the Beraisa in Shabbos Was Acceptable

An objection presses immediately. The sugya (סוּגְיָא) of Chanukah does appear in Shabbos 21b. If the Roman concern was so grave, how did the material reach the printed page at all?

The answer draws on a distinction of redaction rather than of date. The Chanukah material in Shabbos is a beraisa (בְּרַיְתָא) — a body of tanna’ic teaching that stands outside the Mishnah’s canonical spine and that entered the written record through the redactional labors of later generations. The Mishnah, by contrast, is the foundational canonical document of the Oral Torah — the text that Rabbeinu HaKadosh himself signed and released. What appears in the Mishnah carries an editorial weight that a beraisa does not; the political stakes of what goes into the canon differ from the stakes of what circulates alongside it. When later generations of tanna’im and amora’im committed the Chanukah beraisa to the redacted text of the Bavli, the acute political pressure that had constrained Rabbeinu HaKadosh had largely eased. The Mishnah’s silence is thus an editorial silence; the Gemara’s eventual speech is a redactional inheritance.

The Sefas Emes: Concealment as the Inner Yesod

The political explanation of the Mishnah’s silence receives, in the teaching of the Sefas Emes,[24] an inner counterpart. Chanukah, in his framing, is the festival of tarpa balbeis (תַּרְפָּ"א בַּלֵּיל) — the festival at which all is concealed, all is b’sod, all awaits the revelation that will come with Mashiach. What Rabbeinu HaKadosh enacted at the editorial level — the deliberate withholding of the festival’s written codification — participates in a deeper truth about the festival itself: that its full meaning belongs to a time not yet come, and that its interim status in the exile is properly one of veiled rather than open commemoration. The political and the mystical are not competing accounts of the Mishnah’s silence; they are two registers of a single teaching about a festival whose disclosure remains partial.

VIII. B’Sha’as HaSakanah: The Window as Inheritance

The concealment that shaped the Mishnah (מִשְׁנָה)’s treatment of Chanukah persists in a small but living halakhic detail. The original obligation of ner Chanukah (נֵר חֲנֻכָּה), as recorded in Shabbos (שַׁבָּת) 21b,[25] is to place the menorah (מְנוֹרָה) at the doorway of the house, opposite the mezuzah (מְזוּזָה), on the side of the reshus ha’rabbim. Where this is not possible, or in a time of danger, the Gemara (גְּמָרָא) permits placing the menorah on the table indoors and considers the obligation discharged. Diaspora communities have inherited the indoor practice by the intermediate step of lighting at the window — a compromise between the ideal outdoor placement and the concealed indoor lighting of b’sha’as ha’sakanah.

In much of Yerushalayim (יְרוּשָׁלַיִם) today the ideal has returned: neighborhoods light in glass menorah boxes at the entrances to their courtyards, k’tikkun. In the diaspora the window remains the norm. Whether this constitutes a permanent l’chatchilah or a b’di’eved that persists on inertia is a live halakhic question; what is not disputed is that the practice preserves in miniature the same discretion that shaped the Mishnah’s silence — a discretion born of the moment when celebrating openly would have provoked those who knew exactly what was being celebrated.

IX. Sefer HaMaccabim and the Yiddish Bridge

The question of sifrei chitzonim (סִפְרֵי חִיצוֹנִים) — the Gemara (גְּמָרָא)’s prohibition against learning “outside books” — has been raised more than once against the practice of consulting Sefer HaMaccabim (סֵפֶר הַמַּכַּבִּים). The prohibition is serious and the concern for it is well-founded, yet the historical record shows that a chain of the most mainstream poskim (פּוֹסְקִים) — the Tosfos Yom Tov (תּוֹסְפוֹת יוֹם טוֹב), the Aruch HaShulchan (עָרוּךְ הַשֻּׁלְחָן), and a long line of Lithuanian and Galician acharonim (אַחֲרוֹנִים) from the eighteenth century onward — cite Sefer HaMaccabim by name in their halakhic writings.

The mechanism by which they could do so has only recently been reconstructed. The original Hebrew of Sefer HaMaccabim was lost; only the Greek translation, preserved in Christian Bibles, survived antiquity. The Engelman edition of Sefer HaMaccabim I (2018) and Sefer HaMaccabim II (late 2024),[26] in its introductory apparatus, traces the survival of the text into the Ashkenazic halakhic world through an eighteenth-century Yiddish translation of the work, now itself preserved in only a handful of copies. It was the Yiddish version, or its intermediate Hebrew back-translations, that supplied the citations found in the Tosfos Yom Tov and his successors. That a chain of mainstream poskim found the work acceptable is itself evidence for its permissibility — not as canonical Torah she’b’al peh, but as a historical source that may be consulted where it clarifies the substance of the tradition.

X. The Sixth Answer: The Remez of the Chenvani

Mattisyahu ben Yochanan: A Question of Identity

The Al HaNissim (עַל הַנִּסִּים) that Klal Yisrael (כְּלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל) recites each day of Chanukah names Mattisyahu ben Yochanan Kohen Gadol (מַתִּתְיָהוּ בֶּן יוֹחָנָן כֹּהֵן גָּדוֹל). The Bnei Yissaschar (בְּנֵי יִשָּׂשכָר)[27] raises a difficulty. The Gemara (גְּמָרָא) in Berachos (בְּרָכוֹת)[28] records that Yochanan Kohen Gadol served in the Kehunah Gedolah (כְּהֻנָּה גְדוֹלָה) for eighty years and at the end of his life turned Tzeduki (צְדוּקִי). If Mattisyahu’s father was this same Yochanan — the one who fell to Tzedukiyus in the closing chapter of his life — why mention him at all in the sacred liturgy of Chanukah? Why not simply say “Mattisyahu Kohen Gadol”?

Historically, it is possible that the two are not the same Yochanan. Chronologically the identification is difficult and modern historians have generally distinguished them. The Bnei Yissaschar nevertheless takes as his working assumption that they are one and the same, and accordingly develops several answers to his own difficulty. The most beautiful of these answers is the remez (רֶמֶז) that follows.

Eight Mentions in Shas

The word Chanukah appears in the Mishnah (מִשְׁנָה) — despite the absence of a tractate bearing its name — some seven times, with an additional appearance of the term Chashmonai (חַשְׁמוֹנַאי). The count is eight — corresponding, as is traditional to notice, to the eight days of the festival.[29] The mishnah (מִשְׁנָה) that concerns this study is one of these eight: the mishnah at the end of perek HaKonis (פֶּרֶק הַכּוֹנֵס) in Bava Kama (בָּבָא קַמָּא).[30]

The Mishnah of the Camel and the Storekeeper

A man drives a camel laden with pishtan (פִּשְׁתָּן), flax, through the reshus ha’rabbim. A chenvani (חֶנְוָנִי) — a shopkeeper — has arranged his wares on the stoop of his store, with a candle burning to illuminate them. The overflowing flax catches fire from the candle; the camel is engulfed; the store burns. Who bears the loss?

  • If the shopkeeper’s candle was inside the store, the camel-driver pays: his overladen animal had no business projecting into the storefront.
  • If the candle was outside in the public way, the camel-driver is patur (פָּטוּר): the shopkeeper had no business kindling a flame in the reshus ha’rabbim.
  • But if the candle outside is a ner Chanukah (נֵר חֲנֻכָּה), the camel-driver pays: every Jew knows that on the nights of Chanukah candles stand at the doorways of the town, and the driver ought to have steered his laden animal wide.

The Bircas HaShalom’s Remez

The Bircas HaShalom[31] — a sefer of a Chassidishe tzaddik of the generation after the Baal Shem Tov (בַּעַל שֵׁם טוֹב), deep, elevated, and little known — reads this mishnah as a coded testimony to Yochanan Kohen Gadol’s fall and to the tikkun (תִּקּוּן) effected by his sons. The equation that opens the reading is precise:

חֶנְוָנִי   =   יוֹחָנָן
The letters of chenvani and Yochanan are the same.

To see the drash (דְּרָשׁ), one must recall a second sugya (סוּגְיָא). The Gemara in Yoma (יוֹמָא)[32] records the first ma’aseh of the Tzedukim (צְדוּקִים). The Kohen Gadol on Yom Kippur (יוֹם כִּפּוּר), before entering the Kodesh HaKodashim (קֹדֶשׁ הַקֳּדָשִׁים), must place the coals of the fire on the kaf (כַּף) of the ketores (קְטֹרֶת) within. The Tzedukim, on the strength of a private drasha (דְּרָשָׁה), insisted that the fire be kindled outside, before the Kohen Gadol entered. The first Kohen Gadol to accede to their reading was Yochanan Kohen Gadol, in the closing chapter of his eighty-year service; his acquiescence gave the Tzedukim their first great public triumph. The Gemara pronounces its verdict: chayav (חַיָּב). Anyone who performs the avodah (עֲבוֹדָה) in this manner is liable.

Now the two sugyos may be laid side by side. Yochanan’s cheit (חֵטְא) was a fire placed outside where the halakhah (הֲלָכָה) required it to be inside. His descendants — the Chashmonaim (חַשְׁמוֹנָאִים) — enacted a takanah (תַּקָּנָה) that placed a fire outside where earlier practice had known no such placement at all: the ner Chanukah at the doorway, on the reshus ha’rabbim. On the surface the two acts appear to share the same posture; in halakhic substance they are opposites. The Tzedukim’s fire-outside was an assertion of private drasha against the received tradition of Chazal (חֲזַ"ל). The Chashmonaim’s fire-outside was a gezerah (גְּזֵרָה) of the sages, publicly enacted in the reshus ha’rabbim as the visible declaration of the very koach d’rabbanan that the Tzedukim had rejected. Where the grandfather’s outdoor fire had inaugurated the ascendancy of the sectarian challenge, the grandsons’ outdoor fire proclaimed the authority of Chazal.

To this the mishnah in Bava Kama speaks with a single word: patur. In its plain sense the term is a technical tort ruling — the camel-driver bears no liability. In the Bircas HaShalom’s reading it opens onto a deeper meaning. Yochanan himself is patur: the cheit of the grandfather has been redeemed by the tikkun of the descendants; the outdoor fire that inaugurated the Tzedukim’s triumph has been answered by the outdoor fire that vindicates the koach of Chazal. The drash reads a technical patur into a theological absolution — a homiletical move whose textual anchor is the chenvani’s presence in the very mishnah that adjudicates ner Chanukah. On this reading the Bnei Yissaschar’s difficulty resolves. Yochanan Kohen Gadol may be named in the liturgy of Chanukah precisely because Chanukah itself is his rehabilitation; the mention of the father is inseparable from the memory of what the sons accomplished on his behalf.

XI. Which Answers Stand?

Six answers have been arrayed. It remains to ask which of them survive.

The first answer — the Vilna Gaon (הַגְּרָ"א)’s mesorah (מְסוֹרָה) of a lost Maseches Chanukah (מַסֶּכֶת חֲנֻכָּה) — is best treated as an unresolved tzurech iyun (צָרִיךְ עִיּוּן). It rests on a chain of oral tradition that need not answer to ordinary evidentiary criteria, and the difficulty from the absence of cross-references is real without being decisive.

The second answer — the reported Chasam Sofer (חֲתַם סוֹפֵר)’s vendetta — falls with its premise when the Sforno (סְפוֹרְנוֹ)’s reading of “Lo yasur” is accepted. On the Sforno’s account there was no cheit (חֵטְא) for Rabbeinu HaKadosh (רַבֵּינוּ הַקָּדוֹשׁ) to hold against the Chashmonaim (חַשְׁמוֹנָאִים), and the Rambam (רַמְבַּ"ם)’s explicit praise of them confirms the direction.

The third answer — the l’chadeish principle — is genuine but partial. It explains why the composer of the Mishnah (מִשְׁנָה) could have refrained from a Chanukah tractate without any editorial cost; it does not by itself determine why he did so.

The fourth answer — the existence of Megillas Taanis (מְגִלַּת תַּעֲנִית) — is likewise genuine and independent of the others. It reduces the pressure of the kushya (קֻשְׁיָא) by observing that the historical and halakhic corpus was already b’kesav; it does not exhaust the question but it contributes to any adequate account.

The fifth answer — Rav Margaliyos’s political reading — is the deepest and, on the evidence assembled here, the load-bearing account. The three-and-a-half centuries of the Tumultus Iudaicus, the destruction of Alexandrian Jewry, the Roman surveillance of halakhic texts attested by the shnei sardiotin (שְׁנֵי סַרְדְּיוֹטִין): these are not decorative context. They are the historical situation in which the Mishnah was composed, and they render intelligible in a single stroke both the absence of a Chanukah tractate and the presence of Chanukah material in the later, safer, redactional layers of Shabbos (שַׁבָּת). The Sefas Emes (שְׂפַת אֱמֶת)’s teaching of Chanukah as the festival of concealment stands as the inner counterpart of the same account: political discretion in the outer world; tarpa balbeis (תַּרְפָּ"א בַּלֵּיל) in the inner one.

The sixth teaching — the Bircas HaShalom (בִּרְכַּת הַשָּׁלוֹם)’s remez (רֶמֶז) — is not an alternative to the other answers but their crown. The Mishnah that Rabbeinu HaKadosh did compose contains, in its treatment of the camel and the chenvani (חֶנְוָנִי), a coded testimony to precisely what he declined to codify openly: that Chanukah is the redemption of a priestly line, the vindication of koach d’rabbanan, the exemption of a father through the merit of his sons. What the tractate could not say the mishnah (מִשְׁנָה) whispers.

To this whisper the opening of the study responds. Erev Zos Chanukah (זֹאת חֲנֻכָּה) is the day of the last outstretched hand — the day on which teshuvah (תְּשׁוּבָה) is received after all other gates have closed. The Bircas HaShalom’s Yochanan is a fitting emblem: the father whose failing was answered, generations later, by the fidelity of his descendants. The Chanukah lights kindled in the doorways of the exile are the sons’ hand outstretched to receive the father. That is the note on which Zos Chanukah ends the year, and it is the note on which this study must end as well.

The principle: Rabbeinu HaKadosh’s method in the Mishnah was l’chadeish, to record what was not already universally practiced; Chanukah’s halakhos had settled so deeply into Jewish domestic life by the redaction of the Mishnah that no tikun b’kesav was required. ↩
  14. Rambam, Pirush HaMishnayos, Menachos, perek 4, mishnah 1. — The yesod that supplies the halakhic backing for the third answer: there is no mishnah anywhere that mandates the twice-daily recitation of Krias Shema, the affixing of a mezuzah, or the daily wearing of tefillin. These were universal Jewish practices and required no formal codification. The same principle, applied to Chanukah, is developed by the Chasam Sofer (fn 13). ↩
  15. Kiddushin 72b. — The Gemara’s statement that Rabi Yehudah HaNasi was born on the day Rabi Akiva died. Combined with the standard dating of Rabi Akiva’s death to roughly 135 CE and the Maccabean revolt to 167 BCE, this yields an interval of approximately three centuries between the revolt and Rabi’s birth, with an additional five decades to the redaction of the Mishnah — roughly three hundred and fifty years in all. ↩
  16. Megillas Taanis — the earliest written work of Torah she’b’al peh. — A calendar in leshon ha’kodesh of the yamim tovim ketanim on which hesped and ta’anis are forbidden. Quoted throughout Bavli and Yerushalmi; predates the Mishnah. The majority of its content concerns the victories of the Chashmonaim, including sustained treatment of Chanukah and its halakhos. Its existence in written form when Rabbeinu HaKadosh sat to compose the Mishnah reduces the pressure of the kushya: the corpus was already accessible b’kesav. ↩
  17. Rav Reuven Margaliyos (1889–1971), Yesod HaMishnah V’Arichaso; cf. Margaliyos HaYam on Sanhedrin. — The sustained study of the historical motivations behind Rabbeinu HaKadosh’s composition of the Mishnah. Rav Margaliyos, who emigrated from Galicia to Eretz Yisrael in 1946 or 1947 and supported himself as a shopkeeper, produced dozens of sefarim in his hours of leisure. His Margaliyos HaYam on Sanhedrin incorporates classical Roman and Greek historical sources where they illuminate the substance of the sugya; it is required companion reading for anyone learning that tractate in depth. ↩
  18. Source-type note (Roman historian)
    Cassius Dio Cocceianus (c. 155–235 CE), Roman History, especially Books 68–69. A near-contemporary of Rabbeinu HaKadosh; the author of an eighty-book Roman history of which substantial portions survive. His descriptions of the Diaspora Revolt (115–117 CE) and of the destruction of the Alexandrian, Cypriot, and Cyrenian Jewish communities constitute the principal extant non-Talmudic record of these events; his 120,000-gentile death toll must be weighed against the systematic Roman-source bias in his account. Cited here as a historical source, not as sifrei kodesh.
    ↩
  19. Yerushalmi Sukkah 5:1; Bavli Sukkah 51b. — The Great Synagogue of Alexandria: “mi she’lo ra’ah… lo ra’ah b’hodah shel Yisrael.” Seventy kateidros shel zahav in memory of the seventy zekenim of the Sanhedrin; the shamash’s flag as signal for the tzibbur’s amen in a building too vast for the chazan’s voice to carry. ↩
  20. Yerushalmi Sukkah 5:1; Bavli Sukkah 51b — Trajan’s massacre of Alexandrian Jewry. — The Gemara narrates the personal trigger (Trajan’s son born on Tisha B’Av; his daughter died on Chanukah), the wife’s incitement, the destruction of the Alexandrian community, and the closing declaration “karno shel Yisrael nigdah” — the horn of Israel cut off until Mashiach ben David. Roman sources place the direct execution of the campaign in the hands of Trajan’s general Lusius Quietus, from whose name the war acquired its Talmudic designation as the Kitos War; the Gemara’s attribution of the action to Trajan himself is faithful to the Talmudic register (in which the emperor bears responsibility for what his agents perform in his name). ↩
  21. Devarim 28:49–52 — tochachah of Parshas Ki Savo. — The foreign nation from afar, the unintelligible tongue, the high and fortified walls — the parshah the yeshiva of Alexandria was learning when Trajan entered. ↩
  22. Megillah 9a. — The translation of the Torah for Ptolemy; the seventy sages in separate chambers; the eleven identical shinuyim inserted by ru’ach ha’kodesh. Among them, the substitution of “tze’iras raglayim” for “v’es ha’arneves” in deference to a member of Ptolemy’s family bearing the name Arnevet; the traditional gloss identifying that family member with the line of Amalek is homiletical and appears in the surrounding mesorah rather than in the plain sugya. The Roman inheritance of the Targum is what makes the episode relevant to the political-surveillance reading developed in this study. ↩
  23. Bava Kama 38a. — The malchus dispatching shnei sardiotin (variant: shnei istrategin) — two high-ranking officers — to copy the laws of Bava Kama from the Chachmei Yisrael. Direct Talmudic attestation to imperial surveillance of Jewish halakhic material; the anchor of Rav Margaliyos’s political reading of the Mishnah’s silence on Chanukah. ↩
  24. Sefas Emes, Likutim, Chanukah. — The teaching of Chanukah as tarpa balbeis: all concealed, all b’sod, all awaiting the disclosure of Mashiach. The mystical counterpart of the political reading — Chazal’s deliberate suppression of the national-redemption narrative in galus is here framed as the outward face of a deeper spiritual necessity of veiling. ↩
  25. Shabbos 21b–22a. — “Ner Chanukah mitzvah l’hanicha al pesach beiso mi’bachutz… u’visha’as ha’sakanah, manicha al shulchano, v’dai lo.” The original obligation to place the menorah at the doorway on the side of reshus ha’rabbim; the heter for indoor placement b’sha’as ha’sakanah. The diaspora practice of lighting at the window is the intermediate inheritance between these two poles. ↩
  26. Source-type note (modern critical edition)
    Engelman publishing house, Sefer HaMaccabim I (Eretz Yisrael, 2018), Sefer HaMaccabim II (late 2024). A Hebrew critical edition with maps, illustrations, and a full apparatus documenting every early Ashkenazic authority that cites the sefer. Its introduction reconstructs the eighteenth-century Yiddish translation, now nearly lost, through which the text reached the Tosfos Yom Tov, the Aruch HaShulchan, and their Litvish and Galician successors.
    ↩
  27. Bnei Yissaschar, Kuntres on Chanukah. — The kushya of mentioning Yochanan Kohen Gadol by name in the Chanukah liturgy despite his end-of-life turn to Tzedukiyus; the rejection of the possibility that the two Yochanans are distinct; the several teirutzim, chief among them the tikkun effected through his descendants, developed in this study through the Bircas HaShalom’s remez. ↩
  28. Berachos 29a. — “Yochanan Kohen Gadol shimeish bi’kehunah gedolah shemonim shanah, u’l’sof na’aseh tzeduki.” The source of the mishnah’s warning “al ta’amin b’atzmecha ad yom mosecha.” The textual basis for the identification of Mattisyahu’s father with the Kohen Gadol whose end-of-life turn to Tzedukiyus generates the Bnei Yissaschar’s difficulty. ↩
  29. The eight mentions of Chanukah / Chashmonai in the Mishnah. — The seven appearances of the word Chanukah and the single appearance of Chashmonai in the Mishnah are distributed across Bikkurim, Rosh HaShanah, Ta’anis, Megillah, Middos, Bava Kama, and Menachos (locations vary by mishnaic edition and by whether the term appears in the mishnah proper or in accompanying beraisos). The count of eight corresponds to the eight days of the festival — a symmetry noted in the Chassidishe tradition and preserved here as a matter worth recording. ↩
  30. Mishnah, Bava Kama 6:6 (end of perek HaKonis). — The mishnah of the camel laden with pishtan and the chenvani’s candle. Three rulings: candle inside — camel-driver pays; candle outside in reshus ha’rabbim — camel-driver patur; ner Chanukah outside — camel-driver pays. This mishnah is the anchor of the Bircas HaShalom’s remez developed in Section X. ↩
  31. Source-type note (Chassidic, elevated register)
    Bircas HaShalom — a work of a Chassidishe tzaddik of the generation following the Baal Shem Tov, described in the tradition as very gehoiben and not widely disseminated. The identification of chenvani with Yochanan is the anchoring equation; the drash that reads the mishnah’s patur as the theological absolution of Yochanan Kohen Gadol through the tikkun of his descendants proceeds from the surface tort ruling into a homiletical register whose textual anchor is the chenvani’s presence in the very mishnah that adjudicates ner Chanukah. Cited by the Rabbi in his own research on the Bnei Yissaschar’s teirutzim.
    ↩
  32. Yoma 19b and 53a. — The narrative of Yochanan Kohen Gadol’s avodah with the ketores: the Tzedukim’s insistence, on the basis of a private drasha, that the fire be kindled outside before entering the Kodesh HaKodashim; his acquiescence as the first great public triumph of the Tzedukim; the Gemara’s ruling “chayav” against anyone performing the avodah in this manner. The Bircas HaShalom’s drash: the Chashmonaim’s ner Chanukah reverses precisely this act — a fire brought outside rightly, as the public vindication of koach d’rabbanan. ↩

This edition incorporates the structural and editorial recommendations of the Shiur Review and Editorial Review: the six answers are arrayed in ascending order and expressly weighed against one another in a closing synthesis; the Section-V header confusion has been resolved by folding the Rambam material into the Sforno’s answer as a single unit; the Trajan / Kitos-War chronology has been corrected (the Diaspora Revolt of 115–117 CE preceded the Bar Kochva Revolt of 132–135 CE, and the specific narrative of Bar Kochva survivors seeding the Kitos War has been retired in favor of the more general observation that Judean rebels dispersed into the diaspora across the successive failed revolts); the length of the Bar Kochva Revolt has been corrected to roughly three and a half years; a note has been added on Roman sources placing the direct execution of the Alexandrian campaign in the hands of Lusius Quietus; the 9/11 anecdote has been moved to a sidebar outside the Trajan climax; the Sefas Emes teaching has been folded into the political-reading section as its inner counterpart; the b’sha’as ha’sakanah discussion has been tightened; a coda has been added returning to the outstretched hand of the opening.
