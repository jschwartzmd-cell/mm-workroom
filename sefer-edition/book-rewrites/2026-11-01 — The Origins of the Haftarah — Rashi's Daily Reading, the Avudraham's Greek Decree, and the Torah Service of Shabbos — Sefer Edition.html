מְקוֹרוֹת הַהַפְטָרָה — קְרִיאַת הַנָּבִיא בְּכָל יוֹם וּגְזֵרַת יָוָן, וְסֵדֶר הוֹצָאַת סֵפֶר תּוֹרָה בְּשַׁבָּת

The Origins of the Haftarah — Rashi’s Daily Reading, the Avudraham’s Greek Decree, and the Torah Service of Shabbos
Studies in the Shabbos Tefillos (תפילות) — From the Song of the Day and the Opening of the Ark to Atah Hareisa (אתה הראת), the Mi Sheberach (מי שברך), and the Two Ancient Accounts of the Haftarah (הפטרה)
21 Cheshvan 5787  ·  Sefer Edition

This discourse was delivered לעילוי נשמת Miriam bas Chaim Yitzchak (מרים בת חיים יצחק); the morning’s study and breakfast were sponsored by a friend of the congregation on the occasion of her yahrzeit (יארצייט).[1] The lecturer asks that her soul have an aliyah (עלייה) and that her merit bring salvation (ישועה) to all Israel.

Whence comes the haftarah (הפטרה)? The discourse sets out two ancient answers that differ as sharply as two answers can. The first, ascribed to Rashi (Rashi (רש״י)) in the Sefer HaPardes (ספר הפרדס), holds that a chapter of the Prophets was once read every day after prayer, and that the practice survives on Shabbos (שבת) alone because poverty brought it to an end on weekdays. The second, that of the Avudraham (אבודרהם), holds that the haftarah is a substitute, instituted when a Greek decree forbade the reading of the Torah (תורה) and measured by the seven aliyos (עליות) it replaced; on the testimony of Eliyahu Bachur (אליהו בחור) (Elijah Levita) and the Levush (לבוש), that decree was the decree of Antiochus, and it extended to the Writings as well as to the Torah. Because the lecturer reaches the question by way of the service that precedes the reading, the discourse first passes through the song of the day, the opening of the ark, the verses of Masechet Soferim (מסכת סופרים) and the Or Zarua (אור זרוע) who brought them into the siddur, Atah Hareisa (אתה הראת), and the mi sheberach (מי שברך) said after each aliyah. The lecturer sets the two accounts side by side and does not declare for either.

I. The Song of the Day and Its Place

The study of the Shabbos tefillos (תפילות) having reached the end of Shacharis (שחרית), one item remains before the Torah is read: the shir shel yom (שיר של יום), the psalm of the day. On a weekday the congregation says “Today is the first day of the week,” and so on through the week. On Shabbos it says היום יום שבת קודש, שבו היו הלוים אומרים בבית המקדש, “Today is the holy Sabbath, on which the Levites used to say in the Temple,” and then Psalm 92, מזמור שיר ליום השבת.[2]

The rite the lecturer follows, Nusach Sefard (נוסח ספרד), says the psalm of the day after Shacharis; Nusach Ashkenaz (נוסח אשכנז) says it after Musaf (מוסף). The difference is the more striking because both rites have already said Psalm 92 among the Pesukei DeZimrah (פסוקי דזמרה), and Nusach Sefard nevertheless goes out of its way to say it again, as the psalm of the day, after Shacharis.

The lecturer calls the Ashkenazic placement a peleh (פלא), a wonder. In the Temple, Shacharis corresponds to the tamid shel shachar (תמיד של שחר), the daily morning offering. After that offering the Kohanim (כהנים) brought the libations (nesachim (נסכים)) that accompany every tamid (תמיד), and while they did so the Levites (לויים) stood on the platform (דוכן) and sang the psalm of the day. The psalm therefore belongs to the morning offering, and there is no reason to defer it to Musaf. On the contrary: whenever a Musaf offering was brought, on Rosh Chodesh (ראש חודש), Shabbos and Yom Tov (יום טוב), the Levites sang a special psalm for it. The Magen Avraham (Magen Avraham (מגן אברהם)), as the lecturer understands him, therefore finds the Ashkenazic custom senseless, since the psalm said after Musaf is the wrong one; and the Aruch HaShulchan (Aruch HaShulchan (ערוך השלחן)) wonders why the Musaf psalm is not said at all.[3]

The lecturer answers that Masechet Soferim, a minor tractate printed after Avodah Zarah (עבודה זרה), sets out the psalm for each Musaf: Rosh Chodesh, Shabbos, Yom Tov, Shavuos (שבועות), Sukkos (סוכות) and Pesach (פסח).[4] A further suggestion from the room, that each psalm is designated to its day and so would not be called “of the day” at Musaf, leaves the lecturer unsatisfied: the Musaf psalm could still be added.

The same reasoning raises a second question. The afternoon offering, the tamid shel bein ha’arbayim (תמיד של בין הערבים), also had its libation and its song; why is no psalm of the day said after Minchah (מנחה)? The Magen Avraham answers that the afternoon service of the Temple was so crowded with offerings that the libation sometimes fell after sunset (shkiah (שקיעה)), and the law is that when the libation falls after sunset the Levites do not sing at night. Since the song might therefore not have been sung, it is not said at Minchah.[5]

The lecturer was told of Ashkenazic congregations that have moved the psalm earlier, and of one that moved both the psalm of the day and Anim Zemiros (אנעים זמירות) to follow Shacharis, not for the Magen Avraham’s reason but for decorum. After Musaf there was noise and conversation, and worshippers were folding their talleisim (טליתים) before the prayer was over: “You have not finished davening; speak to the Almighty.” Placed earlier, the songs could be said with the respect due them.[6] In the lecturer’s judgment the practice of saying the psalm after Shacharis is the correct one.

II. Opening the Ark

The reading of the Torah on Shabbos differs in several respects from the weekday reading, which the series treated earlier. First, a custom of the lecturer’s congregation that not every congregation keeps: on Simchas Torah (שמחת תורה), when the honors are sold, one man buys the right to take the Torah out of the ark for the whole year. When that man yields the honor, it goes to a bridegroom, or to a man whose wife is expecting.

The custom has a source in the Or Zarua, one of the great Rishonim (ראשונים): Rav Yitzchak ben Moshe of Vienna, of the thirteenth century, whose work records a great many halachos. He writes that it is a great merit (zechus (זכות)) to buy the hotzaas sefer Torah (הוצאת ספר תורה), and an honor to the Torah (כבוד התורה).[7]

For the man whose wife is in her ninth month the lecturer cites the Chida (Chida (חיד״א)), who brings it as an ancient custom (minhag vasikin (מנהג ותיקין)). The idea is symbolic: the wife will need the opening of the womb (פתיחת הרחם), and so the husband opens the ark; and the opening of the ark is an auspicious time (zman segulah (זמן מסוגל)), a time of favor (eis ratzon (עת רצון)) for prayer.[8] The lecturer stands beside the ark when a young man comes up for the opening and tells him: do not merely open it and take out the Torah, but pray; pray for children, and that the child be a ben Torah (בן תורה). For a daughter the lecturer is unsure what to say, and a voice from the room suggests that she marry a talmid chacham (תלמיד חכם).

Why a bridegroom (chasan (חתן)) receives the opening is not given. The suggestions from the room, that it is the same auspiciousness or that it is a preparation for a mitzvah (hechsher mitzvah (הכשר מצוה)), are met with the lecturer’s “perhaps” (קען זיין).

III. The Verses of Taking Out the Torah: Soferim and the Customs of Eretz Yisrael

On a weekday, as the Torah is taken out, the congregation says Kel Erech Apayim (קל ארך אפים) or a few verses such as אין כמוך, and then Vayehi Binsoa HaAron (ויהי בנסוע הארן); on Shabbos the opening differs. The lecturer asks where these verses come from, and answers from the history of the siddur.

Among the masechtos ketanos (מסכתות קטנות), the minor tractates printed after Avodah Zarah, stands Masechet Soferim, “the tractate of the scribes.” It contains many rulings found in neither the Bavli (בבלי) nor the Yerushalmi (ירושלמי), and scholars are still unsettled as to when and by whom it was written, when it was first circulated, and whence its customs derive. The lecturer, who is reading a book on the subject by a professor in Eretz Yisrael (ארץ ישראל), concludes from what he has read that Soferim is primarily a record of the ancient customs of Eretz Yisrael (מנהגי ארץ ישראל), which stood in a great dispute with the customs of Babylonia (מנהגי בבל).[9]

In Eretz Yisrael, it seems, a long series of verses was said as the Torah was taken from the ark, some by the chazzan (חזן) and answered by the congregation, some said by the chazzan and repeated twice by the congregation. The lecturer says that to practice what Soferim prescribes whenever the Torah is taken out on Shabbos and Yom Tov would add as much as an hour to the service. Brich Shmei (בריך שמיה) is not in Soferim; it comes from the Zohar (זוהר). Our own text is a much abbreviated version: some of its verses are found in Soferim, and others were added. ויהי בנסוע הארן, said whenever the Torah is taken out, is not among those of Soferim.

The Or Zarua and the Restoration of the Soferim Verses

The Or Zarua was, in the lecturer’s words, one of the Baalei HaTosafos (בעלי התוספות) and the chief rabbi of Vienna, and the lecturer tells why his book bears its name. Writing a get (גט), a bill of divorce, for a man named Akiva, the author had to decide whether the name ends in an alef (אלף) or a hei (ה׳). The lecturer reports that the Yerushalmi and the Tosefta (תוספתא), which originate in Eretz Yisrael, write the name עקיבה with a hei, while the Bavli and the Mishnah (משנה) write עקיבא with an alef. A get with one letter wrong is invalid, and the case is that of a married woman, an eshes ish (אשת איש); the matter is not light.[10]

The author relates in his introduction that he was shown in a dream, from Heaven, the verse אור זרוע לצדיק ולישרי לב שמחה (Tehillim (תהלים) 97:11). The final letters of its words spell ר-ע-ק-י-ב-ה, “R. Akiva” ending in a hei, because the last word, שמחה, ends in a hei:

אוֹר זָרֻעַ לַצַּדִּיק וּלְיִשְׁרֵי לֵב שִׂמְחָה
Light is sown for the righteous, and gladness for the upright in heart.
Tehillim 97:11 (the final letters marked; see note 10)

He wrote the get accordingly, and, so fond was he of the revelation, named his book after the verse. The gittin question itself, including whether some authorities write two gittin, is beyond the scope of the discourse.[11]

The Or Zarua matters here because the custom of the Torah service is traced to him. He writes that in the course of his travels in Ashkenaz (אשכנז) and Poland in the thirteenth century he found that in the region he calls Medinas Reinus (מדינת ריינוס), the Rhineland, nothing at all was said when the Torah was taken out: after Shacharis the congregation walked to the ark, took out the scroll, carried it to the bimah (בימה), and began to read. He urges that the custom follow the ancient custom of Eretz Yisrael found in Soferim, in an abbreviated form of a few verses, and that is what is said.[12]

The lecturer gives a second account, from Italy, which he does not reconcile with the first. The Shibbolei HaLeket (שבלי הלקט), much cited by the Beis Yosef (Beis Yosef (בית יוסף)) in the laws of prayer, was written by the son of a physician, Rav Avraham the doctor (רב אברהם הרופא), a leading halachic authority and head of a yeshiva in Rome. (The lecturer gives the author’s name hesitantly: “Rav Avraham ben Tzidki, I think Tzidkiyahu ben Avraham HaRofe.”) The family belonged to the ענוים, one of the four families which, by a legend recorded by the Rishonim, were noble families of Jerusalem exiled to Rome by Titus at the destruction of the Second Temple. Many Rishonim and halachic authorities descend from them, and their rulings carry weight because they preserved without change the customs of Eretz Yisrael they brought with them.[13] In Rome, as in the Rhineland, nothing was said when the Torah was taken out; Rav Avraham proposed that the custom of Soferim be restored, and from that time, the lecturer says, Israel everywhere has said these verses. The two accounts need not conflict: they describe the same restoration urged in two lands at the same period, the Or Zarua for Ashkenaz and Rav Avraham for Italy, and the discourse takes the Or Zarua, who is cited by name for the custom, as the principal witness. The reconciliation is the editor’s.

The verses are all from Scripture, from David in Tehillim and elsewhere. The lecturer does not know exactly what the Sefardim (ספרדים) say as the Torah is taken out, and thinks they say more verses.

IV. Atah Hareisa and Vayehi Binsoa HaAron

In place of the weekday verses, Nusach Sefard on Shabbos begins with אתה הראת לדעת. The weekday Kel Erech Apayim speaks of pardon and forgiveness (מחילה וסליחה), a weekday matter; Atah Hareisa is said of the giving of the Torah (Matan Torah (מתן תורה)), in Parashas Vaeschanan (פרשת ואתחנן). Since the Torah was given on Shabbos, the verse is said before the Torah is taken out on Shabbos, to recall that fact. The lecturer cites the Tur (Tur (טור)) in siman 281.[14]

אַתָּה הָרְאֵתָ לָדַעַת כִּי יְהוָה הוּא הָאֱלֹהִים אֵין עוֹד מִלְּבַדּוֹ
You have been shown, that you may know that Hashem (השם), He is God; there is none else besides Him.
Devarim 4:35

This is one more division of the kind the discourse keeps meeting between Nusach Sefard and Nusach Ashkenaz. The Tur lived in Spain in the second half of his life and reports that in Spain Atah Hareisa is said; Nusach Sefard adopted the custom. Nusach Ashkenaz, as the lecturer recalls it (he notes he has not prayed Nusach Ashkenaz for a long time), does not say it at all, but says אין כמוך. The other weekday verses are left out on Shabbos.

Then comes ויהי בנסע הארן. As noted, it is not in Soferim, but it is most fitting: the Torah is being carried out of the ark, and the ark is in motion.[15] Then בריך שמיה, treated earlier in the series.

V. The Mi Sheberach After Each Aliyah: The Pledge

On Shabbos a מי שברך is said after every aliyah; on Monday and Thursday there is none, except for a woman who has given birth (yoledes (יולדת)) or one who is ill. Whence the custom of interrupting the reading? The lecturer answers: “For money.”

The Midrash (מדרש), in Vayikra Rabbah (ויקרא רבה) and Kohelet Rabbah (קהלת רבה), reflects an ancient practice that, in the merit of the Torah open on the bimah, the man called up was blessed: מי שברך אבותינו אברהם יצחק ויעקב. The point of the mi sheberach, the lecturer says, is the gift (matanah (מתנה)): the pledge made at that moment.[16]

A Warning About Pledges The lecturer will not repeat what the Midrash says of one who pledges charity publicly (פוסק צדקה ברבים) and does not pay the congregation what he promised at the mi sheberach, “because it is very frightening.” The Gemara (גמרא)’s own statement is that for the sin of unfulfilled vows (בעוון נדרים) a man’s wife and children die: Shabbos 32b (the lecturer ascribed it to the Gemara in Nedarim (נדרים), but the passage is in Shabbos).[17] One who makes a vow, above all a public one, and does not keep it, transgresses מוצא שפתיך תשמור and לא יחל דברו ככל היוצא מפיו יעשה. The gabbai (גבאי) therefore does well to add בלי נדר, “without a vow.”

The lecturer adds a point of practice. The formula that a gabbai says when giving an aliyah is בעבור שיתן מתנה to the synagogue, “because he will give a gift”; the formula בעבור שצוה לברכם, “because he commanded to bless them,” empties the mi sheberach of its point, and is how rabbis suppose they can excuse themselves from pledging. The first, he says, was the original custom, though he gives no source for the claim.

VI. The Mi Sheberach for the Sick on Shabbos

The מי שברך לחולים, for the sick, receives particular attention in the lecturer’s congregation. A woman in the women’s section once told him that she comes to synagogue on Shabbos morning looking forward to this moment above all: she has a list of the sick for whom she prays, and she asks why everyone is talking at that time. The gabbai now raps and announces a call for quiet before the mi sheberach for the sick. People wait the whole week to say it while the Torah is open, so that the merit of the Torah (זכות התורה) may bring healing.

But how may healing be sought on Shabbos, when the Shabbos Amidah has no Refaenu (רפאנו) and individual requests are not made, since on Shabbos it is as though all one’s work were done (כאילו כל מלאכתך עשויה) and the day is יום שכולו שבת, יום שכולו מנוחה לחיי העולמים? The lecturer reports that the Beis Yosef was asked this and answered, in a responsum in Avkas Rochel (אבקת רוכל), that a mi sheberach is not a prayer (tefillah (תפלה)) for the sick but a blessing (berachah (ברכה)): the family is told that the congregation is thinking of the patient and that the merit of the Torah should bring healing, a simple blessing and not a request. The custom is therefore justified; as the Beis Yosef concludes, הנח להם לישראל, let Israel follow it.[18]

VII. The Haftarah: One Question, Two Origins

On Shabbos seven aliyos are read, as the series treated in connection with Monday and Thursday, and then the haftarah. The word is הפטרה, from מפטיר, and not הפטורה, as many say; the error, the lecturer remarks, is of no great consequence. Where the reading comes from, and why it is read, is the subject of what he calls a very radical dispute, extreme from one end to the other, among the Rishonim. He adds, as he does of each part of the discourse, that this is all Jewish history.

VIII. The First Origin: Rashi and a Daily Reading of the Prophets

Rashi, in the Sefer HaPardes (a work which quotes much from him, and is not his commentary on the Talmud or on the Torah), says that in earlier times a chapter of the Prophets was read every single day after prayer, and not only on the days of the Torah reading. After davening the congregation closed the siddur and sat down to read a chapter of Navi (נביא). The purpose was that before going to work a man should have read Scripture (Mikra (מקרא), the Chumash (חומש)), Prophets, Mishnah and Beraisa (ברייתא), and Gemara.[19]

The lecturer shows how much of this the daily prayers already contain. Mikra is fulfilled by the Shema (שמע) (Kerias Shema (קריאת שמע)): the Gemara in Menachos (מנחות) (99b; the lecturer gave the daf as צ) teaches that one who recites the Shema morning and evening has fulfilled והגית בו יומם ולילה, though it may not be said in the presence of the unlearned (amei haaretz (עמי הארצות)), who would conclude that they need do no more.[20] Mishnah is fulfilled by איזהו מקומן and by אלו דברים after the blessings on the Torah (Birchos HaTorah (ברכות התורה)); Beraisa by רבי ישמעאל אומר and the other beraisos said through the service. Prophets remain. “When do we say Navi?” The lecturer, prompted from the room, points to ובא לציון גואל, a verse of Yeshayahu (ישעיהו).

So the ancient custom, according to Rashi, was to read a chapter of the Prophets daily; “you would happily be well versed in Tanach (תנ״ך) if we did this every day.” The lecturer gives Rashi’s list as follows: Mikra, Prophets, Mishnah and Beraisa, with Gemara added at the first listing (the lecturer’s wording at that word is unclear); at a second listing he gives Mikra, Prophets and Writings, Mishnah and Beraisa, with no Gemara. On this he bases a suspicion that Rashi is quoting a custom older than the Gemara itself, which is why the list contains none.

Why did it stop? The reason is economic. Rashi writes רבתה העניות, and the lecturer finds it sad to read: people were poor and had to earn a living, and one cannot keep them in synagogue for an extra half hour of study as part of the prayer, with an instruction not to remove the tefillin (תפילין) until a chapter of the Prophets has been read. A daf one may invite people to; a daily compulsory session one cannot enforce. The custom lapsed on weekdays and survives on Shabbos alone, when no one is hurrying to work.

On this account the haftarah is, in the lecturer’s words, “a law of every day” (דין) that has nothing to do with the weekly portion and that we keep only once a week. It is a notable novelty (chiddush (חידוש)) that most people do not know. The haftarah carries blessings before and after, as part of Rashi’s view; and in those days the reading need not have matched the occasion (מעין המאורע): it was simply a chapter of Tanach, perhaps starting from Yehoshua (יהושע) and reading a chapter a day. “I don’t know if they read two chapters a day; we have no clear indication as to what they read.”

IX. The Haftarah Joins the Day: The Aufruf, the Rambam, and Rav Sherira Gaon

What follows is not Rashi’s account but the later history of the haftarah. In the account the lecturer attributes to Rashi, a reading that began as a daily one became tied to the occasion. Even now, on Yom Tov, on Rosh Chodesh, or on a Shabbos that falls on the eve of Rosh Chodesh, the haftarah of the day is read in place of the haftarah of the portion.

The lecturer reports that the Rema (Rema (רמ״א)) brings a custom for a bridegroom: when there was a groom in the synagogue on the Shabbos of his aufruf (אויפרוף), a different haftarah was read, שוש אשיש, the passage in Yeshayahu on bridegroom and bride, one of the seven haftaros of consolation (shevah denechemta (שבע דנחמתא)). He adds that the Sefardim adopted the custom, and that we do not keep it.[21] (Asked what a large Sefardic congregation does with a groom every week, the lecturer was told that they do keep it.)

“A greater novelty still,” the lecturer says, “I found out only recently”: that, according to the Rishonim, when something significant occurred in a week, the haftarah of the week was set aside and the haftarah relating to the event was read. When the Rambam (Rambam (רמב״ם)) died, the haftarah that Shabbos, in the lecturer’s telling throughout Eretz Yisrael, was a chapter of Shmuel (שמואל), the chapter in which the Philistines take the ark of the covenant from Israel. The ark in exile: the Rambam was like the ark, the power of Torah of his generation, and his soul went up to Heaven. And when Rav Sherira Gaon (רב שרירא גאון) died, in the Geonic age, a chapter was read about the death of a righteous man. “We do not do this today”; the lecturer notes that we do not do even what is done for a groom.

The lecturer could not recall the date of the Rambam’s yahrzeit, saying it is “somewhere in Shevat (שבט).” It is the twentieth of Tevet (טבת), and the Rambam died in Egypt, so that the lecturer’s “throughout Eretz Yisrael” is to be read, if the tradition is sound, as the communities that mourned him. Since the Rambam died more than a century after Rashi, the tradition cannot be Rashi’s, and the lecturer does not name its source.[22]

X. The Second Origin: The Avudraham and the Greek Decree

The second account is that of the Avudraham. At one time, he writes, a decree (gezeirah (גזירה)) was issued against reading the Torah. The Sages (or whoever was there at the time) feared that the reading would be forgotten: people would come to synagogue, pray Shacharis with no break for a reading, pray Musaf, and go home. They therefore instituted a rule (takanah (תקנה)): since the Torah cannot be read, at least twenty-one verses of the Prophets should be read, and a man should rise and make a blessing. The number is twenty-one because Shabbos has seven aliyos and each aliyah must have at least three verses, three times seven. The haftarah is to be on the theme of what would have been read had it not been for the decree, that Shabbos’s portion, so that it is as though one had read the Torah, and people will not forget the reading. When the decree was lifted, the Torah was read again, and the haftarah remained.[23]

The measure of the substitute is thus the thing it replaced. The reading that was forbidden was divided among seven men, three verses at least for each; the replacement is divided the same way. The lecturer adds the confirmation from the opposite case: on Yom Tov, when the Torah reading is shorter and fewer men are called, fewer verses are required. Customs differ as to exactly where the haftarah ends, but there must be twenty-one verses on Shabbos.

According to the Avudraham there never was a daily reading of the Prophets such as Rashi describes; and on this the lecturer remarks that it is a major dispute in Jewish history. The obligation to study the Prophets daily is met, on his view, by the prophetic verses already said in the prayers, such as ובא לציון גואל. The question that remains is the one the lecturer puts: when was there a decree that forbade the Torah but permitted the Prophets?

XI. When Was the Decree? The Tishbi, Antiochus, and the Levush

The answer is found in the Sefer HaTishbi (ספר התשבי) of Eliyahu Bachur, which the lecturer loves and calls a very rare book, one that came to light, he says, in the sixteenth century.[24] Eliyahu Bachur writes that it was the Greek kingdom (מלכות יון), in the days of Antiochus, who ruled Eretz Yisrael, that made the decree against reading the Torah every Shabbos. (The lecturer began by naming the age of Nebuchadnezzar and then settled on Antiochus; the name is as the lecturer gives it in relaying Eliyahu Bachur.)

The Levush adds a detail that the lecturer finds startling: the decree forbade not only the Torah but the Writings (Kesuvim (כתובים)), and left the Prophets alone. “Perhaps the Greeks thought the Prophets were all right to read, but the Writings not.” That, says the Levush, is why we never take a haftarah from Kesuvim. The lecturer calls it a ruling of the Gemara in Megillah (מגילה) whose reason nobody knows; the Levush supplies it from history. Since that time, writes Eliyahu Bachur, the Prophets have been read every Shabbos, and the rule remained.[25]

Eliyahu Bachur and the Christian Hebraists Rav Eliyahu Bachur, known to the world as Elijah Levita, was born in Germany and lived in Italy in the sixteenth century, a brilliant man and one of the most prolific writers on Hebrew grammar (dikduk (דקדוק)) of his day; he is better known among Christians than among Jews. He taught Israel, and he taught the Christian Hebraists, priests and cardinals who had become curious about the Hebrew Bible, Mishnah, Tosefta and Gemara. The lecturer says he boarded with one of the most famous cardinals of Italy, whose name he cannot recall, and that Sebastian Münster learned Torah from him. The lecturer suspects that Eliyahu Bachur drew criticism in his own day for teaching non-Jews, and that “maybe bad things came of it”; those he taught did not convert but remained Christians, and later authorities were not sure that everything he wrote could be trusted. But the Tishbi entered our literature and the halachic authorities.[26]

XII. Further Questions Bearing on the Two Accounts

Three questions were put to the lecturer in the course of the Avudraham passage. They are gathered here, and the discourse notes the placement.

The Triennial Cycle

What of the ancient practice of reading the Torah over three years, in which the portions are far shorter? The Acharonim (אחרונים) observe that on this practice the haftarah was chosen to match whatever small portion was read that week, so that there were many more haftaros than there are now; and the division of the Chumash was different. The lecturer and the questioner speak of the “custom of the people of Bavel,” but the triennial cycle was the custom of Eretz Yisrael, Babylonia having read the Torah annually, and the lecturer’s own next example is of the Eretz Yisrael rite. That example is Benjamin of Tudela (Binyamin MiTudela (בנימין מטודלה)), who writes that in Cairo, next door to the synagogue of the Rambam, was another congregation that kept the ancient custom of completing the Torah only once in three years, with whatever haftarah connected to the portion of that week.[27]

Why Seven Aliyos?

The Gemara in Megillah (23a) gives several reasons for the seven, among them the seven ministers at the opening of the Book of Esther. The lecturer adds a novelty from one of the Acharonim, which he thinks the Levush brings and which the Rishonim do not give: in earlier times some Jews came to synagogue only on Shabbos, even in Europe, where those who lived in small villages without a quorum went to town on Shabbos and sometimes on Monday and Thursday. They had missed during the week the recitations of ברכו את ה׳ המבורך, and so, to make up for those recitations, there are seven aliyos, each man called up making a blessing.[28]

Machar Chodesh

A haftarah such as מחר חודש has nothing to do with the day: it speaks of tomorrow. The lecturer calls this an excellent question and says the Magen Avraham raises it. According to many customs, מחר חודש is read only on a Shabbos that is the eve of Rosh Chodesh; in the custom the lecturer follows, the haftarah of Rosh Chodesh is read when Shabbos is itself Rosh Chodesh. “I don’t have a good answer; the Magen Avraham doesn’t like it.”[29] The question presses most on the Avudraham’s account, which ties the haftarah to the portion; on Rashi’s account, in which the reading was once independent of the day, it is less pointed.

XIII. The Two Accounts Compared

Rashi (Sefer HaPardes)The Avudraham (with Levita and the Levush)
OriginA daily custom, so that a man read Scripture before workA response to a Greek decree against reading the Torah
How oftenEvery day; now Shabbos onlyShabbos (and the days of Torah reading), as a substitute for the reading
Relation to the portionNone at first; later joined to the dayThe haftarah follows the portion that was forbidden
Why from the ProphetsProphets were one of the five things to be read dailyThe decree, per the Levush, struck the Torah and the Writings, not the Prophets
Why twenty-one versesNot addressedThree verses for each of the seven aliyos
Why it lapsed on weekdaysPoverty (רבתה העניות)It never was daily

The two accounts agree on the point that matters in practice: the haftarah is read on Shabbos with blessings, and is drawn from the Prophets. They disagree as to what it was for. On Rashi’s account it is the remnant of a custom of daily study; on the Avudraham’s it is a memorial to a reading that a hostile king suppressed. The lecturer does not choose between them, and remarks only that one is a dispute “extreme from one to the other.”

The lecturer closes by saying that the following discourse will take up the blessings of the haftarah and the question whether one should read the haftarah together with the reader (baal korei (בעל קורא)), and ends the study with the words רצה הקדוש ברוך הוא לזכות את ישראל, לפיכך הרבה להם תורה ומצות, שנאמר: ה׳ חפץ למען צדקו, יגדיל תורה ויאדיר.

Not located: the chapter of Soferim was not consulted, and the lecturer’s summary is given as his.   6. Ashkenazic congregations that moved the psalm of the day to follow Shacharis. The lecturer reports that a Brooklyn congregation of strongly Ashkenazic character moved the psalm to follow Shacharis, and that a Long Island congregation did the same some thirty years ago, moving both the psalm of the day and אנעים זמירות for reasons of decorum. He says he learned the second account only shortly before, from a member of the room. Oral report: communal history, not checkable; the congregations and the rabbi concerned are not named here. ↩

The lecturer cites the Or Zarua as the source of the custom of selling, on Simchas Torah, the honor of taking out the Torah for the year, as a great merit and an expression of כבוד התורה. Not located: the specific passage. The identification of the author as Rav Yitzchak ben Moshe of Vienna and of his century is the lecturer’s. ↩

The lecturer says the Chida records this as an ancient custom, with the symbolism of פתיחת הרחם and the opening of the ark, and the עת רצון at the ark; he notes that the matter was treated earlier in the series. Not located: no title of a work was given and none is supplied. The reasons offered for a bridegroom (auspiciousness, הכשר מצוה) are suggestions from the room, not sources. ↩

The lecturer says Soferim records ancient customs of Eretz Yisrael, among them a long responsive order of verses between the chazzan and the congregation, and that ויהי בנסוע הארן and בריך שמיה are not in it. Partly verified: Soferim 14:8, in the text consulted, has a longer series of verses (beginning with אשרי יושבי ביתך and אין כמוך באלהים ה׳) and has no אתה הראת.   10. אור זרוע: the spelling of עקיבא/עקיבה in a get; תהלים צ״ז:י״א. The lecturer tells that the Or Zarua, writing a get, had to choose between the Bavli (בבלי)’s עקיבא and the Yerushalmi (ירושלמי)’s and Tosefta (תוספתא)’s עקיבה, and relates the dream in which he was shown אור זרוע לצדיק ולישרי לב שמחה. Verified as to the verse (Tehillim (תהלים) 97:11): the last letters of its words are ר, ע, ק, י, ב, ה. The ר is the last letter of אור; the lecturer reads the string as רבי עקיבא with a final hei, taking the ר as the abbreviation of רבי, which is the editor’s gloss on his words. Not located: that the Or Zarua tells the dream in his introduction, and the pattern of spelling in the Bavli and Mishnah against the Yerushalmi and Tosefta, which is a sweeping claim about the manuscripts; both are the lecturer’s reports. ↩

In the discussion he agreed with a remark from the room about names of men and women in gittin (the room gave the reference as קכ״ט) and added that in Europe yods, alefs, ayins and vavs were added to names; he calls the subject beyond the scope of the discourse and says that “he wrote a whole sefer about this” (the author is not named). Not checkable. There is no daf 129 in Gittin; the reference is probably to Even HaEzer (אבן העזר) 129, the siman on the names of men and women in gittin (the editor’s inference, to be confirmed). ↩

  3. Where does the Or Zarua tell the dream of אור זרוע לצדיק, and which passage says that in the Rhineland nothing was said when the Torah was taken out? Is the pattern of עקיבא and עקיבה in the sources as the lecturer reports (notes 7, 10 and 12)?
  4. Who restored the Soferim verses, the Or Zarua in the Rhineland or Rav Avraham the doctor in Rome, or both? What was the fourth of the four exiled families (note 13)? Is the gittin reference Even HaEzer 129 (note 11)?
  5. Where in the Midrash are the passages on the mi sheberach in Vayikra Rabbah and Kohelet Rabbah, and what is the source for the claim that בעבור שיתן מתנה is the original wording (note 16)? Where is the Beis Yosef’s responsum in Avkas Rochel (אבקת רוכל) (note 18)?
  6. Where in the Sefer HaPardes does Rashi’s account stand, is the work Rashi’s own or his school’s, and does the list of what is to be read include Gemara (note 19)?
  7. Where is the Rema’s custom of שוש אשיש for a bridegroom (note 21), and what is the source for the haftaros read at the death of the Rambam and of Rav Sherira Gaon (note 22)?
  8. What are the Avudraham’s and the Levush’s own words on the decree, on the twenty-one verses on Shabbos and the fewer verses on Yom Tov, and on the Writings? Is siman 284 the siman intended (notes 23 and 25)? Which Gemara in Megillah is meant?
  9. Which volume and responsum of the Igros Moshe is meant, and does the Tishbi say what the lecturer reports of Antiochus (notes 26 and 24)?
  10. Did the lecturer mean the people of Bavel or of Eretz Yisrael for the triennial cycle, and how does the arithmetic of the missed Barchu recitations work (notes 27 and 28)? Which sentence did he mean about Machar Chodesh (note 29)?
  11. Does the lecturer incline to Rashi’s account or to the Avudraham’s? The discourse presents both and records no preference.
Sefer Edition of Rabbi Ephrati’s discourse on Tefillah, 21 Cheshvan 5787. The edition begins from the service that precedes the reading, sets the two ancient accounts of the haftarah side by side, presents the lecturer’s reports as reports, and records his uncertainties as he stated them.
