יְדִיד נֶפֶשׁ — מְקוֹרוֹ וְהִתְפַּתְּחוּתוֹ שֶׁל הַפִּיּוּט

Yedid Nefesh: Origin and Journey of the Piyut
A Detour from Hilchos Krias Shema — Hei Teves, Yahrtzeit of Rav Shlomo Molcho
Recorded 5 January 2025 (Hei Teves 5785)  ·  Sefer Edition

L’ilui nishmas Rav Shlomo Molcho ha’kadosh, who was killed al kiddush Hashem in Mantua on Yud-Gimmel Teves of the year 5292 (1532); and in memory of that chevra shel chaverim in Tzfas whose secret tefillah we recite each morning without knowing what we ask.

Among the piyutim of the siddur, few are so widely sung, and few are so poorly understood, as Yedid Nefesh (יְדִיד נֶפֶשׁ). This chapter traces the journey of the piyut from its mechaber, Rabbeinu Elazar Azkari (רבינו אלעזר אזכרי) of Tzfas, through the extraordinary parshah that occasioned its composition — the life and death of Shlomo Molcho (שלמה מולכו) al kiddush Hashem, and the Maggid Meisharim (מגיד משרים) of the Beis Yosef (בית יוסף) — and finally into the pages of the modern siddur. What emerges is a piyut that most Jews sing without knowing that they are pleading, in coded language, for the survival of the man to whom Klal Yisrael owes the Shulchan Aruch.

I. The Piyut and Its Three Stations in the Siddur

In the siddurim in common use today, Yedid Nefesh appears in three distinct liturgical stations. In the morning it is placed between the daily korbanos (קרבנות) and Pesukei d’Zimra (פסוקי דזמרא). On Erev Shabbos (ערב שבת) it stands before Kabbalas Shabbos (קבלת שבת) — a placement embraced especially in the Dati Le’umi (דתי לאומי), Yeshurun, Chabad, and German-Ashkenaz communities. And on Shabbos afternoon it is sung at Shalosh Seudos (שלוש סעודות) as the melody of the day’s waning light.

The piyut has become so embedded in the daily seder that its late arrival to that seder is easily forgotten. In its origin, Yedid Nefesh was not tefillah at all — not in any of the three stations in which it now sits. Its mechaber composed it as a shir ahavah (שיר אהבה), a love-song to be sung with a chevra shel chaverim. Its migration into the siddur would take a century and a half.

II. The Mechaber — Rabbeinu Elazar Azkari

The Simple Gabbai of the Arizal

The author of Yedid Nefesh, Rabbeinu Elazar Azkari, is best known to the olam as the author of Sefer Charedim (ספר חרדים). He lived in Tzfas (צפת) in the sixteenth century, in the years of the Arizal (האר"י ז"ל), and served as the shamash (שמש) — the gabbai — of the Arizal’s shul. Outwardly he presented as a modest, unremarkable ba’al ha’bayis. In the eyes of many he was little more than a simpleton. The full stature of the man remained hidden.

The Revelation at Meron

The moment in which the olam’s perception of Rabbeinu Elazar was overturned is preserved in the collections of Ashkenazic hanhagos printed in the name of the Hadras Zekenim tradition of Meron.[1] It was the Arizal who popularized the minhag of ascending to Meron on Lag B’Omer to the kever of Rabi Shimon bar Yochai (רבי שמעון בר יוחאי); the annual hilula in its present form owes its shape to him. On one such Lag B’Omer, in the middle of the dancing in the chatzer beside the me’arah of the Rashbi, the Arizal suddenly drew the humble gabbai into the innermost circle and danced with him with intense dveikus (דבקות) for a great length of time.

The talmidim stood astonished. What place had this simple gabbai at the very center of the hilula? Only afterward did the Arizal reveal what he had seen: Rabbeinu Elazar Azkari is a gadol me’od ba’Kabbalah. I saw Rabi Shimon dancing with him; I joined them. From that moment the perception of the man among his contemporaries was radically overturned.

Yiras Hashem, Ahavas Hashem, and the Duty of Love-Song

In the opening chapters of Sefer Charedim,[2] Rabbeinu Elazar establishes the twin foundations of the oved Hashem: yiras Hashem (יראת ה’) and ahavas Hashem (אהבת ה’). The two are inseparable, and the generation must be strengthened in both. As the pasuk teaches, reishis chochmah yiras Hashem — only from yirah can one arrive at ahavah, as the Chovos HaLevavos (חובות הלבבות)[3] and the classical mussar sefarim establish.

Building on the framework of the Chovos HaLevavos, Rabbeinu Elazar advances a striking teaching: part of love is the constant expression of love. To love is to speak that love. This is why we recite Tehillim (תהילים): to give voice to the love of HaKadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא). More than this — Rabbeinu Elazar teaches that one must compose shirei ahavah, love-songs, to the Beloved. HaKadosh Baruch Hu is the Beloved; the neshamah of the Jew is the lover. And it is in this hakdamah that Yedid Nefesh is born.

The Piyut and Its Roshei Teivos

The roshei teivos (ראשי תיבות) of the four stanzas spell the Sheim Havayah (שם הוי"ה):

  • ידיד נפש
  • הדור נאה
  • ותיקך יהמו רחמיך
  • הגלה נא

Yet the piyut was composed neither for the siddur nor for the tzibbur. Rabbeinu Elazar records that he sang this shir together with a chevra shel chaverim — the intimate circle of mekubalim among whom he moved in Tzfas. He did not designate a time for its recitation; he did not petition that it enter the liturgy. From the day it was composed until it first appeared in a printed siddur, roughly one hundred and thirty years would pass.

III. The World in Which the Piyut Was Composed

The Expulsion from Spain

To grasp how Yedid Nefesh reached the siddur, one must understand the atmosphere of Sephardic Jewry in the sixteenth century. In the year 5252 (1492), the Jews of Spain were driven from their homes — one of the most catastrophic tekufos in the history of galus. The wealthiest and most learned community in the diaspora was destroyed in a matter of years. The eyewitness account of Don Yitzchak Abarbanel (דון יצחק אברבנאל), royal treasurer and one of the leaders of Spanish Jewry, is preserved in his hakdamah to Sefer Melachim:[4] hundreds of thousands expelled, with nowhere to receive them; bankers, talmidei chachamim, royal advisors driven into the sea and into the mountains.

The Anusim

Those who could neither leave nor accept martyrdom became Anusim (אנוסים) — the Conversos. They lived as Catholics in public and as Jews in secret, baking matzah (מצה) in cellars, making sederim (סדרים) by whispered haggadah, transmitting whatever fragments of Yiddishkeit they could preserve to children raised outwardly in the Church.

The Yearning for Geulah

Jews were scattered through the Middle East, North Africa, and Greece. In such a tzar-saturated moment, any vision of geulah (גאולה) — any promise that the redemption stood at the door — became magnetic. It is the fundamental principle of historical reading in mesorah that the generations must be understood in their own atmosphere and not reproached with the categories of later centuries. The tekufah that followed the Expulsion was, in the phrase of the mekubalim, re’uyah la’geulah (ראויה לגאולה) — ripe for redemption — even if Klal Yisrael was not zocheh to see it come.

IV. Diego Pires in the Portuguese Court

In the year 5260 (1500), eight years after the Expulsion, a child was born in Lisboa (ליסבון), Portugal, to a family of Anusim. His name at the baptismal font was Diego Pires. He was raised outwardly Catholic; his parents told him only that he was, in some hidden sense, a Jew. He received no bris milah (ברית מילה); he knew no Shabbos, no Yiddishkeit, no Torah (תורה).

Gifted with extraordinary intellect and personal presence, Diego rose swiftly in the Portuguese royal service. Still in his early twenties, he was appointed Secretary of the Highest Appeals Court of Portugal under King João III (reigned 1521–1557), the son of the King Manuel I who had forcibly converted Portuguese Jewry a generation earlier. Diego moved in the innermost circles of the court, secretly bearing a Jewish identity that he neither professed nor practiced.

V. The Arrival of David HaReuveni

The Emissary from the Ten Tribes

In the year 5283 (1523), there appeared at the Portuguese court a figure whose origins have remained an unsolved question of Jewish history: David HaReuveni (דוד הראובני). Dark of complexion and Sephardic in appearance, he spoke chiefly Arabic with fragments of Hebrew. He claimed to have come from a distant desert; his brother, he said, was king over the Aseres HaShevatim (עשרת השבטים), the Ten Lost Tribes, and commanded three hundred thousand Jewish soldiers prepared to make war.

The claim rested upon a real machlokes in Chazal. The Gemara in Sanhedrin[5] preserves a dispute over whether the Ten Tribes will return; various rishonim and acharonim placed them in India, in China, and in yet more distant regions. Whatever the truth of HaReuveni’s personal biography, his story engaged a sugya that stood open in the mesorah.

The Proposed Alliance

HaReuveni brought a proposal fit for a European court. The armies of his brother, joined with the Christian forces of Portugal, Spain, and France — the Holy Roman Empire — would march against the Ottoman Empire, which then held Eretz Yisrael (ארץ ישראל). Christians and Jews would jointly conquer the Land and share its sovereignty. His personal magnetism was such that the King of Portugal at one stage committed eight warships, ammunition, and soldiers; the original correspondence survives in the Portuguese archives. The king afterward withdrew from the enterprise but furnished HaReuveni with letters of introduction to the other monarchs of Christendom.

VI. Bris Milah in Secret

At court HaReuveni encountered the young Secretariat Diego Pires. The impact was profound. Diego approached him with a single request: Perform a bris milah for me. HaReuveni refused. To be discovered circumcising an Anus in the royal precincts would have alerted the king that the entire enterprise of Ferdinand and Isabella was being quietly reversed on his own soil; it would have destroyed the political mission at a stroke.

Diego acted alone. He performed his own bris milah. He records the event in his own hand in his sefer HaMefoar (ספר המפואר),[6] preserved to this day and now available in digital form.[7] He describes the nissim (נסים) attending the healing: a physician summoned in haste, the wound closing within days. He took the name Shlomo Molcho — “Molcho” from malchus (מלכות), a name that from its first utterance carried a suggestion of messianic aspiration — and committed himself entirely to a life of Torah.

The halachic dimension of the act is not incidental. The status of one who acts as mohel upon himself is treated in the sugya of Avodah Zarah and its nosei keilim;[8] the Rambam (רמב"ם) and the Shulchan Aruch in Yoreh De’ah hold that a person may indeed be mohel for himself. For the class of Anusim as a whole, this halachah was no theoretical matter; whole families in Sefarad received bris milah in secret precisely because self-circumcision, or circumcision at the hands of a fellow Anus, was the only path available.

VII. From Anus to Talmid Chacham

Under Rav Ovadyah Sforno

The newly-named Shlomo Molcho left Portugal and travelled to Italy, where he applied himself to Torah and mitzvos. There he found in Rav Ovadyah Sforno (רב עובדיה ספורנו) — the great parshan on Chumash (חומש) — a rebbi and a source of encouragement. But personal teshuvah was not, for him, the end of the road. Molcho had been so impacted by HaReuveni’s messianic vision that he cast his lot with the mission itself. In his Sefer Chayas Kinah (ספר חית קנה)[9] he writes with astonishing self-abnegation: I am as a servant to his master. My master David HaReuveni leads me; I do his bidding. HaReuveni, who had at first held the new ger-in-spirit at arm’s length, eventually accepted him as a full partner.

In the Circle of the Maharitatz

In Greece, Molcho reached the study-hall of one of the greatest talmidei chachamim of the era: Rabbeinu Yosef Taitazak (רבינו יוסף טאיטאצאק), known by his acronym as the Maharitatz (מהרי"ט"ץ), frequently cited by the Beis Yosef.[10] Within his beis midrash in Salonika had learned or would learn some of the very builders of the Shulchan Aruch-generation:

  • Rabbeinu Shlomo Alkabetz (רבינו שלמה אלקבץ), the mechaber of Lecha Dodi (לכה דודי);
  • The Beis Yosef himself (Rabbeinu Yosef Karo, רבינו יוסף קארו), whose broader circle of teachers included the Maharitatz alongside his primary rabbanim in Adrianople and Tzfas.

Molcho, the former Marrano at the Portuguese court, had thus made his way into the most elite circle of talmidei chachamim, mekubalim, and poskim of his age — the very circle whose halachah and hanhagah would flow forward as the Shulchan Aruch-mesorah we follow to this day.

The Beis Yosef Drawn into Torah HaSigulah

The Beis Yosef, at least twelve years Molcho’s senior, was so impressed by the younger man that his own path shifted. His absorption into torah ha’sigulah (תורת הסגולה) and kabbalah — his intense preoccupation with Mashiach (משיח) and geulah — can be traced in significant part to Molcho’s influence. From that current would in turn flow influences reaching the Arizal and Rav Chaim Vital (רב חיים ויטאל). Molcho himself was deeply engaged in calculations of the keitz (קץ) — despite the Gemara’s stinging ma’amar in Sanhedrin, tipach atzman shel mechashvei kitzin[11] — and he received visions and dreams. The Acharonim extend various heteirim for such calculations in a tekufah of severe tzar; whether or not Molcho would today have received formal sanction, his contemporaries evidently understood his activity within that framework.

The Tzitzis of Shlomo Molcho

The single most extraordinary halachic remainder of Molcho’s life is his tzitzis (ציצית). The Beis Yosef in Hilchos Tzitzis[12] records the specific manner in which Molcho tied his tzitzis — the kshorim (קשרים) — and brings it down l’halachah l’ma’aseh.

The Magen Avraham (מגן אברהם), writing some two hundred and fifty years after the events, testifies that Molcho’s actual tzitzis still existed in his time.[13] The Tosfos Yom Tov (תוספות יום טוב) — Rabbeinu Yom Tov Lipman Heller, who served as av beis din of Prague and whose kever lies in Krakow beside that of the Rema (רמ"א) — personally examined the tzitzis in Prague and confirmed the Beis Yosef’s description in every detail.[14] The Mishnah Berurah (משנה ברורה) preserves this chain and rules accordingly.

The consequence, felt in every Jewish morning, is stark. The tzitzis tied upon the tallis of every observant Jew today is tied, in part, on the pattern once tied by an Anus who circumcised himself in secret in the Portuguese court and died al kiddush Hashem in Mantua. His personal hanhagah has become the halachah of Klal Yisrael.

VIII. A Century Before Shabbsai Tzvi

HaReuveni and Molcho travelled together from city to city and from court to court, urging their messianic proposal upon Christian monarchs and Jewish communities alike. Chronologically, their campaign preceded that of Shabbsai Tzvi (שבתי צבי) — the most infamous false mashiach of Jewish history — by almost exactly a century. Not every kehillah welcomed them. Salonika, the great post-Expulsion Sephardic center of Greece, feared sin’as Yisrael (שנאת ישראל) from the political ripples the two men produced; the trauma of the Expulsion was still fresh. Yet the support they garnered was considerable.

IX. The Prophet at the Gates of Rome

The Two Prophecies

In Italy Molcho uttered two prophecies that were publicly fulfilled. The first foretold the great flooding of the Tiber River; one month later thousands perished when the river broke over Rome. The second foretold the earthquake that struck Portugal in 5292 (1532), a catastrophe recorded on the secular register that killed thousands.

Before Pope Clement VII

The Cardinals of Rome admitted him to an audience with Pope Clement VII. The Pope was overwhelmed. He entertained, at least in outline, the proposed alliance with the Jews and with HaReuveni against the Ottoman Empire; he provided letters of introduction. A tradition preserved among historians reports that the Pope himself intervened to protect Molcho from the Inquisition at one juncture. In the end the political currents proved too intricate; the alliance was never consummated.

The Metzora at the Gates

Before entering the Pope’s presence, Molcho sat for thirty days at the very gates of Rome, dressed in the torn clothing of a pauper, fasting. The ma’aseh he was enacting was not obscure. The Gemara in Sanhedrin[15] records that Rabi Yehoshua ben Levi asked Eliyahu HaNavi where Mashiach was to be found. Eliyahu answered: at the gates of Rome. Mashiach sits there as a metzora (מצורע), a leper, changing his bandages; but unlike other lepers, who unwrap all their bandages together and rebind them together, Mashiach unwraps one at a time and rebinds each before proceeding to the next. Why? So that at any moment, when HaKadosh Baruch Hu shall command him, he may spring instantly into action; not one bandage may delay him. When Rabi Yehoshua ben Levi asked Mashiach himself when he would come, the answer was: today. Sunset came; he did not. When Rabi Yehoshua returned to Eliyahu in complaint, Eliyahu revealed the tnai: hayom im b’kolo tishma’u — today, if you will hearken to His voice. The redemption stands ready; it awaits our teshuvah.

Molcho was walking, deliberately and knowingly, in the pattern of that sugya. By the time he entered the Pope’s presence, all Rome was speaking of the strange Jew at the gates whose prophecies had come to pass.

X. Mantua — Yud-Gimmel Teves 5292

Eventually the two companions were seized. HaReuveni disappeared into the dungeons of the Inquisition and was put to death, according to the commonly accepted tradition, around the year 1538 (the Portuguese archival record varies between 1535 and 1541). A single entry in the Portuguese national archive states that he accepted Christianity before his death — a claim to be received with appropriate reserve, given that the Church was accustomed to record what it wished to record.

In the year 5292 (1532), in Mantua (מנטובה), Italy, on the day of Yud-Gimmel Teves, Shlomo Molcho was brought before an Inquisitorial tribunal, condemned, and led to be burned alive. The historical record preserves the details of his final hours. The authorities stuffed his mouth with cotton, so persuasive a speaker was he that they feared even Christians might be swayed if he were permitted to speak before the flames. Before the pyre was lit they removed the cotton and offered him a final choice: return to the faith into which he had been baptized, and he would be saved.

His Final Words

I regret ever having been born and having practiced Christianity. My neshamah is going to return to her Father in Shamayim.

And then, from a mouth that had lately been stopped with cotton lest the flames themselves be delayed by his eloquence, rose the ancient pasuk that has escorted the kedoshim of Klal Yisrael across every generation:

שְׁמַע יִשְׂרָאֵל ה' אֱלֹקֵינוּ ה' אֶחָד!
Hear, O Israel: Hashem is our God; Hashem is One.

On Yud-Gimmel Teves 5292 he was killed al kiddush Hashem.

XI. The Reuveni-Molcho Paradox

Even at this point in the narrative a question demands to be voiced. Why is David HaReuveni consigned to what one might call the dustbins of history — recalled, when at all, as a strange episode, a Sephardic mashiach sheker at the edge of respectability — while his partner Shlomo Molcho, equally engaged in the same messianic enterprise, is drawn b’kahal kedoshim (בקהל קדושים) and remembered with reverence, his tzitzis-kshorim ruled to halachah in the Beis Yosef?

The answer rests upon a distinction fundamental to the mesorah. HaReuveni bore his messianic vision; Molcho bore both the vision and the Torah. He entered the beis midrash of the Maharitatz; he became a genuine talmid chacham; he was the mechaber of two sefarim; his hanhagah in tzitzis was received by the Beis Yosef himself; and he sealed his life al kiddush Hashem. HaReuveni, lacking the limud and lacking the seal, is forgotten. Molcho, sealed and studied, stands within the mesorah. Limud haTorah and kiddush Hashem together, in the reading of Klal Yisrael, are what confer place among the kedoshim.

XII. The Beis Yosef’s Request

“I Wish to Die Like Shlomo Molcho”

When word of Molcho’s kiddush Hashem reached Tzfas, the entire Jewish world was shaken. The Beis Yosef, who had held Molcho in the highest personal esteem, uttered a wish that his talmidim preserved: May I be zocheh to die al kiddush Hashem like Shlomo Molcho. It was not a passing exclamation. It became a fixed request in his tefillah.

The Maggid’s Threefold Promise

The Beis Yosef was accompanied through his life by a Maggid (מגיד) — an entity from Shamayim that revealed sodos and hanhagos to him. His encounters with this Maggid are recorded in his sefer Maggid Meisharim,[16] a genuine sefer that has passed through many printings and stands upon the shelves of every serious Jewish library.

The Maggid spoke to the Beis Yosef of his request. I hear you are davening to die al kiddush Hashem like Shlomo Molcho. Three separate times — in Parshas Noach and in Parshas Lech Lecha, at the concluding entries of those parshiyos — the Maggid promised that the Beis Yosef would in truth be zocheh to die al kiddush Hashem, to be burned by the enemies of Yisrael, k’Shlomo b’chiri — like My chosen Shlomo.

The Difficulty

The Beis Yosef lived to the age of eighty-seven. He was niftar on Yud-Gimmel Nisan of 5335 (1575), two days before Pesach, in his bed, in peace. The Maggid’s threefold promise from Shamayim — the promise of a kiddush Hashem like Molcho’s — had, by the plain reading of his life, gone unfulfilled. This is the difficulty that stands at the very heart of the parshah of Yedid Nefesh.

XIII. The Secret Tefillah of Rabbeinu Elazar Azkari

The Realization

Rabbeinu Elazar Azkari was born after Molcho’s death and knew this entire parshah at close range. He was a talmid in the wider circle of the Beis Yosef’s Tzfas and drank from the wellsprings of the Arizal; he knew the Maggid’s threefold promise, and he knew the Beis Yosef’s daily prayer.

Rabbeinu Elazar and his chevra in Tzfas reached a difficult and radiant realization. Klal Yisrael could not afford for the Beis Yosef’s prayer to be answered as he himself desired it. Were the Beis Yosef to be taken young, his magnum opus — the Shulchan Aruch — would be left incomplete or unwritten; and generations of Jews yet unborn would be deprived of the sefer that would order their entire religious life. The Beis Yosef had already completed his Beis Yosef upon the Tur by the year 5302 (1542), and it had begun to circulate in the following decades; the Shulchan Aruch itself was completed and printed only in the years leading to 5325 (1565). The years in which the Shulchan Aruch was being drawn together, printed, and disseminated across the diaspora were precisely the years in which the Beis Yosef’s tefillah for a martyr’s death might have been answered.

Therefore Rabbeinu Elazar and his chevra dedicated themselves to daven daily that the Beis Yosef’s wish not be granted — that he live to complete his labor. The piyut they composed for this tefillah was Yedid Nefesh.

The Manuscript Evidence: “Bincha Yechidcha”

The witness to this reading of the piyut lies in the original manuscript, the ksav yad of Rabbeinu Elazar Azkari, preserved to this day in the collection of the Jewish Theological Seminary in New York.[17] The manuscript diverges in several respects from the printed text familiar from modern siddurim. The most striking divergence stands in the third stanza. Where the printed text now most commonly reads:

וְחוּסָה נָּא עַל בִּנְךָ יְחִידְךָ.
Have mercy, we pray, upon Your only son.

— the manuscript preserves an alternation attributed within the mesorah of the Tzfas mekubalim to Rabbeinu Elazar’s own hand. In the reading transmitted within that mesorah, bincha yechidcha was a coded reference to the Beis Yosef himself — the ben yachid whom Klal Yisrael could not spare, the only one in whose hand the Shulchan Aruch could yet be completed.[18]

When, b’siyata di’Shmaya, the tefillah was accepted and the Beis Yosef survived to complete his chibbur, Rabbeinu Elazar himself amended the text. In editions prepared under his hand thereafter, the third stanza was generalized:

וְחוּסָה נָּא עַל עַמְּךָ יְחִידְךָ.
Have mercy, we pray, upon Your only nation.

Since the danger was past, the remez was no longer required. Later printers, coming upon the earlier manuscript, restored the reading bincha yechidcha, and it is this reading that now stands in the majority of printed siddurim. Most of those who recite it are wholly unaware that they are voicing a coded plea for the survival of the man whose halachah orders their daily life.

Two Layers, Not Two Readings

It should be observed that the framing of Yedid Nefesh as a shir ahavah in Sefer Charedim and its function as a coded tefillah for the Beis Yosef are not in tension. The piyut operates on two layers at once, as the mekubalim composed their tefillos: the outer peshat, the shir ahavah of the neshamah to her Creator; and the inner sod, the specific petition sung by the chevra shel chaverim for the survival of a single tzaddik whose life the generation could not spare. The chevra sang the shir of love, and within its cadences carried the plea for the Beis Yosef; the outer meaning was emes, and the inner meaning was emes, and both were sung in one breath.

XIV. Kiddush Hashem: Actualized and Accepted

The Question the Doros Have Asked

The gedolei ha’doros across the generations grappled with the difficulty presented by the Beis Yosef’s peaceful death against the Maggid’s threefold promise. The Lubavitcher Rebbe (האדמו"ר מליובאוויטש) addressed the matter in a sichah on Chof Teves 5736,[19] framing the question in its full weight before proposing his own haaros.

The Teretz of Rav Yaakov Chaim Sofer

The posek Rav Yaakov Chaim Sofer (רב יעקב חיים סופר) of Eretz Yisrael, who was niftar in the years shortly preceding this shiur, devoted an entire chapter of his chiddushim to the question.[20] His teretz rests upon a fundamental distinction. There is a kiddush Hashem that is actualized in deed — the martyr who is in truth killed al kiddush Hashem. And there is a kiddush Hashem that consists in the acceptance itself — the Jew who takes upon himself, in full readiness, moser nefesh al kiddush Hashem, even where the gzeirah is in the event lifted from him.

His proof stands in Sefer Chasidim.[21] The sefer records that in every generation Jews have been seized by hostile authorities, informed that they were to be killed, and have accepted upon themselves moser nefesh al kiddush Hashem. In many such cases the authorities relented and the Jews were released. The ruling of the Sefer Chasidim: it is accounted as though they had died al kiddush Hashem. The koach of acceptance equals, in Shamayim, the koach of the deed itself.

Upon this principle the difficulty of the Beis Yosef’s Maggid-promise finds its resolution. The Beis Yosef’s wish to die al kiddush Hashem was so complete and so continuous — it rose from his lips daily; it accompanied his every Shema for eighty-seven years — that it fulfilled the promise of the Maggid in acceptance if not in deed. He did not need to be led to the pyre because his soul was already there. The wider reach of this teretz touches many other sugyos: the classical machlokes between the Rambam and the Ramban on whether the mitzvah of kiddush Hashem is fulfilled in machashavah alone or requires ma’aseh, the daily b’chol nafshecha at Shema, and the entire framework of lo na’aseh v’lo asah in korbanos. If acceptance suffices, the daily Shema of a tzaddik is itself no small kiddush.

Rabbeinu Bachya on the Asarah Harugei Malchus

The principle is not without precedent in the earlier mesorah. Rabbeinu Bachya (רבינו בחיי) on the Torah, in Parshas Mikeitz,[22] cites a midrash from Pirkei Heichalos (פרקי היכלות) touching on the Asarah Harugei Malchus (עשרה הרוגי מלכות). The midrash preserves a startling alternative: that in the case of certain of these kedoshim, a malach min ha’shamayim substituted another form for their bodies, and they were spared the actual harigah. If so — why then do we mourn them in Eileh Ezkerah (אלה אזכרה) on Yom Kippur, why do we recite them in the kinos of Tishah B’Av? Because they accepted upon themselves moser nefesh al kiddush Hashem. Their acceptance was itself the harigah. This is not the accepted peshat of the sugya in Avodah Zarah[23] or of our liturgy, which read the harigah as actualized; but the opinion stands within our mesorah, and it is the same principle upon which Rav Yaakov Chaim Sofer builds his teretz.

XV. The Piyut Enters the Siddur

The Yaavetz — 1730s

For a full century and a third after its composition, Yedid Nefesh stood outside the printed siddur. The first siddur to include it was Siddur Beis Yaakov of Rabbeinu Yaakov Emden (רבינו יעקב עמדין), the Yaavetz (יעב"ץ), in the 1730s — a siddur that came to stand among the most widely received in the Ashkenazi world.[24]

The Yaavetz did not place Yedid Nefesh at Shalosh Seudos, nor at Kabbalas Shabbos, nor between Korbanos and Pesukei d’Zimra. He placed it, rather, at Krias Shema Al Ha’Mitah (קריאת שמע על המטה), after Maariv. His reason: his father, the Chacham Tzvi (חכם צבי), had recited Yedid Nefesh each night before sleep. It awakened, said the Chacham Tzvi, his cheishek (חשק) and his ahavah for HaKadosh Baruch Hu. His son the Yaavetz preserved the hanhagah in print.

The Mekubalim of Tzfas — Late 1700s and Early 1800s

Some fifty years after the Yaavetz’s siddur, a siddur appeared in Livorno, Italy, that included Yedid Nefesh; from Livorno the mekubalim of Tzfas gradually incorporated the piyut into three further stations:

  • In the morning, between Korbanos and Pesukei d’Zimra, as a piyut “good to say every morning”;
  • Before Kabbalas Shabbos, in a tradition — of uncertain provenance — that attributed the placement to the Arizal;
  • At Shalosh Seudos, as a mizmor for the Shabbos afternoon table.

A Question of Minhag

Shalosh Seudos presents no difficulty: one may sing any mizmor that draws the soul. But the insertion of an extra piyut into tefillah proper touches a wider and much-debated parshah — the whole question, running from the Kalir piyutim in the machzor through the Rambam’s well-known reservations and the Vilna Gaon’s selective pruning, of what may and may not be added to the fixed nusach. The recitation of Yedid Nefesh in the morning between Korbanos and Pesukei d’Zimra is a minhag of no more than one hundred and fifty to two hundred years’ standing. It is, in the end, a beautiful hanhagah, and the tzibbur that says it says a shir composed by one of the greatest mekubalim of the Tzfas generation.

XVI. Physical Remnants of Shlomo Molcho in Prague

Physical relics of Molcho are preserved to this day in Prague, in two of the historic shuls of the city.[25] In the Altneu Shul (אלטנוישול) — the shul of the Maharal (מהר"ל) — is preserved a flag of Shlomo Molcho, inscribed with pesukim from Tehillim: the messianic standard he carried from court to court. In the Meisel Synagogue is preserved a second flag, inscribed Maccabi — Mi Kamocha Ba’eilim Hashem, and his tachrichim (תכריכים) — the burial shrouds of the man himself. The witnesses of an Anus-turned-tzaddik-turned-kadosh stand today upon the walls of a city he never lived in, testifying across five centuries to a life that ordinary categories can barely contain.

XVII. Closing

The paradox that Yedid Nefesh preserves is not easily exhausted. A piyut was composed by mekubalim in Tzfas whose deliberate tefillah was to defeat a promise given from Shamayim itself — a promise the Beis Yosef had begged for and been granted, and which those who came after him understood Klal Yisrael could not endure to see fulfilled. The tefillah succeeded so completely that the Beis Yosef lived to eighty-seven and gave to the Jewish people the Shulchan Aruch; and the piyut through which it succeeded entered, in later generations, into the daily siddur, so that today the tzibbur recites it morning by morning without knowing that they are asking for the survival of the man to whose hand they themselves owe the ordering of their entire Yiddishkeit.

Rabbeinu Elazar Azkari’s teaching in Sefer Charedim was that ahavas Hashem must be spoken; that love, unspoken, is not love. He composed a shir ahavah in obedience to his own teaching, and the shir ahavah carried within its stanzas the whispered plea of the chevra shel chaverim of Tzfas. Both meanings are true; both were sung together; both have made their way, without our knowledge, into the siddur. From this day forward, one who says Yedid Nefesh in the morning between Korbanos and Pesukei d’Zimra, or on Erev Shabbos before Kabbalas Shabbos, or at Shalosh Seudos as the light fades, may know what he is doing when he sings it. He is voicing the love of the neshamah for its Creator; and he is voicing the ancient plea of the mekubalim of Tzfas — the plea for the survival of the man who gave Klal Yisrael the Shulchan Aruch, whose tefillah for his own kiddush Hashem was fulfilled in Shamayim in the accepting, and in this world in a life extended one further hour and one further year and one further chapter, so that the halachah of Klal Yisrael should have its mesader.

This is the piyut of Yedid Nefesh. This is what we say when we say it.
