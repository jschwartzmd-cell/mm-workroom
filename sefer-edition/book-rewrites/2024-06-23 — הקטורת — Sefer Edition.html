הַקְטֹרֶת — הִיסטוֹרִיה, סוֹד וְהַשְפָעָה

The Ketores: History, Mystery, and Impact
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Sunday Morning Series on Tefillah
Recorded 23 June 2024  ·  Sefer Edition · Revised 10 August 2026

L’ilui nishmas Rav Moshe Wolfson, zt”l זצ״ל, who was niftar the morning this teaching was delivered, and whose manner of prayer — trembling with gratitude at Nishmas kol chai each morning well into his tenth decade — is itself a commentary on all that follows.

I. The Ketores within the Order of Prayer

The Practice of the Yeshiva World and Its Justification

Among the sections that precede the formal structure of Shacharis (שחרית) — Pesukei d’Zimra (פסוקי דזמרא) and the Shema with its blessings — there stands a prior stratum: the recitation of the daily korbanos (קרבנות) and the parshas ha’ketores (פרשת הקטורת). In the world of the great Lithuanian yeshivos (ישיבות), it became common to begin davening directly from Baruch She’amar (ברוך שאמר), omitting the preceding sections of Korbanos and Ketores. This practice rests on explicit halakhic ground. The Mishnah Berurah (משנה ברורה)[1] rules that one who is osek b’Torah (עוסק בתורה) — genuinely occupied with Torah study — is not obligated to recite Korbanos. There is a precedent of the highest order: Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai, whose entire existence was Torah (Torasan umnasan — תורתן אומנותן), was obligated only in Kriyas Shema (קריאת שמע), for his Torah study was itself his prayer. For the rest of human beings, reciting Korbanos is a mitzvah of significant weight, but one that yields to sustained Torah study.

The Theological Foundation of Korbanos Recitation

The deeper question is why reciting these passages carries such weight in the first instance. The Talmud formulates the principle: תְּפִלּוֹת כְּנֶגֶד תְּמִידִין תִּקְּנוּם — the daily prayers were instituted to correspond to the daily sacrifices.[2] Prayer, in this understanding, is not merely a petition but the spiritual analog of the avodah (עבודה) of the Beis HaMikdash (בית המקדש). When one recites the passages describing the korbanos — their laws, their procedures, their Talmudic elaboration — it is accounted as though one has actually brought the offering. The recitation does not merely precede prayer; it constitutes a form of avodah that elevates the tefillah that follows. Today the focus narrows specifically to the ketores — the incense offering — whose place in the liturgy is the product of one of the most consequential developments in post-medieval Jewish religious history.

II. The Zohar HaKadosh and the Transformation of the Siddur

To appreciate the presence of parshas ha’ketores in the modern siddur (סידור), it is necessary to reckon with how profoundly Kabbalah (קבלה), and specifically the Sefer HaZohar (ספר הזוהר), reshaped the Jewish tefillah. The siddurim of the pre-Zoharic era looked markedly different from those in use today. Many of the additions that now frame the Shemoneh Esrei (שמונה עשרה) — preceding it and following it — derive not from the enactments of the Anshei Knesset HaGedolah (אנשי כנסת הגדולה) but from the teachings of the Zohar HaKadosh and their elaboration by the Arizal (האר״י ז״ל). The principal example is the recitation of parshas ha’ketores. Every morning, the traditional Jew recites the Talmudic passage known as Pitum HaKetores (פיטום הקטורת),[3] detailing the eleven samimanim (סממנים) and the halakhos of their preparation. The source for this recitation is not the Talmud itself but the Zohar HaKadosh — a sefer whose very provenance is among the great questions of Jewish intellectual history.

III. The History and Authenticity of Sefer HaZohar

Eleven Hundred Years of Silence

Jewish tradition attributes the authorship of Sefer HaZohar to רבי שמעון בר יוחאי (Rashbi), a Tanna of the second century of the Common Era, a disciple of Rabbi Akiva who taught in the years following the destruction of the Second Temple. Yet for over eleven hundred years after Rashbi’s era, no Jewish community possessed or referenced this work.[4] The Mishnah, compiled by Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi from the teachings of Rashbi’s generation and the generations before him, contains no mention of the Zohar. Neither does the Talmud, neither the Geonim (גאונים), neither the Rambam, the Rif, or the Rosh. The work’s very silence in all of classical Jewish literature constitutes the central difficulty for those who maintain its Tannaic authorship.

The Discovery of Rabbi Moshe de León

In the middle of the thirteenth century, approximately the year 1255, a great mekubal (מקובל) from Spain named Rabbi Moshe de León announced that he had come into possession of an ancient manuscript: the very work written by Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai, containing the deepest secrets of the Torah (sodos haTorah — סודות התורה). The text, he explained, had been transmitted in secret across the generations among a select few yechidei segulah (יחידי סגולה) — exceptional individuals who possessed the spiritual capacity to receive such knowledge. The world had not yet been ready for its disclosure. The moment had now arrived.

The claim was met at once with skepticism. Many mekubalim who had dedicated their lives to the tradition of Jewish mysticism declared that they had never encountered such a text. The authenticity of the work was contested from the first hour of its appearance.

The Quest of Rabbi Yitzchak of Akko

Among those who resolved to investigate the matter was a Rishon of the first rank: רבי יצחק דְּמִן עַכּוֹ (Rabbi Yitzchak of Akko), a student of the Ramban who had lived in the city of Akko during the Ramban’s period of residence in Eretz Yisrael.[5] He resolved to dedicate his life to resolving the question, and to that end he undertook the long and arduous journey from Eretz Yisrael to Spain — a voyage that, in the thirteenth century, consumed years of a man’s life.

He arrived finally in the city where Rabbi Moshe de León was living. The moment of confrontation had come. Rabbi Yitzchak pressed him: show the manuscript. Rabbi Moshe de León, faced with this formidable authority, gave his solemn oath — בְּשֵׁם ה’ — that he possessed the ancient text at home, and he agreed to produce it the following day.

When Rabbi Yitzchak returned the next morning, the house was filled with wailing. Rabbi Moshe de León had died that night, an hour before his visitor arrived. The promised manuscript had never been produced.

After the seven days of mourning, Rabbi Yitzchak of Akko sought out the widow. He explained his mission — the journey from Eretz Yisrael, the oath, the appointment, the devastating outcome — and asked her: could he see the ancient text her husband had sworn existed?

Her answer was shattering. What are you speaking of? she said. My husband wrote it. He composed it himself. The reason he had attributed it to Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai, she continued, was purely pragmatic: Had he written it in his own name, there would have been no buyers. By attributing it to Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai, it became a bestseller.

This testimony, as recorded by Rabbi Yitzchak of Akko, poses one of the gravest evidentiary challenges to the Zohar’s traditional attribution. Yet the story does not end there. Already before Rabbi Yitzchak had departed Eretz Yisrael, two competing traditions had existed side by side: one held that the Ramban himself possessed an authentic ancient copy of the Zohar and transmitted it to Rabbi Moshe de León for copying and dissemination; the other held that the entire work was a modern composition. The controversy was not born at a widow’s doorstep — it accompanied the Zohar from the first moment of its appearance.

More striking still: in a later work, Rabbi Yitzchak of Akko himself wrote that he had become convinced of the Zohar’s authenticity. What evidence or spiritual encounter transformed his judgment he does not reveal. The reversal stands as one of the enduring mysteries of Jewish intellectual history.

IV. The Sabbatean Misuse and the Internal Challenge

Shabbetai Tzvi and the Corruption of Kabbalistic Language

By the sixteenth century, the Arizal’s immense authority had effectively settled the question of the Zohar’s acceptance for mainstream Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל). But the work’s very power made it vulnerable to misappropriation. The Zohar’s imagery is saturated with the categories of zachor u’nekeivah (זכר ונקבה) — masculine and feminine principles — and with the architecture of zivugim (זיווגים), partzufim (פרצופים), and Adam Kadmon (אדם קדמון). These categories, when read in their proper kabbalistic register, describe metaphysical processes; when read with a corrupted mind, they can be twisted into a justification for antinomian license. This is precisely what Shabbetai Tzvi (ימח שמו וזכרו), the false messiah of the seventeenth century, effected: he translated mystical language into actual violations of Torah, claiming that the redeemer’s task was to descend into impurity in order to elevate the fallen sparks.

The Frankist Continuation

The corruption deepened further in the person of Yaakov Frank, a disciple of the Sabbatean tradition, and his daughter Eva (“Chava”) Frank, who built a mass movement that spread through Poland and Bohemia in the eighteenth century. In that era, mysticism was the dominant intellectual current in Jewish life — perhaps more widely pursued even than formal halakhic study — and this hunger for spiritual experience left communities susceptible. Many Jews were drawn into the Frankist movement before its true character was recognized. The tragedy served to remind the gedolim of the generation that the Zohar’s power was matched by the danger of its misappropriation.

The Yaavetz and Linguistic Critique

In the wake of the Sabbatean upheaval, Rabbi Yaakov Emden, the Yaavetz (יעב״ץ), took up his pen against both the Sabbatean movement and the unqualified acceptance of the entire Zohar as the unalloyed word of Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai. In his Mitpachas Sefarim (מטפחת ספרים),[6] the Yaavetz employed a rigorous internal analysis to demonstrate that not every passage in the printed Zohar can bear the stamp of a second-century Tanna. He pointed to anachronisms, to Aramaic constructions that post-date the Talmudic period, and to internal contradictions. The Yaavetz was not rejecting the Zohar; he was insisting on intellectual honesty about what could and could not be attributed to Rashbi. By the Yaavetz’s time, the Zohar had already achieved canonical status through the Arizal’s authority, and this critique caused considerable alarm among the mekubalim. Yet its motivation was precisely to protect the authenticity of those passages that could confidently be attributed to Rashbi, by separating them from later accretions.

Rabbi Elazar Fleckeles: A Sworn Declaration

The challenge was taken up with equal seriousness by Rabbi Elazar Fleckeles, a prominent student of the Noda biYehudah (נודע ביהודה) who served as Av Beis Din of Prague. In his sefer Teshuvah Me’Ahavah (תשובה מאהבה),[7] Rabbi Fleckeles made a declaration of remarkable force: “I swear that not all of the Zohar is from Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai.” His proofs were largely linguistic. The Aramaic of the Zohar does not correspond to the Aramaic spoken in Eretz Yisrael in the second century; set against the Aramaic of the Talmud from those centuries, the differences are evident. Loan-words suggestive of medieval Iberian influence have been identified. Rabbi Fleckeles concluded that significant portions of the work were composed by Rabbi Moshe de León in thirteenth-century Spain.

The same responsum expresses gratitude that Emperor Francis I of Austria had prohibited the printing of the Zohar in his domains, given the ongoing distortion of its teachings by the Frankist movement and the consequent disruption to public order. That prohibition was eventually lifted, and the Zohar returned to print; today it stands among the most studied works in the Jewish world.

V. The Tradition’s Response: Mesorah, the Arizal, and the Resolution

Where the Tradition Stands

The academic argument against the Zohar’s Tannaic authorship has been pressed most systematically by modern scholars, most famously by Professor Gershom Scholem of the Hebrew University, whose careful philological and historical work assembles the case that the Zohar is the product of a circle of thirteenth-century Spanish mystics rather than a product of ancient Palestine. This scholarship is not without genuine force.

Yet the tradition of Klal Yisrael — the community of Torah Jews across the generations — has not rested its acceptance of the Zohar on philological argument. It has rested it on mesorah (מסורה): the transmission of received tradition through an unbroken chain of the greatest Torah authorities of each age. Every major posek from the sixteenth century onward has ruled on Zohar-based questions without hesitation. The Bet Yosef, the Rema, the Magen Avraham, the Taz — the architects of normative halacha — engaged Zohar as a source of binding legal consequence alongside Talmudic texts. This uniform acceptance, across cultures and centuries and communities in sharp disagreement on many other questions, is itself a form of evidence that exceeds the reach of philological analysis.

The central figure in this authentication is the Arizal. His authority derived not from historical-critical argument but from a tradition of direct spiritual reception that transcends ordinary scholarly methodology. Klal Yisrael has accepted the Arizal’s ruling on many questions of halakha and minhag that diverge from the plain meaning of the Shulchan Aruch — which is to say, the community extended to him a degree of trust that goes beyond the normal protocols of pesika. His authentication of the Zohar participates in that same trust.

A further consideration: Rabbi Yonasan Eibeschitz, himself a profound master of Kabbalah, ruled explicitly that one who does not believe in the Zohar’s authenticity is not thereby a koifer (כופר).[8] This ruling is significant in both directions. It preserves the theological legitimacy of honest dissent — the question is not one of dogmatic necessity. And it reveals, by implication, how seriously the question was taken even among those who accepted the Zohar: one does not issue such a ruling unless the question is genuine and the stakes are real.

The Vilna Gaon’s Position

The stance of the Vilna Gaon (הגאון מווילנא) is particularly illuminating. He was the foremost rabbinic intellect of the eighteenth century, and he revered every word of the Arizal with an intensity that his students unanimously attest. There is no indication that the Gaon rejected the Zohar’s general attribution to Rashbi. Yet his primary student, Rav Chaim of Volozhin, writes in Nefesh HaChaim (נפש החיים) that there were certain passages in the Zohar that his rebbe did not accept — without specifying which passages.[9] The picture is further complicated because the talmidei haGra themselves transmitted somewhat different versions of the Gaon’s view: Rav Mendel of Shklov and Rav Chaim Volozhin preserve accounts that do not fully align. The precise scope of whatever reservations the Gaon may have held is not recoverable with clarity from the available record.

What can be said with confidence is this: the Zohar, received as a whole, has shaped the inner life of the Jewish people beyond measurement. The way Jews pray, the rhythms of Jewish spiritual practice, the architecture of religious consciousness as it has been lived for five centuries — none of it can be understood without the Zohar. Whatever questions remain about the precise provenance of individual passages, the work as a living force in Jewish life stands beyond dispute.

VI. The Unique Station of the Ketores

More Beloved than Tefillah

With this background established, the Zohar HaKadosh’s teaching about the ketores can be properly heard. The Zohar in Parshas Vayakhel (פרשת ויקהל)[10] declares something that, taken at face value, is nothing less than revolutionary: the parsha of the ketores is more chaviv (חביב) — more beloved and cherished — before HaKadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא) than tefillah itself. The daily prayer, which stands at the center of the Jewish religious life and which the entire tradition regards as the primary vehicle of the human soul’s address to its Creator — this is surpassed in Divine favor by the recitation of the incense passage.

The conceptual logic of this claim runs through the very root of the word. Ketores shares its etymology with kesher (קשר): a bond, a knot, a tying-together. Tefillah is the human being’s approach to God through the medium of language and petition; it moves through the normal channels of finite address to infinite addressee. The ketores, by contrast, represents something more direct: a binding of the human and the Divine that bypasses the mediating structure of verbal prayer. In the Beis HaMikdash, the incense rose as a column of fragrant smoke before the Kodesh HaKodashim (קדש הקדשים); it was offered in the most interior of the Temple’s spaces, before the very place of the Divine Presence. Its recitation in tefillah carries that intimacy into the age of exile.

Because of this elevated status, the Zohar instructs that the parsha of the ketores be recited both before and after the daily prayer — as a frame, an enhancement, a bracketing of ordinary tefillah with an act of yet deeper avodah. There is an internal resonance here with the Temple service itself: in the Beis HaMikdash, the ketores was offered after the korban tamid (קרבן תמיד), giving the order of our davening a structural correspondence with the order of the ancient avodah.

The Condition of Kavvanah

The Zohar’s promise is conditioned on kavvanah (כוונה) — on reciting the passage with genuine understanding and inner intention. The Chofetz Chaim (חפץ חיים) elaborates in the Mishnah Berurah that in the present age this condition is fully achievable: the passage is brief, its ingredients are discussed in the Talmud, and accessible works — among them Rabbi Aryeh Kaplan’s The Living Torah[11] — offer clear identification of each of the eleven samimanim. A few minutes of prior study suffices to bring the daily recitation into the realm of genuine kavvanah.

Rabbi Shimon’s Testimony

אָמַר רַבִי שִמְעוֹן, אִלּוּ הֲווֹ יָדְעִין בְנֵי נָשָא כַמָה עִילָאָה עֲבוֹדָתָא דִקטֹרֶת קֳדָם קוּדשָא בְרִיך הוּא, הֲווֹ נָטְלִין כּל מִלָּה וּמִלָּה מִנֵיה וְסַלְקִין לָה עַל רֵישַיְיהוּ כְכִתְרָא דִדְהֲבָא.
Rabbi Shimon said: If human beings would know how elevated the service of the ketores is before the Holy One, Blessed be He, they would take every single word of it and place it upon their heads like a crown of gold.
Zohar, Parshas Vayakhel

The Segulos of Daily Recitation

The Zohar elaborates: daily recitation of parshas ha’ketores, performed with kavvanah, has the power to annul harsh decrees (dinim — דינים), to protect from disturbing thoughts (hirhurim ra’im — הרהורים רעים), and to draw down yeshuah (ישועה) in time of need. It is also a recognized segulah (סגולה) for material blessing (ashirus — עשירות). For this reason, many communities commission a parchment with parshas ha’ketores written in ksav Ashuris (כתב אשורית) — the script of a Sefer Torah — to be read after davening. There is a traditional wordplay here: reading from Ashuris (spelled with aleph — the name of the script) is a segulah for ashirus (spelled with ayin — wealth).

VII. The Ketores Enters the Siddur: The Shelah HaKadosh

A Liturgical Revolution

The recitation of parshas ha’ketores before and after davening was not always a feature of the standard siddur. Earlier siddurim — including those compiled under the influence of the greatest Ashkenazic authorities — did not include the ketores in this liturgical position. The innovation was the work of the Arizal, who, drawing on the teaching of the Zohar in Parshas Vayakhel, directed that the ketores be recited both before and after davening. But the figure most responsible for embedding this practice permanently in the text of the siddur used by all subsequent generations is the Shelah HaKadosh (השל"ה הקדוש) — Rabbeinu Yeshaya HaLevi Horowitz.[12]

Rabbeinu Yeshaya HaLevi Horowitz and Shaar HaShamayim

Rabbeinu Yeshaya was a product of the great Ashkenazic world of Prague — Bohemia, in the Habsburg realm — whose father had studied under the Rema (הרמ"א), placing him at the intersection of the Ashkenazic halakhic tradition and the emerging influence of Lurianic Kabbalah. In 1622, he undertook the journey to Eretz Yisrael, a voyage of approximately a year by way of Syria. He arrived first in Jerusalem and later settled in Tiberias, where he is buried near the Rambam.

The week he disembarked — at Haifa or Akko — the Torah reading was Parshas Vayetzei, which contains the verse: אֵין זֶה כִּי אִם בֵּית אֱלֹקִים וְזֶה שְַעַר הַשָּמָיִם — “This is none other than the house of God, and this is the gate of Heaven.” He took this as a sign and named the siddur he was preparing Shaar HaShamayim (שַעַר הַשָּמַיִם).

In the last will and testament that he composed for his children in his final years — he died in Tiberias in 1630 — he instructed them to publish his siddur. He added a note of urgency: he had nearly forgotten to include one crucial element. Do not forget to add the ketores. Since the publication of Shaar HaShamayim, virtually every subsequent siddur — across all major rites and communities — has included parshas ha’ketores both before and after davening.

VIII. The Three Pesukim: Bond and Battle Cry

A Yerushalmi Source and the Arizal’s Addition

Immediately following the recitation of the ketores, every siddur includes three verses from Tehillim, each recited three times. This practice, too, originates with the Arizal, who grounded it in the Zohar’s teaching that these verses are deeply connected to the esoteric significance of the ketores. The Zohar explicates the ketores as an act of kesher — of bonding the human with the Divine — and these verses, each repeated three times, extend and deepen that bond.

The first two of the three verses appear in the Yerushalmi Berachos,[13] which teaches that a Jew ought to keep them constantly upon his lips. The Yerushalmi does not include the third verse; the Arizal added it and instituted the threefold repetition of each.

The Hidden Radak: Israel’s Battle Cry

The third of these verses — ה’ הוֹשִיעָה, הַמֶּלֶך יַעֲנֵנוּ בְיוֹם קָרְאֵנוּ — carries a dimension of meaning that few who recite it daily suspect. The commentator Rabbi David Kimchi (Radak) on Tehillim[14] records that this verse served as the battle cry of the Jewish nation. When the armies of Israel advanced toward their enemies in ancient times, this is the verse that rose from their throats: Hashem, save! May the King answer us on the day we call.

ה' הוֹשִיעָה, הַמֶּלֶך יַעֲנֵנוּ בְיוֹם קָרְאֵנוּ.
Hashem, save; may the King answer us on the day we call.
Tehillim 20:10, with Radak ad loc.

The verse that rises from a Jew’s lips every morning, after the recitation of parshas ha’ketores, is the same verse that Jewish soldiers called out running toward their enemies. The tefillah of the exile and the battle cry of ancient Israel are one and the same. This is not a detail; it is a statement about the nature of Jewish prayer itself — that it has always been the mode in which a people without earthly supremacy calls upon the only power that matters.

IX. Closing

The structure of the Jewish tefillah — with its layers of Korbanos, Ketores, Pesukei d’Zimra, Shema, and Shemoneh Esrei — is not a single composition but the accumulated deposit of Halacha, Midrash, and Kabbalah across many centuries. Each stratum has its source and its authority. The ketores passage stands as the clearest example of Zoharic teaching reshaping the practical religious life of Klal Yisrael: from the Zohar’s revelation of its unique status, through the Arizal’s integration of it into the daily order, to the Shelah HaKadosh’s deathbed insistence that it be preserved in the siddur for all time — at every stage, the greatest figures of the tradition recognized that this passage was not optional decoration but essential avodah.

The ketores is a kesher: a bond between the human being and the Creator that inheres not in petition, not in praise, not in verbal formulation alone, but in the act of directing one’s attention, word by word, to the most intimate of all the Temple’s offerings. What the aged tzaddik achieved through long decades of patient tefillah — that trembling, grateful union that can be visible on the face and in the body of a person who has learned truly to pray — the ketores is, in the teaching of Rashbi, the path toward which all of this striving points.

  8. R’ Yonason Eibeschitz, Ya’aros Devash; cf. parallel formulations in his drashos. — The ruling that one who does not believe in the authenticity of the Zohar is not thereby a koifer. The same position is implied by other poskim who accepted the Zohar while preserving the legitimacy of honest dissent. The ruling reveals the gravity with which the authenticity question was treated even by those who themselves revered the sefer. ↩
  9. R’ Chaim of Volozhin, Nefesh HaChaim, Sha’ar III–IV (general reference). — Rav Chaim records that there were certain passages in the Zohar the Vilna Gaon did not accept, without specifying which. The account is reflected as well in additions preserved in early Vilna prints of the Zohar. The talmidei haGra — notably R’ Mendel of Shklov and R’ Chaim Volozhin — preserved somewhat different versions of the Gaon’s position. The precise scope of whatever reservations the Gaon held is not recoverable with full clarity from the surviving record. ↩
  10. Zohar, Parshas Vayakhel (Zohar II, the section on ketores). — The parsha of the ketores as the most beloved service before HaKadosh Baruch Hu: the Zohar teaches its power to annul decrees, shield from hirhurim ra’im, break harsh dinim, and draw down yeshuah. The famous ma’amar of אָמַר רַבִי שִמְעוֹן, אִלּוּ הֲווֹ יָדְעִין בְנֵי נָשָא… appears in this section, urging that every word of Pitum HaKetores be treated as a crown of gold. ↩
  11. R’ Aryeh Kaplan, The Living Torah (Moznaim, 1981). — Rabbi Aryeh Kaplan’s English Torah translation and anthology, with extensive endnotes identifying each of the eleven samimanim of the ketores and explaining their botanical and historical background. Recommended as a practical aid for achieving kavvanah when reciting parshas ha’ketores. ↩
  12. R’ Yeshaya HaLevi Horowitz (the Shelah HaKadosh), Shnei Luchos HaBris; Siddur Shaar HaShamayim. — The Shelah’s tzava’ah to his children to publish his siddur and to “not forget to add the ketores” is the historical pivot point for parshas ha’ketores entering the standard liturgy. The Shelah arrived in Eretz Yisrael in 1622 and is buried in Tiberias near the Rambam. The siddur takes its name from the verse read on Parshas Vayetzei the week he arrived: אֵין זֶה כִּי אִם בֵּית אֱלֹקִים וְזֶה שְַעַר הַשָּמָיִם. ↩
  13. Yerushalmi Berachos, ch. 5 (the sugya of ה' צבאות עמנו / ה' צבאות אשרי אדם). — The Yerushalmi teaches that a person should have these two verses constantly upon his lips. The third verse — ה' הוֹשִיעָה, הַמֶּלֶך יַעֲנֵנוּ — is not in the Yerushalmi; the Arizal added it and instituted that each of the three be recited three times. All standard siddurim reflect this Arizal arrangement. ↩
  14. Radak on Tehillim 20:10. — ה' הוֹשִיעָה, הַמֶּלֶך יַעֲנֵנוּ בְיוֹם קָרְאֵנוּ — identified by the Radak as the battle cry of Klal Yisrael: the verse the Jewish nation called out when advancing into battle, in the same way that surrounding nations had their own battle cries. The Radak reads the entire twentieth chapter of Tehillim as a poem of military and spiritual preparation for war, with this final verse as the cry with which the people went forward. The verse thus recited each morning after parshas ha’ketores is not merely a liturgical flourish; it is the ancient voice of Israel in extremis, carried into the age of prayer. ↩
