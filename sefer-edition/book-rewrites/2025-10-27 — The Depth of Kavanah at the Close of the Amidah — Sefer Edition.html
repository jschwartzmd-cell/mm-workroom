עֹמֶק הַכַּוָּנָה בִּסְיוּם תְּפִלַּת שְׁמוֹנֶה עֶשְׂרֵה

The Depth of Kavanah at the Close of the Amidah
Yihyu Leratzon, Elokai Netzor, Dust and Stars, the Four Lema’an Phrases, and the Three Steps Back
27 October 2025  ·  Sefer Edition  ·  ה׳ חשון תשפ״ו

לעילוי נשמת ר׳ חסקל פלדמן ז״ל, whose yahrtzeit fell in the week of the discourse; לעילוי נשמת הרבנית ויינגרטן ליבע שמילצר ע״ה, on her second yahrtzeit; and לעילוי נשמת יאיר בן בנימין ז״ל.

The close of the Amidah (עמידה) is a small text of great weight. In a few lines (Yihyu Leratzon, Elokai Netzor, and the four phrases of lema’an) the worshipper gathers everything he has prayed, asks that even the thoughts he could not utter be received, and prepares to step out into a day of dealings with other people. This discourse follows that close line by line: the hidden structure of the verse Yihyu Leratzon (יהיו לרצון); the history of Elokai Netzor (אלהי נצור) and the reason why one prayer, out of the many personal prayers of the amoraim (אמוראים), became the prayer of all Israel; its closing request, “let my soul be as dust to all”, and what the dust teaches about humility; the four phrases of lema’an and the teaching of the Chasam Sofer (חתם סופר) that they are the bow set in the cloud; and the three steps back.

The discourse is as much about practice as about sources. It asks the worshipper to use his own words after the fixed text, to say Yihyu Leratzon slowly, to hold in two pockets the words “the world was created for me” and “I am dust and ash”, and to understand the three steps back as the leave-taking of a servant from the presence of his King.

Two matters are treated with particular care. The stories of Yoma 53, of disciples who took leave of their teachers walking backward, are set out as the Gemara (גמרא) tells them; and the discourse’s reports about Nebuchadnezzar’s steps and about the reason given for the three steps by the Mahari”a are set beside the texts of the Gemara and of the Beis Yosef (בית יוסף). Where a report in the discourse differs from the text consulted, or could not be checked, the endnotes say so.

I. The Ten Words and Ten Yuds of Yihyu Leratzon (יהיו לרצון)

The verse with which the Amidah (עמידה) closes, יִהְיוּ לְרָצוֹן אִמְרֵי פִי וְהֶגְיוֹן לִבִּי לְפָנֶיךָ ה׳ צוּרִי וְגוֹאֲלִי (Tehillim, תהלים 19:15), is said, by a widespread custom, twice: once before Elokai Netzor (אלהי נצור) and once after it. The Seder HaYom (סדר היום) of Rabbi Moshe ben Machir (רבי משה בן מכיר, also spelled ben Mochir), a scholar of Safed in the days of the Arizal (האר״י) and the Beis Yosef (בית יוסף), treats this verse with exceptional gravity.[1]

He calls it a segulah (סגולה) to say the verse with kavanah (כוונה), and says that it must be said slowly and not mumbled. In this one sentence is gathered every intention that accompanied the prayer and every word of the Shemoneh Esrei (שמונה עשרה): the worshipper asks that all of it be accepted, even the thoughts that could not be put into words.

The Seder HaYom adds a hidden structure. The verse in full is ten words; it opens with a yud and closes with a yud; and the letter yud appears in it ten times.[2] This, he writes, is a great secret (סוד): those who know the secrets of the Torah concentrate on this verse, and when they do, Heaven answers their prayer. For the rest, the lesson is plainer: the verse is not to be rushed.

II. How Elokai Netzor Entered the Amidah

Not Originally a Part of the Amidah

Elokai Netzor is a relatively late fixture of the liturgy. It appears that the Rishonim (ראשונים) did not recite it as part of the Amidah. There is a single early hint among the Geonim (גאונים): Rav Amram gaon (גאון) (רב עמרם גאון) mentions the recitation of prayers of this kind,[3] but they were optional, the province of individuals (yechidim, יחידים), and not meant for all.

Strictly, then, Elokai Netzor is not part of the Amidah. Yet over the centuries it was so thoroughly absorbed that it now carries halachic weight. One who has not yet said Yihyu Leratzon before it is regarded as still within the prayer, and may not respond to Kaddish (קדיש), Kedushah (קדושה) or Barechu (ברכו); he must remain silent.[4]

The Avudraham (אבודרהם), one of the great Rishonim on the liturgy, is reported to testify that in his day the Amidah ended with its concluding blessing, after which the worshipper took three steps back, and that was all.[5] It is reported also that the early printers of the siddur (סדור) had a large share in what followed: they placed Elokai Netzor at the end of every Amidah, and in time it became, in practice, a part of it.

Its Source in the Gemara

The source is Berakhos (ברכות) 16b–17a,[6] where a full page of Gemara (גמרא) records the short personal prayers that individual sages would say after their Amidah: Rabbi Elazar, Rabbi Yochanan, Rabbi Zeira, Rabbi Chiya, Rav, Rebbi and Rav Safra; then Rabbi Alexandri, Rava, Mar son of Ravina and Rav Sheshes. From these, a single prayer was chosen and set at the close of every Amidah as the common request of all Israel: that of Mar son of Ravina (מר בריה דרבינא), Elokai netzor leshoni mera, “My God, guard my tongue from evil.”

III. Each in His Own Words

The choice of one prayer for all has a counterweight. The Men of the Great Assembly (אנשי כנסת הגדולה) fixed a single text of the Shemoneh Esrei for all Israel, and it takes months of study to see how its words embrace every request a Jew may have. But the very page of Gemara that records the personal prayers of the sages teaches that, after the fixed text, each worshipper has a mouth, thoughts, requests and gratitude of his own.[7] The request may be specific: a meeting at ten o’clock, a transaction that must close. It may be made in any language that opens the heart, whether Hebrew, Yiddish or English. One who had no kavanah in Modim (מודים) may give his thanks again, in his own words. Heaven knows thoughts as well as words (hegyon libi, הגיון לבי), yet it is fitting to give them voice.

The custom, accordingly, is to say the prayer of Mar son of Ravina, and after it one’s own. There is also a practical reason for saying Yihyu Leratzon first. One who has much to say to his Creator may find that the shaliach tzibur (שליח ציבור) has already reached Kedushah; having said Yihyu Leratzon, he is free to interrupt for Kaddish, Kedushah and Barechu, and may then make his requests at leisure.

Sensitivity in Prayer

It is told of a beloved rav of the community, whose first yahrtzeit (יארצייט) was then approaching, that when he saw the congregation waiting for him because he was praying a longer Amidah, he would omit Elokai Netzor.[8] Some might say, in astonishment, “The rav did not say Elokai Netzor!” But it is no cause for alarm. The prayer is said by all, yet to omit it in such a case is not like omitting a blessing of the Amidah, and it lies within what the Sages permitted. The Rishonim, whom we revere, did not say it at all. The lesson drawn is the sensitivity to the tzibur (ציבור) of a true mensch.

IV. Why This Prayer, of All the Prayers of the Amoraim?

Among the many beautiful personal prayers of Berakhos 17, why did those who built a prayer into the Amidah choose Mar’s אלהי נצור לשוני מרע, “guard my tongue from evil”? Two answers are given.

Guard My Tongue from Evil

The Gemara teaches that everyone stumbles in lashon hara (לשון הרע), if only in its avak (אבק), its dust, the faintest hint of it.[9] The discourse adds: every day. We think ourselves righteous; yet the hint of evil speech is the hardest to avoid. Of all the commandments and transgressions in the Torah, this is the one for which we ask Heaven’s help at the close of every Amidah: help me guard myself from evil speech today.

And My Lips from Speaking Deceit

A second reading, which the discourse ascribes to the Acharonim (אחרונים), takes the second half of the same request, ושפתי מדבר מרמה, “and my lips from speaking deceit,”[10] as a confession. Mar stood before Heaven for the Amidah, two minutes or ten. Like all of us, he spoke; sometimes he scarcely knew where he was; he mumbled the words, said and said, and at times did not understand what he was saying. “Master of the World, if my heart was not in what my lips said, then I spoke falsely before You, speaking to the King without being present. Forgive me; help me to align my heart and my mouth.”

Read so, Elokai Netzor is an apology at the close of the Amidah. It admits that the worshipper may have been absent the whole time, in fantasies and daydreams, wherever the mind wandered, and it asks for help to be present and focused next time, so as not to come out speaking deceit before the King. It is a beautiful way to end the Amidah: honest in its vulnerability, in the knowledge that Heaven understands that we are human.

V. “Let My Soul Be as Dust to All”

The Plain Reading

The prayer closes with ונפשי כעפר לכל תהיה, “and let my soul be as dust to all.”[11] afar (עפר) is earth, dust, sand. The Rishonim, it is said, explain the request thus. The worshipper is about to go out from Shacharis (שחרית) into his day, and he will deal with other people; and wherever there are people there are quarrels, arguments and misunderstandings, which are the human condition. He does not wish to fight, to speak negatively, to speak falsehood or lashon hara. Of those who curse him and provoke him he asks that his soul be like the dust that all tread upon: whatever happens today, may he not respond wrongly. You take care of it, he says to Heaven; I need not be involved.

The Vilna Gaon: The Reward of Dust

The Vilna gaon (הגר״א) teaches something deeper, which the discourse thinks he draws from the Zohar.[12] People want good children. Heaven promised the Patriarchs that their children would be like the stars of heaven and like the dust of the earth, which cannot be counted. If a Jew resolves to be like the dust that all tread upon, and despite his pride, which urges him, “Will you let so-and-so humiliate you? Answer him!”, he refuses, then he merits the reward of dust: children as numerous as the grains of sand, and good ones. middah keneged middah (מידה כנגד מידה).

The Maharsha: In the End the Dust Prevails

The Maharsha (מהרש״א) is reported to add a quietly humorous reading.[13] Make yourself like dust that all tread on: what is the end of the story? Everyone treads on dust, but at the end the dust treads on us; we all end beneath it. One who makes himself dust now, in humility, will in the end come out on top, as the dust outlives those who walk on it. The prayer that follows is that we merit length of days and years.

The Land Speaks

It is told of Rabbi Chaim of Volozhin (רבי חיים מוולוז׳ין)[14] that two men came to him with a quarrel over a piece of land. Each said, “The land is mine!”, and no one could make peace between them. “Let me hear what the land says,” said Rabbi Chaim. He took them out to the field, bent his ear to the ground, listened a moment, and rose. “The land says that you both belong to it; it does not belong to you. You will both end in the land. What are you fighting for? One hundred and twenty years, and you belong to the land.” One who makes himself as dust ends as the dust does: on top.

VI. Two Schools of Humility: Novardok and Slabodka

The making of oneself into dust was taken to an extreme in Novardok (נוברדוק).[15] The Mussar (מוסר) movement, founded by Rabbi Yisrael Salanter (רבי ישראל סלנטר), developed in the next generation in two directions. Novardok, led by the Alter, Rabbi Yosef Yozel Horowitz (רבי יוסף יוזל הורוביץ), was radical: it emphasized putting oneself down. Slabodka (סלבודקה) emphasized gadlus ha'adam (גדלות האדם), the greatness of man: you are the crown of creation, and should walk with confidence and pride.

Here, it is said, lies the origin of a certain air among older bachurim (בחורים) toward younger ones: I am older; I am holding up the world with my Torah. The thought was that at the impressionable ages of sixteen, seventeen and eighteen, young men should feel that they sustain the world by their learning. The method can go either way; it can turn into what the lecturer calls, with an apology for the phrase, “Litvish ga’avah” (גאוה). Its source is good; its execution requires discipline.

Novardok took the opposite course. Its young men would go into a pharmacy and ask for a package of nails, knowing perfectly well that a pharmacy sells none, in order to be shouted at, to be looked at as nothing, to be treated as fools; it was a deliberate technique to bring a person to shiflus (שפלות), true lowliness. afar is the paradigm of this avodah (עבודה).

VII. The Two Pockets

The present generation speaks incessantly of self-esteem, and in a generation that needs elevation this is true. Yet we are adults, and there is no reason why we should not also work on humility (ענוה). The answer is given in a saying of Rabbi Simcha Bunim of Peshischa (רבי שמחה בונים מפשיסחא):[16] every Jew must have two pockets. In one pocket, the words בשבילי נברא העולם, “the world was created for me” (Sanhedrin 37a): for my service, for my Torah, for my prayer; if I am not here, the world is lacking, “if I am not for myself, who will be for me?” In the other pocket, ואנכי עפר ואפר, “I am but dust and ash” (Bereishis 18:27). It is a balance, and it takes a wise person to maintain it; each person has his own service in this.

The Kli Yakar (כלי יקר) is cited in connection with the two similes that Heaven uses of the Patriarchs’ children: as the dust of the earth, and as the stars of heaven.[17] Dust is humble and simple, a grain of sand; a star is an entire universe, which looks small from here but is enormous up close. A Jew must know both at once: I am a star in heaven, and my light shines very far; and also, I am a grain of sand. He must hold both.

VIII. The End of Elokai Netzor: “You Take Care of It”

The closing petitions of Elokai Netzor concern others. Whoever plans to bring me down today, whoever plans to take advantage of me, a business partner, a friend, someone speaking ill of me: You see to it, so that I need not deal with it. Some texts add, before the closing yehi ratzon, a further petition that no one be jealous of me and that I not be jealous of others, that I not be angry today and that none be angry with me; those who say it, say it.

Personal Kavanah: Where Do You Connect?

It is worth asking oneself, and others: which words of the Shemoneh Esrei are the ones in which you are most truly present, talking to Heaven with full attention? Each Jew has his own; one may hope that we have kavanah throughout. The lecturer shares his own: the petition for the return to Zion, ותחזינה עינינו בשובך לציון,[18] where, asking Heaven to bring everyone back together and redeem His scattered flock, he loses himself. For two thousand years we have asked three times a day for redemption and for the Temple; we beg and weep that the sufferings end. A Yiddish proverb says: ביז מ׳קומט צום קרעטשמע, דארף מען אויך אַ טרינק, “until you reach the inn, you also need a drink.” In the days of travel by horse and wagon there were inns at intervals, where one took a l’chaim, rested by the fire and moved on to the next stage; the Chassidim spoke of going from one inn to the next, a ride of three hours. Until the redemption comes, what will be? Make Your children happy. A father wants his children to be happy; if they are not, he is not. We ask Heaven to make us happy until we reach the next inn.

IX. The Four Phrases: Lema’an Shemecha

In the nusach of Sefard (נוסח ספרד) the close of the Amidah continues with four phrases, which are among the most powerful words of the whole close:[19]

עֲשֵׂה לְמַעַן שְׁמֶךָ, עֲשֵׂה לְמַעַן יְמִינֶךָ, עֲשֵׂה לְמַעַן תּוֹרָתֶךָ, עֲשֵׂה לְמַעַן קְדֻשָּׁתֶךָ.
Act for the sake of Your Name; act for the sake of Your right hand; act for the sake of Your Torah; act for the sake of Your holiness.

It is said that the Rishonim ascribe this prayer to Rabbi Akiva, and perhaps parts of it to Rabbi Eliezer. The Beis Yosef brings an aggadic source (haggadah, הגדה) that whoever says these four things every day after the Amidah merits לקבל פני שכינה, to receive the face of the Shechinah (שכינה). The Sages seldom promise so much for words said within the Amidah.

The Kavanah

What is the kavanah? Not for me. I do not deserve the requests I have just made, and I do not deserve their fulfillment; I know my sins, my thoughts, my failings. Therefore: do it for Your sake. If I have the livelihood I need, the health I need, the good children I need, it is so that I shall be able to serve You: for Your Name, for Your right hand, for Your Torah, for Your holiness. Said with kavanah, the words have tremendous power.

X. The Chasam Sofer (חתם סופר): Keshti Nasati Ba’anan

The students of the Chasam Sofer report that he gave the following teaching every year on the Sabbath of Parashas Noach (פרשת נח).[20] Of the rainbow after the Mabul (מבול) the verse says אֶת קַשְׁתִּי נָתַתִּי בֶּעָנָן, “I have set My bow in the cloud” (Bereishis, בראשית 9:13).[21] The word קשתי, he taught, is the acronym קשת״י of the four phrases:

קְדֻשָּׁתְךָ · שִׁמְךָ · תּוֹרָתְךָ · יְמִינְךָ
Kedushascha · Shimcha · Torascha · Yeminecha: your holiness, your Name, your Torah, your right hand.

Breaking Through the Cloud

The Gemara says, in words difficult for a Jew to hear, that from the day the Beis HaMikdash was destroyed the gates of prayer were locked (Berakhos 32b):[22] “Though I cry and call out, He shuts out my prayer” (Eichah, איכה 3:8). Yet the Gemara continues: even though the gates of prayer were locked, the gates of tears were not locked, for it is written, “Hear my prayer, O Lord, and give ear to my cry; be not silent at my tears” (Tehillim 39:13). A verse in Eichah (3:44) gives the image of what stands in the way: “You have screened Yourself with a cloud, that no prayer may pass through.”[23]

The teaching of the Chasam Sofer is that there is a way through that cloud, and it is what Heaven told Noach: “I have set keshti in the cloud.” If a person prays not for himself but says, “for Your Name, for Your right hand, for Your Torah, for Your holiness”, his prayer goes through the cloud. If Heaven withholds our prayer on account of our own deserts, we say: this is for Your sake, and Heaven opens the way. The keshet is also the bow of the bow and arrow; one who has this kavanah in the words, and who says them from a broken heart, shoots through the cloud. His students report that such a person is assured that his prayer will be accepted: “I have ended my prayer; all I said is for Your sake and not for mine.”

XI. The Three Steps Back

The Retreat from a Presence

Before the words Oseh Shalom (עושה שלום) (“He who makes peace in His heights”), the worshipper takes three steps back. The foundation is the Gemara in Yoma (יומא) 53.[24] The Mishnah teaches that the Kohen Gadol (כהן גדול) left the Kodesh HaKodashim (קודש הקדשים) on Yom Kippur by the way he had entered, not turning his back but walking backward with his face toward the Ark; and the Gemara derives this from Solomon, who “came to the high place at Gibeon” and then to Jerusalem (Divrei HaYamim II 1:13): as his face was toward the holy place when he arrived, so it was when he left. The principle extends. The priests at their service, the Levites on their platform, and the Israelites at their ma'amados (מעמדות) left without turning their backs; they turned sideways. ma'amados were the rotating delegations of Israelites who went up to Jerusalem for a week to stand at the korbanos (קרבנות) on behalf of the nation.[25] And so it is for a student taking leave of his teacher.

A Personal Recollection: The Farher in Brisk

The lecturer recalls that when he first went to take the farher, the entrance interview, at the yeshivah (ישיבה) of Brisk with Rabbi Meshulam HaLevi Soloveitchik (רבי משולם הלוי סולובייצ׳יק), the man who was to be his rebbi for three years and whom he already treated as such, his father had told him beforehand: when you leave a rebbi, as when you leave a Chassidic Rebbe, you take three steps back.[26] He did so on leaving the room, though the rav had not yet told him whether he was accepted; it is very hard to be accepted there. “I do not know whether that is why I was accepted, but I did get a call the next day that I had been.” People who honored their teachers did this; the Chassidim who came to the Lubavitcher Rebbe certainly did not turn their backs on him as they left.

Elazar and Yochanan, and Rava and Rav Yosef

The Gemara gives examples of a disciple’s reverence. When Rabbi Yochanan was departing, Rabbi Elazar would bow and stand where he was until Rabbi Yochanan was out of his sight; and when Rabbi Elazar himself took leave of Rabbi Yochanan, he walked backward until Rabbi Yochanan could no longer see him.

Then Rava, who took leave of Rav Yosef, who was blind. Rava walked backward from his teacher until his legs were bruised and the threshold of Rav Yosef’s house was bloodied. Ordinarily one looks behind to see whether there is an obstacle or a step; Rava did not look. So wholly was he given over to submission before his teacher that he took the steps back, stumbled, and fell again. The commentators, the lecturer reports, say that the blood was never washed away and that the dried blood blackened the threshold.

Rav Yosef could not see it. When they told him, “This is what Rava does,” he blessed him, in the Aramaic of the Gemara: יְהֵא רַעֲוָא דְּתָרוּם רֵישָׁךְ אַכּוּלֵּהּ כַּרְכָּא, “May it be [His] will that you lift your head over the whole city.”[27] The lecturer understands the city as Mechoza (מחוזא), where Rava became head of the yeshivah, at the time the center of Babylonian Torah moved to it from Pumbedita; and from there he became the teacher of all Israel. Rava is one of the fathers of the Babylonian Talmud: on every page he speaks. In the roughly three hundred disputes between Abaye and Rava, the lecturer’s estimate, the halachah (הלכה) follows Rava, except in six cases summarized by the mnemonic יע״ל קג״ם.[28] The commentators connect this with the honor he gave his teacher, never turning his back even at the cost of falling and bleeding. Humility, the discourse concludes, is a school in which a person grows in Torah.

A Question Left Open

The Gemara derives the retreat from Solomon’s departure from Gibeon. A question was raised about the wording of that verse, and the lecturer found it a good question and offered only a tentative suggestion: perhaps the verse describes what came after the leave-taking and a night’s stay there. The matter is recorded here as open.

The Practice

On the basis of this Gemara, the Shulchan Aruch (שולחן ערוך) rules (Orach Chaim 123:1) that one bows and takes three steps back in a single bow; still bowed, one turns the head to the left at oseh shalom bimromav and to the right at hu ya’aseh shalom aleinu, and bows forward like a servant taking leave of his master. The gloss adds the custom of saying, “May it be Your will that the Temple be rebuilt.”[29] One looks first to the sides to be sure that no one is standing behind; for one who is still praying behind him is, in effect, in front of the one stepping back, and it is forbidden to pass or to stand within four amos (אמות) in front of one who is praying, even though benches now separate people, which many poskim (פוסקים) say is not enough.

Why Three?

The number is stated in Yoma 53b, on the page following the stories: Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi, in the name of Rabbi Alexandri, taught that one who prays takes three steps back at the end of his prayer and then says shalom, as one leaving the presence of the Holy One; a baraita teaches the same, and adds that if he does not, it would have been better had he not prayed; and Abaye and Rava were seen to take the three steps within a single bow. The discourse, citing the page on which the stories are told, understood that the Gemara there does not give the number, and sought it elsewhere.

Nebuchadnezzar’s Steps

For the number the discourse cites a further source, which it traces to a Rabbi Kalish, who is said to have it from a Gemara: the story of Nebuchadnezzar (נבוכדנצר) in Sanhedrin (סנהדרין) 96a.[30] Before he reigned he was the scribe of Baladan (בלאדן), king of Babylon, who sent a letter of peace to Hezekiah, king of Judah. The letter was written in his absence: “Peace to King Hezekiah, peace to Jerusalem, and peace to the great God.” When he returned and saw it, he said, “Are you mad? You put God last? Put Him first, then the people, then the king of flesh and blood!” They answered, “You are the scribe; you write it, and you deliver it.” According to the Gemara he ran after the messenger for four paces, until the angel Gabriel stopped him. The lecturer related the story as three steps backward in honor of the Shechinah, and observed that the Gemara says four, so that perhaps the Rishonim had a reading of three. The Gemara records that Heaven rewarded him for those paces; in the discourse’s telling, the reward was that he was permitted to be the destroyer of the Beis HaMikdash, a link the passage itself does not make explicit.

This is Nebuchadnezzar, destroyer of the Temple, head of the wicked, whose name the midrash is said always to follow with the letters ש״ט, שחיק טמיא, “may his bones be ground to dust.” We suffer to this day from what he did to us; yet one thing he did right: he gave honor to Heaven in that letter, and took steps to honor the Shechinah. And the Acharonim say that this is the meaning of the close of the Amidah: we show Heaven that we are like Nebuchadnezzar, but better. We give honor, and we do not have the wickedness in us. Therefore we take three steps back, and ask: עושה שלום במרומיו הוא יעשה שלום עלינו, and that the Beis HaMikdash be rebuilt speedily. He took three steps back and destroyed; we take three steps back and ask Heaven to build.

XII. Rabbeinu Yitzchak Abuhav and the Three Mil

Who He Was

The Beis Yosef (Orach Chaim 123) gives a reason for the three steps in the name of רבינו הגדול מהרי״א, whom the discourse identifies as Rabbi Yitzchak Abuhav (רבי יצחק אבוהב), a figure in the transition between the Rishonim and the Acharonim, “the generation before the Beis Yosef.”[31] He was a rav and head of a yeshivah in Toledo at the time of the expulsion from Spain in 1492, and a leader of Klal Yisrael. With Don Yitzchak Abarbanel (דון יצחק אברבנאל), a wealthy royal official who is told to have tried to bribe Ferdinand and Isabella, he took part in the diplomacy that preceded the expulsion; Abuhav, a head of a yeshivah, appears not to have been wealthy. They tried; in the end they had to leave Spain.

Abuhav went to Portugal, where he met King João II, known for his conversations with Jews and rabbis, and tried to win a return of the Jews to Spain. He could not, and, pained by this, he died in Portugal in 1493. Yet the Beis Yosef calls him rabbeinu hagadol and cites him again and again; he was apparently the gadol hador, and many of our practices, as the Beis Yosef records them, come from him.

In Safed there is a synagogue named for him, Beis HaKnesses Abuhav (בית הכנסת אבוהב), part of the regular tour route. It is said, as a legend, that the Sefer Torah in its Ark, which stands there to this day, is the one he wrote; he was a sofer (סופר). A scroll of five or six hundred years, it is thought no longer to be used, but to be kept under lock and key.

His Reason for the Three Steps

The Beis Yosef cites the Mahari”a for the reason: the three steps correspond to the three mil (מיל) that Israel retreated from Mount Sinai at the giving of the Torah. According to the Gemara in Shabbos (שבת) 88b, when Israel stood at Sinai and heard Anochi (אנכי) from the mouth of the Almighty, they could not bear it. The body could not contain the divine voice; the soul departed; and they were driven back. To remind ourselves that we stand before the Shechinah, and ought to retreat in awe, we should retreat three mil. Three mil would mean walking all the way to the next town, so we commemorate it with three steps.

A Difficulty

On opening the Gemara in Shabbos 88b one finds not three mil but twelve: at each utterance Israel retreated twelve mil, and the angels led them back.[32] Rashi on Shemos (שמות) 20:15 gives twelve, and the Mechilta, it is reported, likewise; no one says three. “I do not understand for the life of me where three comes from.” Perhaps three parsa’os (parsa'os, פרסאות), each of four mil, make twelve mil; but this is conjecture. The discourse remarks that it would pay money to anyone who could resolve the difficulty. Yet this is what the Beis Yosef cites from the Mahari”a as the reason for the three steps at the end of every Amidah.

XIII. The Honor of the Shechinah and the Aron HaKodesh

Some have the practice of leaving a synagogue facing the Aron HaKodesh (ארון הקודש). This is not found in the Gemara; the discourse traces it to the Magen Avraham (מגן אברהם),[33] which draws on the principle of the Gemara. As one does not turn one’s back on one’s teacher, so one does not turn one’s back on the Ark.

XIV. An Aside: The First Blessing

A guest raised the question of where kavanah is most essential. The Gemara’s answer, as the discourse affirms, is that if one can have kavanah in only one blessing, it must be the first, which is the ikar of the Amidah. The Shulchan Aruch rules (Orach Chaim 101:1) that one who had no kavanah in the first blessing must return and pray again, and the gloss adds that nowadays one does not repeat, since a second Amidah would likely lack kavanah as well.[34] The discourse adds that one should not return to say the first blessing again at once: even without speaking, that is a hefsek (הפסק), which disturbs the structure of the Amidah that rests on that blessing. If the heart was not there, one comes back to it at the next prayer.

Here the discourse breaks off; the treatment of Tachanun (תחנון) was to follow.
