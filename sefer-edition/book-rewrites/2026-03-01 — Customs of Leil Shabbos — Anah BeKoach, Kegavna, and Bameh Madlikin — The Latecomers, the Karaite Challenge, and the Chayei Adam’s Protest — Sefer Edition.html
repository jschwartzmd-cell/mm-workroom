מִנְהָגֵי לֵיל שַׁבָּת — אָנָּא בְּכֹחַ, כְּגַוְונָא וּבַמֶּה מַדְלִיקִין: הַמְאַחֲרִים, הַקָּרָאִים וּמְחָאַת הַמִּתְנַגְּדִים

Customs of Leil Shabbos: Anah BeKoach, Kegavna, and Bameh Madlikin — The Latecomers, the Karaite Challenge, and the Chayei Adam’s Protest
Studies in the Friday-Night Tefillos (תפילות) — The Threshold of Shabbos in the Siddur
12 Adar 5786  ·  Sefer Edition

This discourse was delivered לעילוי נשמת שמואל צבי בן יהושע פייביש הכהן, in whose memory the morning’s study and breakfast were sponsored.[1] The lecturer prays that his soul have an aliyah (עלייה) and that the Torah learned and the blessings said be a merit (זכות) for him and a source of nachas (נחת).

The Friday-night service contains three small texts that stand at its threshold: Anah BeKoach (אנא בכח), said within the procession of Kabbalas Shabbos (קבלת שבת); Kegavna (כגוונא), said between Kabbalas Shabbos and Maariv (מעריב); and the chapter of the Mishnah Bameh Madlikin (במה מדליקין), whose place in the siddur has changed more than once. This discourse asks of each why it is said and where. Anah BeKoach is built on the Forty-Two-Letter Name and is placed here, according to one suggestion, because the Account of Creation is bound up with that Name. For Bameh Madlikin the lecturer sets out three reasons: the care of the Geonim for the latecomer who must walk home alone in the dark; the Rishonic view that the chapter reminds each household of what must be done before nightfall; and, told through the biography of Anan, the answer to the Karaites. Kegavna, a passage of the Zohar, was introduced into the siddur, he says, by the Baal Shem Tov and his disciples. The discourse closes with a theory, offered as a theory, that the Ashkenazic placement of Bameh Madlikin before Barchu is a protest by the Misnagdim against that Chassidic insertion, and that the Chayei Adam (חיי אדם) was its first witness.

I. A Prefatory Note: The Author of Lecha Dodi, and the News

The discourse begins where the previous week’s ended, with Lecha Dodi (לכה דודי). Its author, Rav Shlomo HaLevi Alkabetz (רב שלמה הלוי אלקבץ), wrote also a commentary on the Scroll of Esther, the Manos HaLevi (מנות הלוי); the lecturer recounts, from an article sent to him that morning, that a copy of that book lay on the table at a meeting of the highest security consultation in Israel, and he relates it as a point of interest, the more so as Purim (פורים) was near.[2] He then sets the news of the day aside. A Rebbe of Boro Park, he recalls, said in Yiddish at the time of Simchas Torah two years before: מען דארף נישט הערן נייעס, מען דארף מאכן נייעס, one need not listen to the news but make it, by learning, by davening, and by asking the Holy One for mercy on all Israel.[3] So the lecturer turns to the prayers, as he says, “we need our tefillos (תפילות).”

II. Anah BeKoach and the Forty-Two-Letter Name

Between Mizmor LeDavid (מזמור לדוד) and Lecha Dodi the congregation says Anah BeKoach (אנא בכח). The prayer is “the secret of the teaching of Kabbalah (קבלה),” an ancient prayer that was perhaps composed by Rabbi Nechunya ben HaKanah (רבי נחוניא בן הקנה), of whom the previous week’s discourse had spoken.[4] In its words the congregation asks for blessing and purification. It is built, however, on a Name of the Holy One, the Shem Mem-Beis (שם מ״ב), the Name of forty-two letters: the prayer has seven lines of six words, and the initial letters of its forty-two words are the Name itself.[5] This is why, after reciting it quietly (b’lachash (בלחש)), the congregation adds ברוך שם כבוד מלכותו לעולם ועד: one has uttered, perhaps unknowingly, the Name of God.[6]

The lecturer then offers the one source he found for the placement, the Aruch HaShulchan (ערוך השולחן). The Mishnah in Chagigah (חגיגה) (11b) says אין דורשין במעשה בראשית בשנים: the Account of Creation is not expounded before two, because there are subjects, such as the Account of the Chariot and the Account of Creation, that are learned privately and not in public.[7] The lecturer says that the Gemara explains that a person who delves into creation may be led to wrong thoughts, and that this is a matter that he himself wondered about as a child: what was there before the Holy One created the world, and what was the tohu vavohu (תהו ובהו)? Tosafos (תוספות) (Chagigah 11b), he reports, ask what the Mishnah can mean, since everyone learns the verses of the opening of the Torah, and answer that what is not to be expounded is the matter that lies behind the verses, the question of what came before; and that “מעשה בראשית” is the name of the Name of forty-two letters, by which, he says, the world was created, “higher than my comprehension.”

It follows, the Aruch HaShulchan suggests, that since Kabbalas Shabbos welcomes the Shabbos that crowns the six days of creation, Anah BeKoach, the prayer of the Name of Creation, is brought in at this point. That, the lecturer says, is “his opinion”; and he adds that he did not find any other source. He notes also that the prayer is said at other points in the service and in the Counting of the Omer, that there is “a lot of literature” on why it is said in each place, and that, according to what he has been told, some Ashkenazic siddurim do not have it.

III. Standing and Bowing at Lecha Dodi

Some, though “not too many,” stand for the whole of Lecha Dodi. The lecturer says that the Lubavitchers do so, and is told that the Syrians also do; he did not know this and wonders about the Iranians and the Ashkenazim.[8] It is, he says, a very weighty custom, which shows the excitement of one to whom Shabbos is coming. The ordinary custom, in most books of law and in the Sifsei Chachamim (שפתי חכמים), is to stand only for the last stanza, בואי בשלום, because the worshipers then re-enact what the Amoraim (אמוראים) did when they went out to greet the bride: they stand to prepare, and then turn around, not leaving the synagogue, to the west side of the beis medrash (בית מדרש), as if greeting the Shabbos as it enters.[9]

As for the bowing at בואי כלה, there are two customs, each with a source: to bow to the left, then to the right, then to the center; or to the right, then to the left, then to the center. The lecturer’s own custom is “always first to the left.” The reason for beginning on the left is that one’s left is the right of the Shechinah, “or whatever that means,” and the lecturer says that he explained the reason for the bowing itself a year and a half before, when the Korbanos were studied.[10] Whichever is done is well; the main thing is the hergesh (הרגש), the feeling, that Shabbos Kodesh is coming.

IV. When Erev Shabbos Falls on Yom Tov

When Yom Tov falls on a Friday, some do not say Lecha Dodi at all, some say only its first and last stanzas, and some Ashkenazim say nothing of it.[11] The question put in the room was why one should not accept Shabbos then, since Mizmor Shir LeYom HaShabbos is said. The reason given by the Rishonim and Acharonim, the lecturer reports, is that a special prayer to accept Shabbos is a bizayon (בזיון) to Yom Tov, which received no such prayer. “Why would it be an insult to Yom Tov?” he asks. “I’m not sure, I don’t understand it.” A participant suggests that Yom Tov, too, is received, in the sanctification of the month, and another that one does not mix one joy with another (אין מערבין שמחה בשמחה).

The lecturer then considers the second explanation, that on Yom Tov the worshiper is moving from holiness to holiness and not from weekday to holiness, and he does not accept it either. The holiness of Shabbos, he says, is greater: on Shabbos one may not cook even for food (אוכל נפש), and the additional soul (neshamah yeseirah (נשמה יתירה)) is given on Shabbos and not on Yom Tov. “There is more to be said on this,” he concludes (יש להאריך בזה), and the matter depends on custom, communities differing in exactly which parts they say. He adds that Kegavna, too, is not said on a Yom Tov that is Erev Shabbos, though the harsh judgments are no more present on Yom Tov than on Shabbos.

V. Bameh Madlikin: Three Reasons for One Chapter

The Chapter and Its Early Place

Bameh Madlikin (במה מדליקין) is the second chapter of Maseches Shabbos (מסכת שבת), which teaches with what oils and what wicks the Shabbos lamp may be lit and with which it may not: in those days, if the lamp burned poorly, a person would tilt it (matteh es haner (מטה את הנר)) to brighten the flame, which is a desecration of Shabbos, and so the Sages forbade certain oils.[12] The first time one finds a custom to say the chapter at all is in the age of the Geonim (גאונים), in the nine-hundreds, in Rav Amram Gaon (רב עמרם גאון) and Rav Nachshon Gaon (רב נחשון גאון), who direct that it be said after Kaddish (קדיש) at the end of davening, the congregation sitting down. The Rishonim of Spain, Germany and France say the same: at the end of davening, and not between Kabbalas Shabbos and Maariv.[13] That is the older custom, and the Shulchan Aruch records both the Ashkenazic custom of saying the chapter and the statement that the Sefaradim say it before Maariv.[14]

The First Reason: The Latecomer

Rav Nachshon Gaon, the lecturer says, gave a reason that is “not the reason you think.” The Sages and the Geonim and the Rishonim were all sensitive to the latecomer. Whoever comes late must pray with the congregation and must have time to say the Shema (שמע) and the Shemoneh Esrei (שמונה עשרה); the number of enactments made for the latecomers, he says, “would be mind-boggling.” He instances the blessings of the aliyah, said before and after, which once were said only by the first and last olim; a gabbai (גבאי) in the room remarks that he likes the principle.

On Friday night people come late to shul, and when the congregation has finished praying and left, the latecomer is stuck alone. In the age of the Gemara and the Geonim the synagogues were outside the city, in the fields, and the man would walk home alone in the dark: on Shabbos he could not carry a light, and there were mazikim (מזיקים), harmful spirits, “and who knows what could happen to him.” So the Geonim ordained that all remain after davening, for five or six or seven minutes, and say a chapter of the Mishnah together, so that the latecomer might catch up and the group walk home together. The lecturer notes that the congregation was then, it seems, accepting Shabbos much earlier, after pelag haminchah (פלג המנחה), so that it was still light when the latecomer arrived; the danger was the walk back.[15] “We do not want a Jew walking home alone on Friday night.”

The Second Reason: A Reminder

The Machzor Vitry (מחזור ויטרי) and other Rishonim give a different reason, which the lecturer calls “mind-boggling.” The end of the chapter has the Mishnah that says there are three things a person must say in his house on the eve of Shabbos at dusk: עישרתם? עירבתם? הדליקו את הנר: have you tithed, have you made an eiruv (עירוב) so that you may carry, and have you lit the lamp? The chapter is said so that each Jew should be reminded, “just in case,” and go home to set it right.[16] But the reason raises the difficulty of the time. If the chapter is said after Maariv, what use is the reminder? It is too late to fix anything. According to those who say it after Maariv, the lecturer explains, it is for the lesson, for the following week.

For this reason the Abudraham (אבודרהם), whom the lecturer calls Yosef Kohen Abudraham, proposed that the chapter be said not after Kaddish at the end of davening but between Kabbalas Shabbos and Maariv, so that, if any Jew had lit the wrong lamp or forgotten some last thing, he might run home and mend it. In those days, he explains, the early acceptance of Shabbos left time between the Shabbos psalms and Maariv for this. The custom changed accordingly, and the Sefaradim, he says, remained with theirs. In his own day, the lecturer remarks, it matters little: people recite Kabbalas Shabbos so close to nightfall that none has time to run home, “besides myself, who live right next door to the shul.” A questioner asks why the reminder is not then said on Erev Yom Tov, and the lecturer proceeds.

The Third Reason: The Challenge of Anan

The third reason is a matter of Jewish history, and the lecturer tells it at length. In the Geonic age there was in Babylonia the Reish Galusa (ריש גלותא), the Exilarch, “a central religious figure and also a political one,” recognized by the local government, which gave him powers to punish.[17] Much of the politics of the time of the Geonim, the lecturer says, concerned who would hold that office. In the eighth century it was held by Rav Chaninai Gaon (רב חנינאי גאון), who died in 770. His son succeeded him; when the son died, the office passed in the family, and Anan (ענן), the grandson of Chaninai, of the family of Bostanai (בוסתנאי), whose line went back to the House of David, wished to hold it.[18]

The sages of the generation did not want him: they did not think him sufficiently God-fearing. He was a Torah scholar, wealthy, and with a following. He complained to the authorities in Baghdad, informed against the sages, and in the end was himself imprisoned on a charge of rebellion; after some months he persuaded the governor, who was not a bad man and shared their laws, and was released; and Anan became Reish Galusa. Then he decided to be revenged on the heads of the yeshivos and the Geonim. He followed the Karaites, who do not believe in the Oral Torah, though he knew the Mishnah and the Gemara well, and he decided that the community should follow only the Written Torah. His followers, the Ananim (עננים), were a continuation of the early Karaites. The lecturer adds that a Karaite synagogue in Ashdod is named for him, and that at the end of his life he went up to Jerusalem and built a synagogue there, in a small alley in the Jewish Quarter of the Old City, where the lecturer, as a young man, knocked and no one answered. Anan is buried on the Mount of Olives. “The worst,” the lecturer says; “he took thousands of Jews and turned them from pious Jews into Karaites.”

What, then, was the chief matter on which the Karaites differed? The verse says לא תבערו אש בכל מושבותיכם ביום השבת.[19] The rabbinic interpretation is that one may not kindle a fire on Shabbos, but a fire lit before Shabbos may burn; this is why we eat cholent (חמין) on Shabbos morning, the lecturer says, to show that the fire was burning all night. The Karaites lit no fire before Shabbos, and sat for twenty-four or twenty-six hours in darkness with cold food. “What type of Shabbos would that be?” And thousands held that this was the Shabbos the Holy One wanted. The Geonim, wishing to take away Anan’s authority, wished to teach the people what it is to keep Shabbos: with the lamp lit, with the Shabbos candles, with peace in the home, and with hot food. Therefore, say some of the Rishonim, they ordained that the chapter במה מדליקין be said, to show that “we do light,” and that the only question the Tannaim have is with what oils and wicks. The lecturer observes that the chapter’s placement in the siddurim of Rav Natronai Gaon, Rav Hai, or Rav Amram came, it appears, right after Anan went up to Eretz Yisrael, and that “many say it makes no difference when you say it”: the point is to remind Jews, before they go home, that Shabbos is with candles.[20]

A questioner asked about Shabbos Chanukah, when the chapter is said by some and not by others; the lecturer answered that the sugya of Chanukah is itself in this chapter (Shabbos 25–28).[21] The lecturer observes that nowadays none of these reasons applies: no one goes home from shul to tell his wife to light the candles, since it is already Shabbos; no one is in danger of following the Karaites; and no one comes late and walks home alone, since the synagogues are in the middle of the city and the regular Friday-night schedule is kept.

VI. Kegavna: The Baal Shem Tov’s Insertion

What, then, is said at this threshold in the Chassidic rite, and in the rite called Nusach Sefard? Kegavna (כגוונא) (the full opening is כגוונא דאינון מתייחדין) is a passage of the Zohar in Parashas Terumah (פרשת תרומה), in Aramaic, which speaks of כל שולטני רוגזין, the harsh judgments, which do not exist on Shabbos, and which praises and elevates the stature of Shabbos.[22] The Zohar does not say anywhere that the passage should be said before Maariv on Friday night. How did it come to be in every siddur? The lecturer answers that it is a Chassidic custom, “from the disciples of the Baal Shem Tov (בעל שם טוב), nothing before that,” and that whoever davens in the rite called Nusach Sefard without any connection to the Chassidim “got stuck with it.”[23] What the Baal Shem Tov wished, the lecturer says, was to say something that would stir excitement for the holiness of Shabbos, and so he took a piece of the Zohar and put it in the siddur, between Kabbalas Shabbos and Maariv. After a thousand years in which Israel had said Bameh Madlikin, the Chassidim came and said Kegavna. Participants of Sefardic background say that they do say Kegavna, and that they say Bameh Madlikin before Kabbalas Shabbos; the lecturer finds this “fascinating,” and observes that his own theory concerns the difference between the Ashkenazim and the Sefaradim.

The Piaseczner Rebbe

The lecturer adds that a book of the Piaseczner Rebbe (פיאסצנער רבי) speaks of Kegavna. Rav Kalonymus Kalmish Shapira, a descendant of the Noam Elimelech (נועם אלימלך), was a Rebbe in Warsaw, with a small rabbinate in Piaseczno not far from the capital, and opened a yeshivah in Warsaw, where many Chassidic young men learned. “He was the rising star among the young Chassidic Rebbes, a mashpia (משפיע).” He was killed in 1943; his son, his daughter-in-law and his sister-in-law were killed in the bombing of Warsaw in 1939 or 1940, and his Chassidim begged him to leave. The lecturer recalls that Rabbi Weinberger named a shul אש קודש after the Rebbe’s sefer, that Rav Shlomo Carlebach told stories of him, and that Rav Milech Biderman, “sweeping the world by storm,” is comparable as a teacher. In his book Chovas HaTalmidim (חובת התלמידים), the lecturer says, the Rebbe writes that if a Jew says Kegavna with kavanah, with joy and with love, taking upon himself the holiness of Shabbos, the Holy One has great pleasure in heaven: “How does he know? I don’t know.”[24]

The Closing Paragraph and Barchu

In the siddurim the opening of Kegavna is in bold, and at its end there is a paragraph in small type, which even the Artscroll siddur says is for one who davens alone. It ends ולימא ברכו את ה' המברך. The question is how a person alone may say Barchu (ברכו), which requires ten. The lecturer’s answer is that he is not saying Barchu as a call to the congregation: he is saying it “as a piece of Zohar that you’re learning,” and the Zohar says to say it. Most people, he adds, do not say this paragraph, even when davening alone.[25]

VII. The Chassidim, the Misnagdim, and the Move of Bameh Madlikin

The history of the placement of Bameh Madlikin is the subject of the last part of the discourse. For most of Jewish history it was said at the end of davening, after Maariv; in the custom of Amsterdam, London, Spain and Portugal it was said after Lecha Dodi, before Mizmor Shir LeYom HaShabbos. The Ashkenazim (אשכנזים) followed the Geonim, as the sources show; but at some point they changed the time and said it only after Kaddish Yasom (קדיש יתום), at the end of Kabbalas Shabbos, before Maariv and Barchu. The Acharonim, the lecturer says, wonder why.[26] The first source for it is the Chayei Adam.

Rav Avraham Danzig (רב אברהם דנציג), the author of the Chayei Adam (חיי אדם), was born in 1748 and died, the lecturer thinks, in 1820; he lived in Danzig and then in Vilna (וילנא). He was a businessman, “just like me,” who writes in the introduction to his book that he is not like the other rabbis who use the Torah to earn a living: he worked and supported his family. He became nevertheless the leading decisor of his generation, and his book was the most popular book of law: ask your grandparents what they learned in Europe between Minchah and Maariv, either the Ein Yaakov (עין יעקב) or the Chayei Adam. He was a mechutan (מחותן) of the Vilna Gaon (וילנא גאון), the lecturer says, his son having married a granddaughter of the Gaon, “something like that”; and he was a great admirer of the Gaon, for whom, in his writing, there was nothing higher.[27]

The lecturer recalls “the painful chapter” of the Gaon and the Chassidim. Today, he says, the Chassidim and the Misnagdim (מתנגדים) are on good terms, but then it was a war: Chassidus was seen as a cult, which changed the text of the prayers, and which, instead of relying primarily on Halachah, followed Kabbalah. “The Misnagdim couldn’t stand it. Halachah is higher than Kabbalah,” and there is a rule that when the masters of Halachah and the Kabbalists differ, we rule by the Halachah. “How could you follow the Arizal over the Beis Yosef and the decisors?” And where, he asks, is the Chassidic change most evident? In that instead of saying Bameh Madlikin, as in the Shulchan Aruch, they read a piece of the Zohar at the threshold of Shabbos.

So there is, the lecturer says, “this is the theory,” a reason for the move: the Chayei Adam, a Lithuanian and a follower of the Gaon, “in order to get at the Chassidim,” who said Kegavna with fire, changed the place of Bameh Madlikin from the end of davening, where the Geonim had put it, to the very place where the Chassidim put their passage: “We Misnagdim say Bameh Madlikin; we do not say Zohar. Go and fly with your thoughts, with the holy Zohar, with ‘all the harsh judgments,’ but we go by the Halachah, by the Shulchan Aruch.” The lecturer adds, “I find this interesting,” and in doing so he reports a theory and does not state a certainty.[28]

The Map of the SiddurThe three texts thus have three histories. Anah BeKoach, at the seam between Mizmor LeDavid and Lecha Dodi, stands on the Forty-Two-Letter Name and, according to the Aruch HaShulchan, on the Shabbos of creation. Bameh Madlikin, in its early home at the end of davening, was said to keep the congregation together for the latecomer, to remind the household, and to answer the Karaites. Kegavna, in the Chassidic rite, is the Baal Shem Tov’s insertion; and the Ashkenazic Bameh Madlikin, said at the same threshold, is, on the lecturer’s theory, the answer of the Misnagdim.

VIII. Close of the Discourse

The discourse ends with a request that those present remain to say Tehillim (תהילים) for the hostages, with a prayer for the mercy of heaven in a time of uncertain news, and with the saying of Rabbi Chananya ben Akashya, רבי חנניה בן עקשיא אומר, followed by the Kaddish. “We will continue next week.”

He adds that Anah BeKoach is also said in the Counting of the Omer and, at several places, in the weekday and Shabbos davening, and that Rav Pinchas [as the recording has it] says that some Ashkenazic siddurim omit it here; he undertakes to look into whether the standard Ashkenazic siddur has it. Not located: these remarks were not checked. ↩
  7. Chagigah 11b: אין דורשין במעשה בראשית בשנים; Tosafos; Aruch HaShulchan. Verified (Sefaria, Chagigah 11b, Mishnah): “nor may one expound the act of Creation and the secrets of the beginning of the world before two or more individuals.” The lecturer reports that Tosafos (Chagigah 11b), asking what “not expounding” means when we all read the verses of Bereishis, explain that it means one may not delve into what came before, and that מעשה בראשית is itself the name of the שם מ״ב; and that the Aruch HaShulchan suggests that this is why Anah BeKoach is said when we welcome the Shabbos of creation. Not located: the Tosafos comment was not found (the segments of Tosafos on Chagigah 11b retrieved, 1:1 and 2:1, do not contain it), and the Aruch HaShulchan at simanim 261, 262 and 270 contains no mention of Anah BeKoach, the Forty-Two-Letter Name or the Account of Creation. The lecturer himself says he found no other source for the placement. ↩
  8. Standing for the whole לכה דודי: Chabad, the Syrians. The lecturer says the Lubavitchers stand for the entire Lecha Dodi; he is told that the Syrians do as well, and wonders about the Iranians and the Ashkenazim; he did not know that the standard Ashkenazic siddur mentions Anah BeKoach and undertakes to look into it. He calls the custom “very important,” showing excitement at the entry of Shabbos. Not located: as reported; no source was cited. ↩
  9. Standing for the last stanza: Aruch HaShulchan, Orach Chaim 262; Shabbos 119a. Verified (Sefaria, Aruch HaShulchan, Orach Chaim 262, final paragraph): ובחרוז האחרון עומדים וחוזרים פנינו כלפי הפתח, at the last stanza we stand and turn toward the entrance; the same paragraph cites the gemara’s בואו ונצא לקראת שבת המלכה. Verified (Sefaria, Shabbat 119a:2): Rabbi Chanina wraps himself and stands at nightfall on Shabbos eve and says, “Come and we will go out to greet Shabbat the queen”; Rabbi Yannai puts on his garment and says, “Enter, O bride. Enter, O bride.” The lecturer’s own sources were “most ספרי הלכה and the שפתי חכמים”; the Shulchan Aruch and Rema at siman 262 (Sefaria, 262:1) concern the setting of the table and do not speak of standing. ↩
  10. Bowing at בואי כלה. The lecturer says that the reason for the bowing was explained “a year and a half ago” when the Korbanos were studied, and does not repeat it here. He reports two customs, each with a source: left, right, center; or right, left, center. For the first, “to your left is the right of the Shechinah, or whatever that means.” His own custom is left first. Not located: the sources of the two customs were not named. ↩
  11. Yom Tov that is Erev Shabbos: Tur, Orach Chaim 270; Beis Yosef. The lecturer says some do not say Lecha Dodi at all on such a night, some say only the first and last stanzas, and some Ashkenazim say none, and that the Rishonim and Acharonim give as the reason that special acceptance of Shabbos would be a בזיון to Yom Tov. Verified (Sefaria, Tur OC 270, Hebrew): ונוהגין באשכנז שלא לאומרו בי״ט שחל להיות בערב שבת מפני שאין יכולין לומר עשרתם ואומר קדיש ונפטרין לבתיהם לשלום. This concerns Bameh Madlikin, not Lecha Dodi. Verified (Sefaria, Beit Yosef OC 270): the Rokeach, Shibolei HaLeket and Kol Bo are cited for not saying it on Yom Tov that falls on Friday. Not located: a source for omitting Lecha Dodi, or for the reason of בזיון יום טוב, was not found. ↩
  12. Mishnah Shabbos, chapter 2; Mishnah 2:7. Verified (Sefaria, Mishnah Shabbat 2:7): “There are three things a person must say in his home on Shabbat eve at nightfall and not before.” The lecturer gives the three as עישרתם? עירבתם? הדליקו את הנר (have you tithed, have you made an eiruv, have you lit the lamp). Hedged: the three were not read from the retrieved text, which gave only the first sentence, and the identification of עירבתם with עירוב חצירות is the lecturer’s; the explanation that the chapter’s concern is the tilting of the lamp (מטה הנר) was not checked. ↩
  13. Rav Nachshon Gaon and Rav Amram Gaon: the original custom. The lecturer says that the first sources for saying the chapter are the Geonim of the nine-hundreds, Rav Amram Gaon and Rav Nachshon Gaon, who direct that it be said after Kaddish at the end of davening, the whole congregation sitting down; and that this custom spread to Spain, Germany and France, where the Rishonim all say it at the end of davening. He mentions that the Young Israel of Lawrence-Cedarhurst still keeps the Kaddish in shul on Friday night (a participant’s report). Not located: the texts of Rav Amram and Rav Nachshon were not retrieved; the dates (“the nine hundreds”) are as given. ↩
  14. Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chaim 270:1. Verified (Sefaria, Shulchan Arukh OC 270:1, as rendered): “It is our custom to say the chapter במה מדליקין … The Sefaradim say it before Ma’ariv and this is the correct way.” The Sefaria rendering joins the two sentences and does not indicate which is the Rema’s. ↩
  15. The latecomers: Rav Nachshon Gaon. The lecturer gives the reason of the latecomers in the name of Rav Nachshon Gaon: the congregation would finish and leave, and the latecomer would be left alone, unable to carry a light on Shabbos, in a shul outside the city, exposed to מזיקים. He notes that in those days Shabbos was received much earlier, after פלג המנחה, so it was still light when the latecomer arrived. The lecturer’s remarks on the aliyah blessings said before and after (once only the first and last olim blessed) are his own illustration. Verified (Sefaria, Tur OC 270): the Tur gives as the reason for the custom only that the chapter contains the law of kindling and three things a person must say at home; it does not give the reason of the latecomers. Not located: Rav Nachshon’s own words were not found, nor was the source of the statement about the aliyah blessings. ↩
  16. The Machzor Vitry and other Rishonim: the reminder. The lecturer gives the second reason, the reminder, in the name of the מחזור ויטרי “and other Rishonim,” and says that the Abudraham, whom he calls “Yosef Kohen Abudraham,” proposed saying the chapter before Maariv so that a forgetful Jew might still correct his household. Verified (Sefaria, Beit Yosef OC 270, Hebrew): ויותר נכון לאומרו קודם כמנהג בני ספרד שע״י קריאת פרק זה יזכור הג׳ דברים שצ״ל בתוך ביתו ויזהיר עליהם and אבל אחר תפלת ערבית מה תועלת יש לו שיזכור הרי אין בידו לתקן מעתה. Not located: the Machzor Vitry and the Abudraham were not retrieved. The Abudraham’s name is David (fourteenth century, Seville); the lecturer’s “Yosef Kohen” is a slip. ↩
  17. The Exilarch’s punishment: Teshuvos HaRashba. In explaining the power of the Reish Galusa, the lecturer tells, in passing, that an Exilarch once had a woman who had sinned deprived of her nose, and says that the תשובות הרשב״א records it. He adds that “you could not do such a thing today, even a בית דין would not.” Not located: the responsum was not found; it is recorded as the lecturer’s digression, and the details are as he gave them. ↩
  18. Anan ben David and the Karaites. The lecturer’s account, given as history: in the eighth century the Reish Galusa (Exilarch) in Babylonia was Rav Chaninai Gaon, who died in 770; his son succeeded him; when the son died, Anan, the son’s nephew and Chaninai’s grandson, of the family of בוסתנאי (which the lecturer says goes back to the House of David, and which he knows from Marcus Lehmann’s novel), demanded the office; the sages, who thought him not sufficiently God-fearing, refused; he informed against them to the authorities in Baghdad and was himself jailed, but after some months was released and became Reish Galusa; he then went with the Karaites, though he knew the Mishnah and Gemara, and his followers were the עננים; he went up to Jerusalem and built a synagogue, and is buried on Har HaZeisim. The lecturer reports that a Karaite synagogue in Ashdod is named for Anan and that in the Jewish Quarter of the Old City there is a Karaite synagogue he never succeeded in entering. Not located: none of this was checked here. Editor’s note: the standard account in the reference literature is that Anan was passed over for the Exilarchate in favor of a younger brother; the lecturer’s version, that he held the office, differs and is given as his. ↩
  19. Shemos 35:3. Verified (Sefaria, Exodus 35:3): “You shall kindle no fire throughout your settlements on the sabbath day.” The lecturer adds that the Karaites sat on Friday night in darkness with cold food, and that the chamin (cholent) eaten on Shabbos morning is a demonstration that the fire burned all night. The lecturer’s statements about cholent are as he gave them. ↩
  20. The Karaite reason: the Geonim and Rishonim. The lecturer names no Rishon for the Karaite reason (“some Rishonim say”), and says that the chapter was introduced “right after Anan left” by Rav Natronai Gaon, Rav Hai, or Rav Amram in their siddurim, and that “many say it makes no difference when you say it.” Verified (Sefaria, Beit Yosef OC 270): the retrieved text does not mention the Karaites. Not located: the Rishonic source of the Karaite reason, and the statement about the Geonim, were not found. ↩
  21. Bameh Madlikin on Shabbos Chanukah. In answer to a question, the lecturer states that the Chanukah lights are the subject of this chapter itself (Shabbos 25–28) and that “on Chanukah, even though we light pure olive oil, it is inside,” and a participant says that Bameh Madlikin is not said on Shabbos Chanukah. Verified (Sefaria, Aruch HaShulchan OC 270, Hebrew): ויש שכתב דאין אומרים במה מדליקין בשבת חנוכה. Not located: the lecturer’s page references were not checked. ↩
  22. The Zohar, פרשת תרומה: כגוונא דאינון מתייחדין. The lecturer describes the passage as speaking of כל שולטני רוגזין (all the harsh judgments do not exist on Shabbos) and as praising and elevating the stature of Shabbos, and says nothing in the Zohar directs that it be said before Maariv. He also mentions the song רזא דשבת and the singing of Nissim Saal. Not located: the daf in the Zohar (Zohar II, 135a, as commonly given) was not verified; the Sefaria English text retrieved for Terumah opened with the verse of Terumah and the passage was not found. ↩
  23. The Baal Shem Tov and Kegavna. The lecturer says the custom of saying Kegavna in the Friday-night service is a Chassidic one, from the Baal Shem Tov and his disciples, “nothing before that,” and that those who daven Nusach Sefard but have nothing to do with Chassidim “got stuck” with it. He observes that we do not say Kegavna on Yom Tov that is Erev Shabbos. Not located: the claim that there is no earlier source was not checked, and the lecturer’s “Nusach Sefard” refers to the Chassidic rite and not to the Spanish-Portuguese communities. ↩
  24. The Piaseczner Rebbe, Rav Kalonymus Kalmish Shapira: אש קודש; חובת התלמידים. The lecturer gives the Rebbe’s name as רב קלונימוס קלמיש שפירא, a descendant of the נועם אלימלך, a Rebbe in Warsaw with a small rabbinate in Piaseczno, who opened a yeshivah in Warsaw; he was killed in 1943, and his son, daughter-in-law and sister-in-law were killed in the bombing of Warsaw in 1939 or 1940 (“I don’t remember exactly”). His Chassidim begged him to flee. The lecturer remarks that Rabbi Weinberger’s shul was named אש קודש and that Rav Shlomo Carlebach’s stories made the Rebbe known, and compares him to Rav Milech Biderman as a “sweeping” teacher. In חובת התלמידים, the lecturer says, the Rebbe writes that a Jew who says Kegavna with kavanah, with joy and love, taking upon himself the holiness of Shabbos, gives the Holy One “great pleasure in heaven”; “How does he know? I don’t know.” Not located: the passage in חובת התלמידים was not found; the year of death and the other details are as given; the lecturer’s memory of the date of the bombing is uncertain. ↩
  25. The closing paragraph of Kegavna and ברכו. The lecturer says the final paragraph, in small type in the siddurim (the Artscroll note: for one davening alone), ends with ולימא ברכו את ה' המברך, and explains that one who davens alone does not say ברכו as a call to the congregation, which needs ten, but as “a piece of Zohar that you’re learning”; the Artscroll rubric says the leader (בעל תפילה) says it. Not located: the lecturer’s reading of the Zohar’s closing words was not checked against the text. Editor’s note: the summary of the recording speaks of an answer “baruch hu”; the recording itself contains no such discussion. ↩
  26. The placements of Bameh Madlikin. The lecturer says that in the communities of Amsterdam, London, Spain and Portugal it was said after Lecha Dodi, before Mizmor Shir LeYom HaShabbos, and that the Ashkenazim, who had followed the Geonim, changed to saying it after Kaddish Yasom, before Barchu, “after the end of Kabbalas Shabbos.” A participant says that the Sefaradim say it before Kabbalas Shabbos. Verified (Sefaria, Shulchan Arukh OC 270:1): the Sefaradim say it before Maariv. Not located: the Amsterdam, London and Iberian customs were not checked. ↩
  27. The Chayei Adam, Rav Avraham Danzig. The lecturer says the Chayei Adam was born in 1748 and died in 1820 (“I think”), lived in Danzig and then Vilna, was a businessman who, in the introduction to his book, says that he did not use the Torah to make money; that his book was “the most popular halachic work” (Ein Yaakov and Chayei Adam were what grandparents learned between Minchah and Maariv); and that he was a מחותן of the Vilna Gaon, one of his sons marrying the Gaon’s granddaughter, “something like that.” He calls the Chayei Adam the first source for saying Bameh Madlikin between Kabbalas Shabbos and Maariv. Not located: Sefaria’s Chayei Adam 31 (the chapter on the Torah reading) does not contain the passage, and the book’s Shabbos chapters were not searched; the marriage connection was not verified. ↩
  28. The theory of a liturgical protest. The lecturer presents the motive as a theory: that the Misnagdim, who saw in Chassidus a cult that followed Kabbalah rather than Halachah, and for whom, when Halachah and Kabbalah conflict, Halachah rules, chose to move Bameh Madlikin so as to say a Mishnah in the place where the Chassidim said a passage of the Zohar. He says that “you see the Chassidic Rebbes say Kegavna with fire,” and calls the change a protest. “This is the theory”; “I find this interesting.” This is the lecturer’s reading and is not attributed to any source. ↩

Editorial Notes  ·  Open Questions

  1. Where in the Aruch HaShulchan, if anywhere, does the suggestion appear that Anah BeKoach is said because of the Account of Creation, and which Tosafos in Chagigah 11b is meant (notes 5, 7)?
  2. Which Rishonim give the Karaite reason for Bameh Madlikin, and what is the source for the Geonic custom of saying it after Kaddish at the end of davening (notes 13, 20)?
  3. Is the account of Anan as Exilarch the lecturer’s intended account, or did he mean, with the standard accounts, that Anan was passed over (note 18)? Are the details of the Karaite synagogues in Ashdod and the Old City, and of the grave on the Mount of Olives, as given (note 18)?
  4. Where does the Piaseczner Rebbe write about Kegavna in חובת התלמידים, and is this the book intended (note 24)?
  5. Where does the Chayei Adam place Bameh Madlikin, and was he a מחותן of the Vilna Gaon (note 27)?
  6. Where in the Zohar is Kegavna (Zohar II, 135a), and is there a source for the statement that nothing earlier than the Baal Shem Tov exists for its recitation (notes 22, 23)?
  7. Who was the Rishon who gives the reason of the dishonor of Yom Tov for the omission of Lecha Dodi, and is there a source for omitting Lecha Dodi on such a night (note 11)?
  8. What is the lecturer’s custom for Bameh Madlikin and Kegavna in his own shul, and does he mean by “Nusach Sefard” the Chassidic rite (note 23)?
  9. Which Rebbe of Boro Park said that one must make news (note 3), and may the news item concerning the Manos HaLevi be printed (note 2)?

The edition follows the order of the inquiry from Anah BeKoach, through the customs of Lecha Dodi and the three reasons for Bameh Madlikin, to Kegavna and the theory of the Misnagdic protest; presents the lecturer’s reports as reports; and records his uncertainties as he stated them.
