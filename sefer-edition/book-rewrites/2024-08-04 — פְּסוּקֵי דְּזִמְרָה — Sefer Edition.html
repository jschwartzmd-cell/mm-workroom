Pesukei d'Zimra: From Hodu to Baruch She'amar
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Sunday Morning Series on Tefillah
Recorded 4 August 2024 · Reworked 30 April 2026 · Mareh Mekomos upgraded 5 May 2026 · Sefer Edition · Revised 10 August 2026

Sponsored l'zecher nishmas Leah Rivka bas Yochanan HaLevi, on her 45th yahrtzeit. This shiur falls on kaf-tes Tammuz, the yahrtzeit of Rashi — the very daf on which his commentary ends (Bava Basra Daf Kaf-Tes) mirrors the date of his passing, a striking confluence noted in most editions of Shas with the words כאן מת רש״י.

I. Rashi, the Tosafists, and a Question Sent to Heaven

On Daf Kaf-Tes of Bava Basra, most printed editions of Shas note: כאן מת רש״י — "Here, Rashi died." From that point through the end of the masechta, the commentary belongs to his grandson, the Rashbam. Rashi's daughters married among the leading gedolim of his generation; from those unions came the Ba'alei HaTosafos. The Rashbam and Rabbeinu Tam were brothers — grandsons of Rashi, sons of Rabbeinu Meir his son-in-law. The entire Ashkenazic tradition of Rishonim flows from this single wellspring.

She'eilos U'Teshuvos Min HaShamayim

Among the great debate points bequeathed by this world of Rishonim stands the question of tefillin: does one follow Rashi's order of the parshiyos or the order of his grandson Rabbeinu Tam? One Rishon addressed this question by a method unique in our entire literature: Rabbeinu Yaakov of Orleans, a Ba'al HaTosafos and great mekubal, would write down profound she'eilos at night, place them under his pillow, and find written answers there in the morning. These were compiled into the sefer She'eilos u'Teshuvos Min HaShamayim.[1]

One of its most celebrated answers concerns the tefillin dispute: the reply from Heaven was that the Yeshiva Shel Ma'alah rules like Rashi while the malachim rule like Rabbeinu Tam. The precise meaning of this reply is left to the deeper peirushim. The premise of the question rests on the Gemara's teaching in Berachos[2] that HaKadosh Baruch Hu Himself "wears" tefillin, and the Gemara specifies even what is written within them. This remarkable passage — that the Holy One wears tefillin — sets a theological stage for the question of which authority's opinion is followed in the heavenly court.

This digression is not without relevance to what follows, for the tefillah of Baruch She'amar — which formally opens Pesukei d'Zimra and which this shiur arrives at in closing — also entered our liturgy "from heaven," through a piece of parchment that descended from Shamayim. The parallel is precise: in both cases, the heavenly origin is treated by the poskim as authentic and normative.

II. Hodu — The Song of the Aron in the Tent

Source: Divrei HaYamim, Not Tehillim

Unlike virtually every other element of Pesukei d'Zimra, Hodu is not drawn from Tehillim. Its source is Divrei HaYamim Aleph,[3] the final sefer of Nach. To understand its presence in our daily prayer, one must return to the historical moment it describes.

The Forty-Three Years of the Tent

When David HaMelech brought the Aron HaBris — the Shnei Luchos HaBris — to Yerushalayim, he erected a tent on Har HaMoriah. This was no permanent structure: the Mishkan Shiloh had been destroyed, and the Mishkan had passed through various locations. For roughly 43 years, the Aron rested in this temporary tent-dwelling — until Shlomo HaMelech completed the Beis HaMikdash and brought it into the Kodesh HaKodashim.

Around this tent, the full daily service was maintained: korbanos were offered — the tamid shel shachar and the tamid shel bein ha'arbayim — as though the Mishkan stood. David HaMelech established a takanah that Asaf — head of the family of Levi'im whose compositions appear throughout Tehillim under the heading Mizmor l'Asaf — together with his fellow Levi'im, would sing a shirah at each daily korban. That shirah was Hodu laHashem kir'u vishmo. The Navi itself records that Asaf stood before the congregation and led, while all the Levi'im responded together.

When we recite Hodu each morning, we are therefore re-enacting a 43-year daily service — the avodah that sustained the people of Israel through the interregnum between Mishkan and Mikdash.

Where Hodu Originally Ended

The original shirah of the Levi'im in Divrei HaYamim concludes at וְאָמְרוּ הָעָם אָמֵן וְהַלֵּל לַה' — not at the point where our siddur ends the passage. The additional pesukim that appear in our text — verses of petition and yeshuah — were appended during the Geonic era and incorporated into the standard nusach from that time onward.

How Hodu Was Recited — and Why Originally Only on Shabbos

The siddurim of the Geonim — Rav Amram Gaon, Rav Saadia Gaon — prescribe Hodu only on Shabbos and Yom Tov, not on weekdays. The reason is tircha d'tzibbur: in their practice, Hodu was recited in the manner the Levi'im had sung it — responsively, with the chazzan leading each verse and the entire congregation answering. This extended the recitation by several minutes, which was feasible on Shabbos and Yom Tov but not practical before weekday work. The modern practice of reciting Hodu daily, with the entire congregation reading together, is a later development; the call-and-response form survives only in the awareness of how it once sounded.

The Nusach Sefard / Ashkenaz Placement Debate

The historical origin of Hodu as the shirah of the daily korbanos resolves one of the more visible puzzles in the structure of the morning prayers: why does Nusach Sefard place Hodu before Baruch She'amar, while Nusach Ashkenaz places it after?

  • Nusach Sefard and the Arizal: Since Hodu was the shirah accompanying the daily korbanos — and our tefillos were established as k'neged temidin tikkenum, corresponding to the daily sacrifices — its proper place is within the korbanos section, before the formal opening of Pesukei d'Zimra with Baruch She'amar.
  • Nusach Ashkenaz and the Tur:[4] Since the brachos of Baruch She'amar and Yishtabach are designed to frame all the praises, one should include every mizmor within that frame rather than leave any outside it.

This difference carries a significant halachic consequence. From Baruch She'amar through the end of Shemoneh Esrei, interrupting with speech is prohibited. According to Nusach Ashkenaz, this prohibition begins immediately at Baruch She'amar and encompasses all the additional mizmorim that follow — including those of Shabbos. According to Nusach Sefard, the recitation of Hodu and Mizmor Shir Chanukas HaBayis precedes Baruch She'amar; they belong to the korbanos section, and one is technically permitted to speak during them — though the separate prohibition against unnecessary speech in the synagogue applies in any case.

Shema Within the Korbanos Section

The Tur explains a related practice: the recitation of the first parsha of Shema (Shema and Ve'ahavta) already during the korbanos section, before the main davening begins. In earlier centuries, when worshippers would recite many piyutim on Shabbos and Yom Tov, or when davening started late, there was a real risk of missing the z'man kriyas Shema. The early insertion served as a safeguard. Only Shema and Ve'ahavta are recited — not all three parshiyos — because reciting all three would constitute full discharge of the mitzvah, rendering the later recitation with birchos kriyas Shema superfluous or halachically problematic. The Vilna Gaon accepts this reasoning, and the Mishnah Berurah generally follows it, though there is a machloikes among the poskim.

A source preserved in the tradition of the Chasidei Ashkenaz — attributed to a grandson of Reb Yehudah HaChasid — records an additional rationale for a brief pasuk appearing at the end of Hodu: just as one must recite Shema daily, one must also be mazkir yetzias Mitzrayim every day (as the Mishnah at the beginning of Berachos requires, which is why parshas Tzitzis is recited). This brief verse within Hodu ensures that one has mentioned yetzias Mitzrayim even if the main davening begins late.[5]

III. Mizmor Shir Chanukas HaBayis — The Culmination of the Story

The Latest Addition to the Standard Siddur

Mizmor Shir Chanukas HaBayis L'David is absent from the writings of the Rishonim, the Geonim, the Rambam, and the Tur. It does not appear in any of the ancient siddurim preserved from Ashkenaz or Sefarad. The first printed siddurim to include it appear in the very late 1400s and early 1500s. The figure responsible for establishing its daily recitation was the Arizal — Rabbeinu Yitzchak Luria Ashkenazi — born in 1534 and niftar at age 38, whose brief lifespan nonetheless left an unparalleled imprint on the structure of our siddur and tefillah.

The Arizal's surname, Luria, is Ashkenazic: his father came from Ashkenaz to Mitzrayim and married a woman from Egypt. Raised by his mother's family in Mitzrayim, the Arizal absorbed Sephardic minhagim deeply — though on specific matters, including placing Hodu before Baruch She'amar, he sided with the Ashkenazic position. On Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur, he is reported to have davened specifically with Ashkenazic congregations in order not to forgo the kedushah of their piyutim. His siddur was adopted first by Sephardic communities and then by Ashkenazic ones.

The Aruch HaShulchan: Pshat Nigleh

On the level of revealed meaning, the Aruch HaShulchan[6] — Rav Yechiel Michel Epstein, the Rav of Novardok, father of Baruch Epstein (the Torah Temimah) — offers an explanation that lends the recitation of this mizmor its fullest meaning. Mizmor Shir Chanukas HaBayis is not an isolated psalm appended to the morning prayers; it is the culmination of the narrative that begins with Hodu.

The Hodu passage describes the period of those 43 years — the Aron in a temporary dwelling, the Levi'im singing daily as the tamid was offered. That period finally ended when Shlomo HaMelech completed the Beis HaMikdash and the Aron was brought into its permanent home. Mizmor Shir Chanukas HaBayis L'David — L'David, attributing the inauguration to the father — is the poem of that moment: Shlomo's song of gratitude and attribution. In a single morning's prayer, one traverses forty-three years of Jewish history, concluding with the gates of the Beis HaMikdash finally opening.

The Gates That Would Not Open

The Gemara[7] records the dramatic moment of the Beis HaMikdash's inauguration. When Shlomo HaMelech attempted to bring the Aron into the Kodesh HaKodashim, the gates refused to open. He recited twenty-four tefillos; they remained sealed. Then he proclaimed the pasuk:

שְׂאוּ שְׁעָרִים רָאשֵׁיכֶם, וְהִנָּשְׂאוּ פִּתְחֵי עוֹלָם, וְיָבוֹא מֶלֶךְ הַכָּבוֹד.
"Lift up your heads, O gates, and be lifted up, eternal openings, that the King of Glory may enter."

At this the gates uprooted themselves from their place and pursued him — for they believed he was claiming to be the Melech HaKavod himself. Shlomo cried out: he was not the Melech HaKavod; it is the Shechinah, dwelling within the Aron with the Luchos HaBris, Who is the Melech HaKavod. Even then the gates did not open, until Shlomo invoked the zechus of his father: b'zchus David avdecha — by the merit of David Your servant. At that moment the gates opened, and Shlomo composed and sang Mizmor Shir Chanukas HaBayis L'David — giving the credit, and the name, to his father.

The Sephardic Variant

Genuine Sephardic communities (Edot HaMizrach) omit the opening pasuk of this mizmor, beginning instead with Aromimcha Hashem ki dilisani. Counted from that word through the end of the chapter, the psalm contains exactly 91 words. The number 91 corresponds to the combined gematria of the Shem Havayah (26) and the Shem Adnus (65) — a union of central significance in Kabbalah.

The Ben Ish Chai: A Vision of the Future

The Ben Ish Chai[8] adds a dimension that transforms the recitation of this mizmor from commemoration into hope. Mizmor Shir Chanukas HaBayis was not only Shlomo's poem at the inauguration of the First Temple; it is destined, the Ben Ish Chai teaches, to become the national shirah of Klal Yisrael at the inauguration of the Third Temple. Each day that one recites it, one is actively planting within oneself the conviction and longing for the Beis HaMikdash HaShlishi — transforming a historical psalm into a daily prayer for redemption.

IV. Hashem Melech, Hashem Malach — The Shirah of the Malachim

Following Mizmor Shir, one rises and proclaims twice:

ה' מֶלֶךְ, ה' מָלָךְ, ה' יִמְלֹךְ לְעוֹלָם וָעֶד.

This sentence is not a single pasuk in Tanach; it is a liturgical composite of three distinct verses.[9] The source for its recitation is Maseches Sofrim,[10] one of the masechtos ketanos appended after the standard tractates, which preserves a teaching about the daily practice of the malachim: each morning, a single malach stands in the center of the rakia and proclaims this verse, and all the malachim and serafim respond in chorus. They say it twice. The congregation therefore stands — because the malachim stand (omdim) — and says it twice, joining the celestial proclamation.

As with Hodu, the early siddurim record this only on Shabbos and Yom Tov, when the call-and-response format it once took did not burden the tzibbur. The daily recitation in its current form is a later development.

V. Lamnatzeach B'Neginos — David's Battle Shield and the Menorah

The Menorah of Fire and the Forty-Nine Words

The 67th chapter of Tehillim, Lamnatzeach B'Neginos Mizmor Shir,[11] contains — excluding its opening pasuk — precisely 49 words. The significance of this number emerges from a teaching of Chazal:[12] when Moshe Rabbeinu struggled to grasp how the Menorah was to be fashioned — hammered from a single block of gold, with all its branches and ornaments — HaKadosh Baruch Hu showed him a menorah shel eish, a menorah of fire, upon which this very mizmor was engraved. The 49 words correspond to the seven branches of the Menorah multiplied by seven — or, in an alternative reckoning, to the total count of the gevi'im, kaftorim, and prachim, the ornamental features of the Menorah, which likewise total 49. The recitation of this mizmor each morning thus commemorates the hadlakas ha'menorah, the daily lighting of the Menorah in the Beis HaMikdash.

David HaMelech's Shield

A further tradition holds that David HaMelech, when going to war, carried a golden plate engraved with Lamnatzeach arranged in the form of a Menorah — the seven verses of the psalm forming the seven branches. To the merit of this engraved text his battlefield victories are attributed. The Bnei Yissaschar[13] records that the Chashmonaim carried the same engraved mizmor into their battles against the Greeks — a connection that accounts for the Chanukah significance traditionally associated with this mizmor.

Many synagogues display this mizmor on a klaf (parchment) written in the form of a Menorah. The mekubalim teach that reading from such a klaf constitutes a tikkun gadol. The Chida[14] records a practice of reciting it seven times each morning before beginning the day's work, corresponding to the seven branches of the Menorah.

VI. Baruch She'amar — A Blessing from Heaven

Baruch She'amar is the brachah that formally inaugurates Pesukei d'Zimra. Its origin is unlike that of any other tefillah in the standard liturgy. It is not found in the Bavli or the Yerushalmi; it is not attributed to any Tanna or Amora; the Zohar HaKadosh mentions the words Baruch She'amar but does not present the text as a fixed formulation.

The Note from Heaven

The Or Zarua[15] records the tradition: a pesek — a slip of parchment — fell from the heavens (Shamayim), and upon it was written the text of Baruch She'amar. The Tur brings this as halacha l'ma'aseh; the Taz reaffirms it. There is no other tefillah in the standard siddur whose textual origin is described in this manner.

As ma'aminim bnei ma'aminim — believers, children of believers — this tradition is accepted as emes l'amito, absolute truth, precisely because it is transmitted through the Tur and affirmed by the major Acharonim. Its heavenly authorship is perhaps the very reason this brachah was deemed fitting to serve as the formal gate to Pesukei d'Zimra — the threshold through which the daily recitation of praises begins.

The category of "a note descended from heaven" is not unprecedented in our tradition, but it demands careful scrutiny. The parallel with the founding narrative of the Na Nach movement — in which Rabbi Yisroel Dov Odesser claimed a note fell to him from Reb Nachman of Breslov — illustrates precisely the difference between a tradition received and transmitted through the channels of halacha (cited in the Tur, affirmed by the Taz, embedded in our normative practice) and a recent, unwitnessed, and contested personal claim. The latter, whatever its spiritual merit, does not carry the weight of the former.

The deeper meaning of Baruch She'amar — its structure, its fourteen expressions of praise, and its function as the gate of Pesukei d'Zimra — awaits discussion in the shiur that follows.

The tradition of Baruch She'amar's heavenly origin stands on the authority of the Tur and Taz regardless of which Or Zarua is meant.
