פְּסוּקֵי דְּזִמְרָה — מֵהוֹדוּ עַד בָּרוּךְ שֶׁאָמַר

Pesukei d'Zimra: From Hodu to Baruch She'amar
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Sunday Morning Series on Tefillah (תְּפִלָּה)
Recorded 4 August 2024 · Reworked 30 April 2026 · Mareh Mekomos upgraded 5 May 2026 · Sefer Edition · Revised 10 August 2026

Sponsored l'zecher nishmas (לְזֵכֶר נִשְׁמַת) Leah Rivka bas Yochanan HaLevi (לֵאָה רִבְקָה בַּת יוֹחָנָן הַלֵּוִי), on her 45th yahrtzeit (יאָרצייט). This shiur falls on kaf-tes Tammuz (כ״ט תַּמּוּז), the yahrtzeit of Rashi (רש״י) — the very daf (דַּף) on which his commentary ends (Bava Basra Daf Kaf-Tes — בָּבָא בָּתְרָא דַּף כ״ט) mirrors the date of his passing, a striking confluence noted in most editions of Shas (ש״ס) with the words כאן מת רש״י.

I. Rashi, the Tosafists, and a Question Sent to Heaven

On Daf Kaf-Tes of Bava Basra, most printed editions of Shas note: כאן מת רש״י — "Here, Rashi died." From that point through the end of the masechta (מַסֶּכְתָּא), the commentary belongs to his grandson, the Rashbam (רשב״ם). Rashi's daughters married among the leading gedolim (גְּדוֹלִים) of his generation; from those unions came the Ba'alei HaTosafos (בַּעֲלֵי הַתּוֹסָפוֹת). The Rashbam and Rabbeinu Tam (רַבֵּינוּ תָּם) were brothers — grandsons of Rashi, sons of Rabbeinu Meir (רַבֵּינוּ מֵאִיר) his son-in-law. The entire Ashkenazic tradition of Rishonim (רִאשׁוֹנִים) flows from this single wellspring.

She'eilos U'Teshuvos Min HaShamayim

Among the great debate points bequeathed by this world of Rishonim stands the question of tefillin (תְּפִלִּין): does one follow Rashi's order of the parshiyos (פָּרָשִׁיּוֹת) or the order of his grandson Rabbeinu Tam? One Rishon addressed this question by a method unique in our entire literature: Rabbeinu Yaakov of Orleans (רַבֵּינוּ יַעֲקֹב מֵאוֹרְלֵיאָנְז), a Ba'al HaTosafos and great mekubal (מְקוּבָּל), would write down profound she'eilos (שְׁאֵלוֹת) at night, place them under his pillow, and find written answers there in the morning. These were compiled into the sefer (סֵפֶר) She'eilos u'Teshuvos Min HaShamayim (שְׁאֵלוֹת וּתְשׁוּבוֹת מִן הַשָּׁמַיִם).[1]

One of its most celebrated answers concerns the tefillin dispute: the reply from Heaven was that the Yeshiva Shel Ma'alah (יְשִׁיבָה שֶׁל מַעְלָה) rules like Rashi while the malachim (מַלְאָכִים) rule like Rabbeinu Tam. The precise meaning of this reply is left to the deeper peirushim (פֵּירוּשִׁים). The premise of the question rests on the Gemara (גְּמָרָא)'s teaching in Berachos (בְּרָכוֹת)[2] that HaKadosh Baruch Hu (הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא) Himself "wears" tefillin, and the Gemara specifies even what is written within them. This remarkable passage — that the Holy One wears tefillin — sets a theological stage for the question of which authority's opinion is followed in the heavenly court.

This digression is not without relevance to what follows, for the tefillah (תְּפִלָּה) of Baruch She'amar (בָּרוּךְ שֶׁאָמַר) — which formally opens Pesukei d'Zimra (פְּסוּקֵי דְּזִמְרָה) and which this shiur arrives at in closing — also entered our liturgy "from heaven," through a piece of parchment that descended from Shamayim (שָׁמַיִם). The parallel is precise: in both cases, the heavenly origin is treated by the poskim (פּוֹסְקִים) as authentic and normative.

II. Hodu — The Song of the Aron in the Tent

Source: Divrei HaYamim, Not Tehillim

Unlike virtually every other element of Pesukei d'Zimra, Hodu (הוֹדוּ) is not drawn from Tehillim (תְּהִלִּים). Its source is Divrei HaYamim Aleph (דִּבְרֵי הַיָּמִים א׳),[3] the final sefer of Nach (נַ״ךְ). To understand its presence in our daily prayer, one must return to the historical moment it describes.

The Forty-Three Years of the Tent

When David HaMelech (דָּוִד הַמֶּלֶךְ) brought the Aron HaBris (אֲרוֹן הַבְּרִית) — the Shnei Luchos HaBris (שְׁנֵי לוּחוֹת הַבְּרִית) — to Yerushalayim (יְרוּשָׁלַיִם), he erected a tent on Har HaMoriah (הַר הַמּוֹרִיָּה). This was no permanent structure: the Mishkan Shiloh (מִשְׁכַּן שִׁילֹה) had been destroyed, and the Mishkan (מִשְׁכָּן) had passed through various locations. For roughly 43 years, the Aron (אָרוֹן) rested in this temporary tent-dwelling — until Shlomo HaMelech (שְׁלֹמֹה הַמֶּלֶךְ) completed the Beis HaMikdash (בֵּית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ) and brought it into the Kodesh HaKodashim (קֹדֶשׁ הַקֳּדָשִׁים).

Around this tent, the full daily service was maintained: korbanos (קָרְבָּנוֹת) were offered — the tamid shel shachar (תָּמִיד שֶׁל שַׁחַר) and the tamid shel bein ha'arbayim (תָּמִיד שֶׁל בֵּין הָעַרְבַּיִם) — as though the Mishkan stood. David HaMelech established a takanah (תַּקָּנָה) that Asaf (אָסָף) — head of the family of Levi'im (לְוִיִּם) whose compositions appear throughout Tehillim under the heading Mizmor l'Asaf (מִזְמוֹר לְאָסָף) — together with his fellow Levi'im, would sing a shirah (שִׁירָה) at each daily korban (קָרְבָּן). That shirah was Hodu laHashem kir'u vishmo (הוֹדוּ לַה׳ קִרְאוּ בִשְׁמוֹ). The Navi (נָבִיא) itself records that Asaf stood before the congregation and led, while all the Levi'im responded together.

When we recite Hodu each morning, we are therefore re-enacting a 43-year daily service — the avodah (עֲבוֹדָה) that sustained the people of Israel through the interregnum between Mishkan and Mikdash (מִקְדָּשׁ).

Where Hodu Originally Ended

The original shirah of the Levi'im in Divrei HaYamim concludes at וְאָמְרוּ הָעָם אָמֵן וְהַלֵּל לַה' — not at the point where our siddur (סִדּוּר) ends the passage. The additional pesukim (פְּסוּקִים) that appear in our text — verses of petition and yeshuah (יְשׁוּעָה) — were appended during the Geonic era and incorporated into the standard nusach (נֻסָּח) from that time onward.

How Hodu Was Recited — and Why Originally Only on Shabbos

The siddurim of the Geonim (גְּאוֹנִים) — Rav Amram Gaon (רַב עַמְרָם גָּאוֹן), Rav Saadia Gaon (רַב סְעַדְיָה גָּאוֹן) — prescribe Hodu only on Shabbos (שַׁבָּת) and Yom Tov (יוֹם טוֹב), not on weekdays. The reason is tircha d'tzibbur (טִרְחָא דְּצִבּוּרָא): in their practice, Hodu was recited in the manner the Levi'im had sung it — responsively, with the chazzan (חַזָּן) leading each verse and the entire congregation answering. This extended the recitation by several minutes, which was feasible on Shabbos and Yom Tov but not practical before weekday work. The modern practice of reciting Hodu daily, with the entire congregation reading together, is a later development; the call-and-response form survives only in the awareness of how it once sounded.

The Nusach Sefard / Ashkenaz Placement Debate

The historical origin of Hodu as the shirah of the daily korbanos resolves one of the more visible puzzles in the structure of the morning prayers: why does Nusach Sefard (נֻסָּח סְפָרַד) place Hodu before Baruch She'amar, while Nusach Ashkenaz (נֻסָּח אַשְׁכְּנַז) places it after?

  • Nusach Sefard and the Arizal (אֲרִיזַ״ל): Since Hodu was the shirah accompanying the daily korbanos — and our tefillos (תְּפִלּוֹת) were established as k'neged temidin tikkenum (כְּנֶגֶד תְּמִידִין תִּקְּנוּם), corresponding to the daily sacrifices — its proper place is within the korbanos section, before the formal opening of Pesukei d'Zimra with Baruch She'amar.
  • Nusach Ashkenaz and the Tur (טוּר):[4] Since the brachos (בְּרָכוֹת) of Baruch She'amar and Yishtabach (יִשְׁתַּבַּח) are designed to frame all the praises, one should include every mizmor (מִזְמוֹר) within that frame rather than leave any outside it.

This difference carries a significant halachic (הֲלָכָתִי) consequence. From Baruch She'amar through the end of Shemoneh Esrei (שְׁמוֹנֶה עֶשְׂרֵה), interrupting with speech is prohibited. According to Nusach Ashkenaz, this prohibition begins immediately at Baruch She'amar and encompasses all the additional mizmorim that follow — including those of Shabbos. According to Nusach Sefard, the recitation of Hodu and Mizmor Shir Chanukas HaBayis (מִזְמוֹר שִׁיר חֲנֻכַּת הַבַּיִת) precedes Baruch She'amar; they belong to the korbanos section, and one is technically permitted to speak during them — though the separate prohibition against unnecessary speech in the synagogue applies in any case.

Shema Within the Korbanos Section

The Tur explains a related practice: the recitation of the first parsha (פָּרָשָׁה) of Shema (שְׁמַע) (Shema and Ve'ahavta — וְאָהַבְתָּ) already during the korbanos section, before the main davening (דַּאװנֶען) begins. In earlier centuries, when worshippers would recite many piyutim (פִּיּוּטִים) on Shabbos and Yom Tov, or when davening started late, there was a real risk of missing the z'man kriyas Shema (זְמַן קְרִיאַת שְׁמַע). The early insertion served as a safeguard. Only Shema and Ve'ahavta are recited — not all three parshiyos — because reciting all three would constitute full discharge of the mitzvah (מִצְוָה), rendering the later recitation with birchos kriyas Shema (בִּרְכוֹת קְרִיאַת שְׁמַע) superfluous or halachically problematic. The Vilna Gaon (הַגְּרָ״א) accepts this reasoning, and the Mishnah Berurah (מִשְׁנָה בְּרוּרָה) generally follows it, though there is a machloikes (מַחְלוֹקֶת) among the poskim.

A source preserved in the tradition of the Chasidei Ashkenaz (חֲסִידֵי אַשְׁכְּנַז) — attributed to a grandson of Reb Yehudah HaChasid (רַבִּי יְהוּדָה הֶחָסִיד) — records an additional rationale for a brief pasuk (פָּסוּק) appearing at the end of Hodu: just as one must recite Shema daily, one must also be mazkir yetzias Mitzrayim (מַזְכִּיר יְצִיאַת מִצְרַיִם) every day (as the Mishnah (מִשְׁנָה) at the beginning of Berachos requires, which is why parshas Tzitzis (פָּרָשַׁת צִיצִית) is recited). This brief verse within Hodu ensures that one has mentioned yetzias Mitzrayim even if the main davening begins late.[5]

III. Mizmor Shir Chanukas HaBayis — The Culmination of the Story

The Latest Addition to the Standard Siddur

Mizmor Shir Chanukas HaBayis L'David (מִזְמוֹר שִׁיר חֲנֻכַּת הַבַּיִת לְדָוִד) is absent from the writings of the Rishonim (רִאשׁוֹנִים), the Geonim (גְּאוֹנִים), the Rambam (רַמְבַּ״ם), and the Tur (טוּר). It does not appear in any of the ancient siddurim (סִדּוּרִים) preserved from Ashkenaz or Sefarad. The first printed siddurim to include it appear in the very late 1400s and early 1500s. The figure responsible for establishing its daily recitation was the Arizal (אֲרִיזַ״ל) — Rabbeinu Yitzchak Luria Ashkenazi (רַבֵּינוּ יִצְחָק לוּרְיָא אַשְׁכְּנַזִי) — born in 1534 and niftar (נִפְטַר) at age 38, whose brief lifespan nonetheless left an unparalleled imprint on the structure of our siddur and tefillah (תְּפִלָּה).

The Arizal's surname, Luria (לוּרְיָא), is Ashkenazic: his father came from Ashkenaz to Mitzrayim (מִצְרַיִם) and married a woman from Egypt. Raised by his mother's family in Mitzrayim, the Arizal absorbed Sephardic minhagim (מִנְהָגִים) deeply — though on specific matters, including placing Hodu (הוֹדוּ) before Baruch She'amar (בָּרוּךְ שֶׁאָמַר), he sided with the Ashkenazic position. On Rosh Hashanah (רֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה) and Yom Kippur (יוֹם כִּפּוּר), he is reported to have davened specifically with Ashkenazic congregations in order not to forgo the kedushah (קְדֻשָּׁה) of their piyutim (פִּיּוּטִים). His siddur (סִדּוּר) was adopted first by Sephardic communities and then by Ashkenazic ones.

The Aruch HaShulchan: Pshat Nigleh

On the level of revealed meaning, the Aruch HaShulchan (עָרוּךְ הַשֻּׁלְחָן)[6] — Rav Yechiel Michel Epstein (רַב יְחִיאֵל מִיכֵל עֶפְּשְׁטֵיין), the Rav of Novardok (נָאװַרְדָק), father of Baruch Epstein (the Torah Temimah — תּוֹרָה תְּמִימָה) — offers an explanation that lends the recitation of this mizmor (מִזְמוֹר) its fullest meaning. Mizmor Shir Chanukas HaBayis is not an isolated psalm appended to the morning prayers; it is the culmination of the narrative that begins with Hodu.

The Hodu passage describes the period of those 43 years — the Aron (אָרוֹן) in a temporary dwelling, the Levi'im (לְוִיִּם) singing daily as the tamid (תָּמִיד) was offered. That period finally ended when Shlomo HaMelech (שְׁלֹמֹה הַמֶּלֶךְ) completed the Beis HaMikdash (בֵּית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ) and the Aron was brought into its permanent home. Mizmor Shir Chanukas HaBayis L'David — L'David (לְדָוִד), attributing the inauguration to the father — is the poem of that moment: Shlomo's song of gratitude and attribution. In a single morning's prayer, one traverses forty-three years of Jewish history, concluding with the gates of the Beis HaMikdash finally opening.

The Gates That Would Not Open

The Gemara (גְּמָרָא)[7] records the dramatic moment of the Beis HaMikdash's inauguration. When Shlomo HaMelech attempted to bring the Aron into the Kodesh HaKodashim (קֹדֶשׁ הַקֳּדָשִׁים), the gates refused to open. He recited twenty-four tefillos (תְּפִלּוֹת); they remained sealed. Then he proclaimed the pasuk (פָּסוּק):

שְׂאוּ שְׁעָרִים רָאשֵׁיכֶם, וְהִנָּשְׂאוּ פִּתְחֵי עוֹלָם, וְיָבוֹא מֶלֶךְ הַכָּבוֹד.
"Lift up your heads, O gates, and be lifted up, eternal openings, that the King of Glory may enter."

At this the gates uprooted themselves from their place and pursued him — for they believed he was claiming to be the Melech HaKavod (מֶלֶךְ הַכָּבוֹד) himself. Shlomo cried out: he was not the Melech HaKavod; it is the Shechinah (שְׁכִינָה), dwelling within the Aron with the Luchos HaBris (לוּחוֹת הַבְּרִית), Who is the Melech HaKavod. Even then the gates did not open, until Shlomo invoked the zechus (זְכוּת) of his father: b'zchus David avdecha (בִּזְכוּת דָּוִד עַבְדֶּךָ) — by the merit of David Your servant. At that moment the gates opened, and Shlomo composed and sang Mizmor Shir Chanukas HaBayis L'David — giving the credit, and the name, to his father.

The Sephardic Variant

Genuine Sephardic communities (Edot HaMizrach — עֵדוֹת הַמִּזְרָח) omit the opening pasuk of this mizmor, beginning instead with Aromimcha Hashem ki dilisani (אֲרוֹמִמְךָ ה׳ כִּי דִלִּיתָנִי). Counted from that word through the end of the chapter, the psalm contains exactly 91 words. The number 91 corresponds to the combined gematria (גִּימַטְרִיָּא) of the Shem Havayah (שֵׁם הֲוָיָה) (26) and the Shem Adnus (שֵׁם אֲדֹנוּת) (65) — a union of central significance in Kabbalah (קַבָּלָה).

The Ben Ish Chai: A Vision of the Future

The Ben Ish Chai (בֶּן אִישׁ חַי)[8] adds a dimension that transforms the recitation of this mizmor from commemoration into hope. Mizmor Shir Chanukas HaBayis was not only Shlomo's poem at the inauguration of the First Temple; it is destined, the Ben Ish Chai teaches, to become the national shirah (שִׁירָה) of Klal Yisrael (כְּלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל) at the inauguration of the Third Temple. Each day that one recites it, one is actively planting within oneself the conviction and longing for the Beis HaMikdash HaShlishi (בֵּית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ הַשְּׁלִישִׁי) — transforming a historical psalm into a daily prayer for redemption.

IV. Hashem Melech, Hashem Malach — The Shirah of the Malachim

Following Mizmor Shir (מִזְמוֹר שִׁיר), one rises and proclaims twice:

ה' מֶלֶךְ, ה' מָלָךְ, ה' יִמְלֹךְ לְעוֹלָם וָעֶד.

This sentence is not a single pasuk (פָּסוּק) in Tanach (תַּנַ״ךְ); it is a liturgical composite of three distinct verses.[9] The source for its recitation is Maseches Sofrim (מַסֶּכֶת סוֹפְרִים),[10] one of the masechtos ketanos (מַסֶּכְתּוֹת קְטַנּוֹת) appended after the standard tractates, which preserves a teaching about the daily practice of the malachim (מַלְאָכִים): each morning, a single malach (מַלְאָךְ) stands in the center of the rakia (רָקִיעַ) and proclaims this verse, and all the malachim and serafim (שְׂרָפִים) respond in chorus. They say it twice. The congregation therefore stands — because the malachim stand (omdim — עוֹמְדִים) — and says it twice, joining the celestial proclamation.

As with Hodu (הוֹדוּ), the early siddurim (סִדּוּרִים) record this only on Shabbos (שַׁבָּת) and Yom Tov (יוֹם טוֹב), when the call-and-response format it once took did not burden the tzibbur (צִבּוּר). The daily recitation in its current form is a later development.

V. Lamnatzeach B'Neginos — David's Battle Shield and the Menorah

The Menorah of Fire and the Forty-Nine Words

The 67th chapter of Tehillim (תְּהִלִּים), Lamnatzeach B'Neginos Mizmor Shir (לַמְנַצֵּחַ בִּנְגִינֹת מִזְמוֹר שִׁיר),[11] contains — excluding its opening pasuk (פָּסוּק) — precisely 49 words. The significance of this number emerges from a teaching of Chazal (חֲזַ״ל):[12] when Moshe Rabbeinu (מֹשֶׁה רַבֵּינוּ) struggled to grasp how the Menorah (מְנוֹרָה) was to be fashioned — hammered from a single block of gold, with all its branches and ornaments — HaKadosh Baruch Hu (הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא) showed him a menorah shel eish (מְנוֹרָה שֶׁל אֵשׁ), a menorah of fire, upon which this very mizmor (מִזְמוֹר) was engraved. The 49 words correspond to the seven branches of the Menorah multiplied by seven — or, in an alternative reckoning, to the total count of the gevi'im (גְּבִיעִים), kaftorim (כַּפְתּוֹרִים), and prachim (פְּרָחִים), the ornamental features of the Menorah, which likewise total 49. The recitation of this mizmor each morning thus commemorates the hadlakas ha'menorah (הַדְלָקַת הַמְּנוֹרָה), the daily lighting of the Menorah in the Beis HaMikdash (בֵּית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ).

David HaMelech's Shield

A further tradition holds that David HaMelech (דָּוִד הַמֶּלֶךְ), when going to war, carried a golden plate engraved with Lamnatzeach (לַמְנַצֵּחַ) arranged in the form of a Menorah — the seven verses of the psalm forming the seven branches. To the merit of this engraved text his battlefield victories are attributed. The Bnei Yissaschar (בְּנֵי יִשָּׂשכָר)[13] records that the Chashmonaim (חַשְׁמוֹנָאִים) carried the same engraved mizmor into their battles against the Greeks — a connection that accounts for the Chanukah (חֲנֻכָּה) significance traditionally associated with this mizmor.

Many synagogues display this mizmor on a klaf (קְלָף) (parchment) written in the form of a Menorah. The mekubalim (מְקוּבָּלִים) teach that reading from such a klaf constitutes a tikkun gadol (תִּקּוּן גָּדוֹל). The Chida (חִיד״א)[14] records a practice of reciting it seven times each morning before beginning the day's work, corresponding to the seven branches of the Menorah.

VI. Baruch She'amar — A Blessing from Heaven

Baruch She'amar (בָּרוּךְ שֶׁאָמַר) is the brachah (בְּרָכָה) that formally inaugurates Pesukei d'Zimra (פְּסוּקֵי דְּזִמְרָה). Its origin is unlike that of any other tefillah (תְּפִלָּה) in the standard liturgy. It is not found in the Bavli (בַּבְלִי) or the Yerushalmi (יְרוּשַׁלְמִי); it is not attributed to any Tanna (תַּנָּא) or Amora (אָמוֹרָא); the Zohar HaKadosh (זֹהַר הַקָּדוֹשׁ) mentions the words Baruch She'amar but does not present the text as a fixed formulation.

The Note from Heaven

The Or Zarua (אוֹר זָרוּעַ)[15] records the tradition: a pesek (פֶּסֶק) — a slip of parchment — fell from the heavens (Shamayim — שָׁמַיִם), and upon it was written the text of Baruch She'amar. The Tur (טוּר) brings this as halacha l'ma'aseh (הֲלָכָה לְמַעֲשֶׂה); the Taz (ט״ז) reaffirms it. There is no other tefillah in the standard siddur whose textual origin is described in this manner.

As ma'aminim bnei ma'aminim (מַאֲמִינִים בְּנֵי מַאֲמִינִים) — believers, children of believers — this tradition is accepted as emes l'amito (אֱמֶת לַאֲמִיתּוֹ), absolute truth, precisely because it is transmitted through the Tur and affirmed by the major Acharonim (אַחֲרוֹנִים). Its heavenly authorship is perhaps the very reason this brachah was deemed fitting to serve as the formal gate to Pesukei d'Zimra — the threshold through which the daily recitation of praises begins.

The category of "a note descended from heaven" is not unprecedented in our tradition, but it demands careful scrutiny. The parallel with the founding narrative of the Na Nach (נַ נַחְ) movement — in which Rabbi Yisroel Dov Odesser (רַבִּי יִשְׂרָאֵל דּוֹב אוֹדֶסֶר) claimed a note fell to him from Reb Nachman of Breslov (רַבִּי נַחְמָן מִבְּרֶסְלֶב) — illustrates precisely the difference between a tradition received and transmitted through the channels of halacha (הֲלָכָה) (cited in the Tur, affirmed by the Taz, embedded in our normative practice) and a recent, unwitnessed, and contested personal claim. The latter, whatever its spiritual merit, does not carry the weight of the former.

The deeper meaning of Baruch She'amar — its structure, its fourteen expressions of praise, and its function as the gate of Pesukei d'Zimra — awaits discussion in the shiur that follows.
