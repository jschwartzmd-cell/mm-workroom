כֵּיצַד נַעֲשׂוּ הַחַשְׁמוֹנָאִים לִמְלָכִים?

How Did the Chashmonaim Become Kings?
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Chanukah / Erev Shabbos Mikeitz 5785
Recorded 25 December 2024  ·  Sefer Edition · Revised 23 August 2026

A Chanukah shiur built around a single deep kushya (קֻשְׁיָא): how did a family of Kohanim (כֹּהֲנִים) ascend a throne that Yaakov Avinu (יַעֲקֹב אָבִינוּ)'s blessing had reserved for the descendants of Yehudah (יְהוּדָה)? The path in is long: the historical arc from Alexander of Macedon through the Misyavnim (מִתְיַוְּנִים), the Seleucid reversal under Antiochus, the corruption of the Kehunah Gedolah (כְּהֻנָּה גְּדוֹלָה), the Jason-Menelaus civil war, and the rebellion of Mattisyahu (מַתִּתְיָהוּ) and his sons. The halachic core rests on a foundational machlokes (מַחֲלוֹקֶת): the Ramban (רַמְבַּ״ן) in Parshas Vayechi (פָּרָשַׁת וַיְחִי), for whom the seizure of the throne was a cheit (חֵטְא) punished by Hordus (הוֹרְדוֹס) wiping out the line; and Rav Yaakov Kamenetsky (רַב יַעֲקֹב קַמֶנֶצְקִי) in Emes L'Yaakov (אֱמֶת לְיַעֲקֹב), whose structural reading — Bayis Sheni (בַּיִת שֵׁנִי) as an interim tekufah (תְּקוּפָה) that was never meant to be the geulah ha'sheleimah (גְּאֻלָּה הַשְּׁלֵמָה) — recasts the malchus (מַלְכוּת) as a defensible hora'as sha'ah (הוֹרָאַת שָׁעָה). The tzivui (צִוּוּי)-vs.-havtachah (הַבְטָחָה) axis that emerges from this dispute recurs in the classical Rambam (רַמְבַּ״ם)-Ramban disagreement on yishuv Eretz Yisrael (יִשּׁוּב אֶרֶץ יִשְׂרָאֵל), and the piece closes with the parallel question of why the yom tov (יוֹם טוֹב) that the Chashmonaim (חַשְׁמוֹנָאִים) gave us has no maseches (מַסֶּכֶת).

I. Framing the Question

The Rambam (רַמְבַּ״ם) ends his entire Yad HaChazakah (יַד הַחֲזָקָה) with Hilchos Melachim (הִלְכוֹת מְלָכִים).[1] The structural choice is instructive: malchus (מַלְכוּת) is not a rhetorical figure for “leadership,” but a full parshah (פָּרָשָׁה) of halachos (הֲלָכוֹת) — with its own dinim (דִּינִים), its own gedarim (גְּדָרִים), and its own prerequisites for the establishment of a king. Throughout Tanach (תַּנַ״ךְ), two malchu’os (מַלְכֻיּוֹת) alternated in Klal Yisrael (כְּלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל):

  • Malchei Yehudah (מַלְכֵי יְהוּדָה) — descended from David HaMelech (דָּוִד הַמֶּלֶךְ).
  • Malchei Yisrael (מַלְכֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל) — the breakaway northern kingdom.

Those who learn Maseches Sanhedrin (מַסֶּכֶת סַנְהֶדְרִין) are familiar with the numerous chilukim (חִלּוּקִים) that follow between the two. But the Chashmonaim (חַשְׁמוֹנָאִים) stand in neither column. They are Kohanim (כֹּהֲנִים) — Shevet Levi (שֵׁבֶט לֵוִי) — and the Rambam at the head of Hilchos Chanukah (הִלְכוֹת חֲנֻכָּה) writes that they seized the throne and ruled kol Yisrael (כָּל יִשְׂרָאֵל) for more than two centuries. How is that b'derech halachah (בְּדֶרֶךְ הֲלָכָה) possible? Yaakov Avinu (יַעֲקֹב אָבִינוּ) in Parshas Vayechi (פָּרָשַׁת וַיְחִי)[2] declared:

לֹא יָסוּר שֵׁבֶט מִיהוּדָה וּמְחֹקֵק מִבֵּין רַגְלָיו, עַד כִּי יָבֹא שִׁילֹה.
“The scepter shall not depart from Yehudah, nor the staff from between his feet, until Shiloh arrives.”
Bereishis 49:10

The malchus is not to depart from Shevet Yehudah (שֵׁבֶט יְהוּדָה); the mechokek (מְחוֹקֵק) — the lawgiver — must come from Yehudah (יְהוּדָה). How, then, do Kohanim become melachim (מְלָכִים)?

II. Historical Background — The Rise of Alexander of Macedon

The Beginning of Bayis Sheni

Roughly a generation after the Beis HaMikdash (בֵּית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ) was rebuilt under Persian permission, an individual rose to power who would change the shape of the ancient world: Alexander of Macedon, son of Philip II. He ascended his father's throne in the year 336 BCE at the age of twenty, and by 332 BCE his armies had swept through Eretz Yisrael (אֶרֶץ יִשְׂרָאֵל). The name entered the Jewish lexicon as a permanent Yiddishe nomen.[3]

Alexander's Education Under Aristotle

Alexander was a pampered child of the Macedonian ruler. His father hired the finest teachers of the age; chief among them was Aristotle. The two studied together for some eight years, beginning when Alexander was a boy of thirteen. The pairing of the great thinker with the great doer set a trajectory in divrei ha’yamim (דִּבְרֵי הַיָּמִים) — and especially in Jewish history — from which Klal Yisrael (כְּלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל) would suffer greatly, until ultimately prevailing in the yom tov (יוֹם טוֹב) of Chanukah (חֲנֻכָּה). Aristotle introduced his young pupil to Greek philosophy, the arts, and the whole architecture of Hellenistic ideology. They had their debates and their disagreements; but the imprint upon Alexander was profound and lasting.

Alexander's Conquests

Following his father's assassination — historians have long debated whether Alexander himself had a hand in it — the young king rapidly subdued South Asia, North Africa, the Mediterranean basin, the Middle East, and Eretz Yisrael, earning the title Alexander the Great (אֲלֶכְּסַנְדֶּר מוֹקְדוֹן): Alexander the Conqueror.

The Encounter with Shimon HaTzaddik

The Gemara (גְּמָרָא) in Yoma (יוֹמָא)[4] records the famous account: when Alexander reached the border of Eretz Yisrael, Shimon HaTzaddik (שִׁמְעוֹן הַצַּדִּיק), then Kohen Gadol (כֹּהֵן גָּדוֹל) and leader of Klal Yisrael, went out to meet him. Alexander descended from his chariot and bowed. To his astonished soldiers and courtiers — who could not comprehend why the conqueror of nations would prostrate himself before a Jewish sage — he explained: whenever he went out to battle, he would see the demus (דְּמוּת) of this very Shimon HaTzaddik before him, blessing him with victory.

The Ten Questions in Maseches Tamid

Alexander was not only a conqueror but a thinker. Talmud Bavli (תַּלְמוּד בַּבְלִי), Maseches Tamid (מַסֶּכֶת תָּמִיד),[5] preserves an entire daf (דַּף) of ten philosophical questions he put to the Chachmei Yisrael (חַכְמֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל) — fierce debates on philosophy and hashkafah (הַשְׁקָפָה). The chochmah (חָכְמָה) of Macedon meets the chochmah of Chazal (חֲזַ״ל) across a full page of the Talmud (תַּלְמוּד).

III. The Hellenistic Era and the Rise of the Misyavnim

What is “Hellenism”?

Hellas was the ancient name for Greece herself; Hellenists are those influenced by the Greeks. The Hellenistic era — some three centuries within the tekufah (תְּקוּפָה) of Bayis Sheni (בַּיִת שֵׁנִי) — saw a major part of the civilized world fall under Greek empire and, more dangerously, Greek influence: in music, in architecture, in science and mathematics, in philosophy, in ideology. Architecture in particular was their special province, as the surviving monuments still attest.

Was It Good for the Jews?

Despite the warm encounter with Shimon HaTzaddik (שִׁמְעוֹן הַצַּדִּיק), the answer is nisht azoy gut. Alexander's declared policy was religious autonomy: every conquered nation retained its religion, paid its taxes, submitted to Greek political rule, and was introduced to Greek culture. (Aristotle himself dissented from this shitah (שִׁיטָה), arguing for a more thoroughgoing Hellenization.) On the surface, Alexander was good for the Jews; Klal Yisrael (כְּלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל) even began naming children after him. But the cultural infiltration was relentless.

Gymnasia and the Rise of the Misyavnim

Many Jews were drawn — first by pressure, then willingly — into the gymnasia, the schools of Greek philosophy and physical culture. There they began to raise their children with Greek priorities and Greek hashkafos (הַשְׁקָפוֹת). The era of the Misyavnim (מִתְיַוְּנִים) — the Hellenized Jews — had begun.

Prushim and Tzedukim

The rabbinic Jews — the Prushim (פְּרוּשִׁים) — fought to preserve the Torah (תּוֹרָה), and especially Torah she’b’al peh (תּוֹרָה שֶׁבְּעַל פֶּה), which at this time was not yet written down. Tanach (תַּנַ״ךְ) alone was on the page; the rest was carried by the yeshivos (יְשִׁיבוֹת) and the rebbeim (רַבֵּיִים) orally. Maintaining the mesorah (מְסוֹרָה) was therefore a matter of survival.

Their opponents — the Tzedukim (צְדוּקִים) — trace to Tzaddok (צָדוֹק) and Baisus (בַּיְתּוֹס), talmidim (תַּלְמִידִים) of Antignos Ish Socho (אַנְטִיגְנוֹס אִישׁ סוֹכוֹ),[6] whose followers misread their master's teaching on avdus mei’ahavah (עַבְדוּת מֵאַהֲבָה) and broke from the mesorah. Alongside them stood the Beisusim (בַּיְתּוֹסִים). The Tzedukim rejected Torah she’b’al peh; the Prushim defended it.

Roughly a Century and a Half of Coexistence

For approximately the century and a half between Alexander's conquest and the accession of Antiochus IV, Yevanim (יְוָנִים), Misyavnim, and observant Jews lived in relative — if uneasy — peace. Schools and gymnasia operated; many Jews remained shomrei mitzvos (שׁוֹמְרֵי מִצְווֹת). By the time we reach the days of the Chashmonaim (חַשְׁמוֹנָאִים), however, a different Greek dynasty has taken over: the Seleucids (Seleukidim). Under their rule, the pressure upon Jewish religious life began to intensify.

IV. Klal Yisrael Without Kings

Who was melech (מֶלֶךְ) during all these generations? Nobody. Our last king was Tzidkiyahu HaMelech (צִדְקִיָּהוּ הַמֶּלֶךְ), dragged in shalsha’os shel barzel (שַׁלְשְׁלָאוֹת שֶׁל בַּרְזֶל) — chains of iron — to Bavel (בָּבֶל) at the end of churban Bayis Rishon (חֻרְבַּן בַּיִת רִאשׁוֹן). After that, the malchus (מַלְכוּת) in Klal Yisrael (כְּלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל) ended.

Leadership devolved to the Kohanim Gedolim (כֹּהֲנִים גְּדוֹלִים) and to the Tana’im (תַּנָּאִים). This is the early tekufah (תְּקוּפָה) of the Tana’im: Antignos Ish Socho (אַנְטִיגְנוֹס אִישׁ סוֹכוֹ) — note the Greek name — at the head of Maseches Avos (מַסֶּכֶת אָבוֹת). Throughout this era a Sanhedrin (סַנְהֶדְרִין) of seventy or seventy-one dayanim (דַּיָּנִים) functioned with a Nasi (נָשִׂיא) at its head. But there was no king.

No Navi → No Melech

The end of nevu’ah (נְבוּאָה) coincides with the beginning of Bayis Sheni (בַּיִת שֵׁנִי): Chaggai (חַגַּי), Zechariah (זְכַרְיָה), and Malachi (מַלְאָכִי) are the last of the nevi’im (נְבִיאִים). As the poskim (פּוֹסְקִים) note, one requires a navi (נָבִיא) to memaneh (מְמַנֶּה) a king.[7] No prophet, no coronation. On its face this itself may seem sufficient to explain the absence of a melech throughout Bayis Sheni, and so also to complicate the Chashmonaim (חַשְׁמוֹנָאִים)'s claim to the throne. Yet the requirement is itself shanui b’machlokes (שָׁנוּי בְּמַחֲלוֹקֶת) among the Rishonim (רִאשׁוֹנִים), and it does not by itself settle the deeper question that lies before us — for even a king appointed by prophecy would still have to belong to Shevet Yehudah (שֵׁבֶט יְהוּדָה) if Yaakov Avinu (יַעֲקֹב אָבִינוּ)'s blessing is a binding tzivui (צִוּוּי). The absence of prophecy is a necessary condition of the whole conversation; the sufficient explanation lies elsewhere, and it is to that we return in Sections XIII–XV.

V. Antiochus Reverses Policy

The Wicked Antiochus IV

Antiochus IV — Antiochus HaRasha (אַנְטִיוֹכוּס הָרָשָׁע), “Antiochus the Wicked” of the Chanukah songs — was unlike his father, his uncle, and his grandfather, all of whom had been Greek emperors before him. He reversed the long-standing policy of religious autonomy. Where prior emperors had said: let them keep their Beis HaMikdash (בֵּית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ), their korbanos (קָרְבָּנוֹת), their Sanhedrin (סַנְהֶדְרִין), their laws — we will tax and govern them but leave their religion alone — Antiochus declared: I am going to get involved.

Corruption of the Kehunah Gedolah

Before the story of Chanukah, the Kohen Gadol (כֹּהֵן גָּדוֹל) held the highest position in Klal Yisrael (כְּלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל): he ran the Beis HaMikdash, the spiritual epicenter of the people. But the Kohanim Gedolim (כֹּהֲנִים גְּדוֹלִים) of Bayis Sheni (בַּיִת שֵׁנִי) were largely not tzaddikim (צַדִּיקִים). Most had become deeply misyaven (מִתְיַוֵּן), deeply influenced by the Greeks. They cultivated political connections with local Greek governors and emperors, and — as recorded both in Chazal (חֲזַ״ל) and in secular history — bribed their way into the Kehunah Gedolah (כְּהֻנָּה גְּדוֹלָה), though they were neither bekiim (בְּקִיאִים) in halachah (הֲלָכָה) nor particularly frum.

The Gemara (גְּמָרָא) in Yoma (יוֹמָא)[8] records, in the context of the ketores (קְטֹרֶת) in the Kodesh HaKodashim (קֹדֶשׁ הַקָּדָשִׁים) on Yom Kippur (יוֹם כִּפּוּר), that many such Kohanim Gedolim were Tzedukim (צְדוּקִים) — including the notorious case of the nisuch ha’mayim (נִסּוּךְ הַמַּיִם) on Sukkos (סֻכּוֹת). They wanted the money, the power, the authority — and that was all.

The Tragic Cycle and Civil War

Historians have wondered how Kohanim Gedolim could bribe so heavily and fight so fiercely for an office in which many of them died during their first Yom Kippur avodah (עֲבוֹדָה). The answer is that the cycle was constant: civil strife was the rule, not the exception, throughout most of the 420 years of Bayis Sheni. There was sibling rivalry among the Kohanim Gedolim, milchamos (מִלְחָמוֹת) between Prushim (פְּרוּשִׁים) and Tzedukim, and open conflict between shomrei mitzvos (שׁוֹמְרֵי מִצְווֹת) and the Misyavnim (מִתְיַוְּנִים). Among Jews, two are enough for a good geshmak argument; in those years, the demographic minority of Jews in Eretz Yisrael (אֶרֶץ יִשְׂרָאֵל) compared with those in galus Bavel (גָּלוּת בָּבֶל) made the in-fighting all the more pronounced.

VI. The Sefarim Chitzonim and Megillas Antiochus

Certain books written in this era — the Sefarim Chitzonim (סְפָרִים חִיצוֹנִים), “external books” — sit outside the canon. They were never accepted into our sifrei kodesh (סִפְרֵי קֹדֶשׁ), and some appear to have been authored by Misyavnim (מִתְיַוְּנִים). Two are especially relevant here: Sefer HaMaccabim I & II (סֵפֶר הַמַּכַּבִּים).[9] We do not learn them as sifrei kodesh; their dates and details are debated. For general background of the era, however, they suffice.

A separate work — Megillas Antiochus (מְגִלַּת אַנְטִיוֹכוּס), also called Megillas Bnei Chashmonai (מְגִלַּת בְּנֵי חַשְׁמוֹנַאי) — is printed in many siddurim (סִדּוּרִים).[10] Tradition holds that it was written by Shammai (שַׁמַּאי) and Hillel (הִלֵּל), transmitted directly from Mattisyahu ben Yochanan Kohen Gadol (מַתִּתְיָהוּ בֶּן יוֹחָנָן כֹּהֵן גָּדוֹל) and his five sons; Rav Saadia Gaon (רַב סְעַדְיָה גָּאוֹן) was the first to print it in his siddur, attesting in his introduction that the mesorah (מְסוֹרָה) traces back to Mattisyahu (מַתִּתְיָהוּ)'s family. It is a heavily abridged account of the events.

VII. Jason and Menelaus — Civil War in the Kehunah

Jason / Yeshua

The Kohen Gadol (כֹּהֵן גָּדוֹל) in the days approaching Antiochus was Jason — a Greek name. According to Yosef ben Matisyahu (יוֹסֵף בֶּן מַתִּתְיָהוּ) (Josephus Flavius),[11] his Hebrew name was Yeshua (יֵשׁוּעַ). There is an earlier Yeshua Kohen Gadol (without the heh (ה)) in Tanach (תַּנַ״ךְ); in our literature this later figure is called Yeshua or Jason. He had connections with the local Greek governor.

Menelaus's Coup

Jason's rival, Menelaus — a Jew and, according to Josephus's account, a brother of Jason and himself a Kohen (כֹּהֵן) — coveted the Kehunah Gedolah (כְּהֻנָּה גְּדוֹלָה) for himself. (The sources here diverge: Josephus makes Menelaus Jason's brother, while Sefer HaMaccabim II (סֵפֶר הַמַּכַּבִּים) identifies him differently and disputes his priestly credentials.[12]) When a rumor spread that Antiochus had been killed in battle in Africa, Menelaus saw his moment: he took a thousand soldiers — a thousand fellow Jews — and attacked. Chaos overtook Yerushalayim (יְרוּשָׁלַיִם): who was the legitimate Kohen Gadol? Even the rabbinic Jews who came to the Beis HaMikdash (בֵּית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ) with their korbanos (קָרְבָּנוֹת) did not know.

VIII. Antiochus's Rage and the Desecration

The rumor was false. Antiochus was alive — and enraged. He returned in haste to Eretz Yisrael (אֶרֶץ יִשְׂרָאֵל) and plunged into local Jewish politics in a way no Greek emperor before him had ever attempted. He determined who would serve as Kohen Gadol (כֹּהֵן גָּדוֹל); eventually he assumed control of the Beis HaMikdash (בֵּית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ) himself.

The Pig in the Kodesh HaKodashim

The first act of the desecration was to bring a pig — a davar acher (דָּבָר אַחֵר) — into our holy Temple, slaughter it there, and sprinkle its blood in the Kodesh HaKodashim (קֹדֶשׁ הַקָּדָשִׁים) and on the mizbe’ach (מִזְבֵּחַ) outside. He forced Kohanim (כֹּהֲנִים) to consume the flesh; those who refused were shechted (נִשְׁחֲטוּ) on the spot. He moved through Yerushalayim (יְרוּשָׁלַיִם) in a boiling rage, killing anyone identified as a rabbinic Jew — the various accounts give figures on the order of tens of thousands slain, with many more exiled.[13] From this point, the milchamos (מִלְחָמוֹת) with the Greeks turned existential.

The Gezeiros

The gezeiros (גְּזֵרוֹת) Antiochus enacted focused upon milah (מִילָה), Shabbos (שַׁבָּת), and Rosh Chodesh (רֹאשׁ חֹדֶשׁ) — three mitzvos (מִצְווֹת) the Misyavnim (מִתְיַוְּנִים) already targeted. The particular chochmah (חָכְמָה) in singling out milah: the Hellenists worshipped the human body. They were narcissists; they had no bushah (בּוּשָׁה) — none of the bayshanim (בַּיְשָׁנִים), rachmanim (רַחְמָנִים), gomlei chasadim (גּוֹמְלֵי חֲסָדִים) that the Gemara (גְּמָרָא) in Beitzah (בֵּיצָה)[14] identifies as the three defining simanim (סִימָנִים) of Klal Yisrael (כְּלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל). In their gymnasia, everyone trained naked. They could therefore see at a glance who had a bris (בְּרִית) — and put him to death.

The Rambam's Atypical Aside

The Rambam (רַמְבַּ״ם) at the head of Hilchos Chanukah (הִלְכוֹת חֲנֻכָּה)[15] does something almost unparalleled elsewhere in the Yad (יָד): he narrates the history. Not the halachos (הֲלָכוֹת) first, but the events. He does not enumerate each gezeirah (גְּזֵרָה), but he paints the picture of the depth of the crisis under malchus Yavan (מַלְכוּת יָוָן). The Rambam writing divrei ha’yamim (דִּבְרֵי הַיָּמִים) at the head of a halachah (הֲלָכָה) is itself a limmud (לִמּוּד) upon the chashivus (חֲשִׁיבוּת) of the events for the halachah that follows.

IX. Mattisyahu — The Unlikely Hero

The Irony of Yochanan Kohen Gadol

Mattisyahu ben Yochanan Kohen Gadol (מַתִּתְיָהוּ בֶּן יוֹחָנָן כֹּהֵן גָּדוֹל) witnessed the destruction in Yerushalayim (יְרוּשָׁלַיִם). He was a rabbinic Jew, a parush (פָּרוּשׁ). The most piercing detail of his biography: his father, Yochanan Kohen Gadol (יוֹחָנָן כֹּהֵן גָּדוֹל), was in the end a Tzeduki (צְדוּקִי). The Gemara (גְּמָרָא) in Berachos (בְּרָכוֹת)[16] records that for eighty years Yochanan was the most venerated Jew of his generation — and at the close of his life turned Tzeduki. From this the Mishnah (מִשְׁנָה) in Avos (אָבוֹת)[17] derives:

אַל תַּאֲמִין בְּעַצְמְךָ עַד יוֹם מוֹתְךָ.
“Do not trust in yourself until the day of your death.”
Avos 2:4

And the wonder is that Yochanan the Tzeduki produced Mattisyahu (מַתִּתְיָהוּ) and the five sons whom we honor in davening as tzaddikim, kedoshim, u’tehorim (צַדִּיקִים, קְדוֹשִׁים, וּטְהוֹרִים). Something turned. That the same tree bore both the collapse at the end of eighty years and the flowering of the rebellion is itself among the quiet miracles of the era.

Modi’in

Mattisyahu fled to Modi’in (מוֹדִיעִין) — still today a place in Eretz Yisrael (אֶרֶץ יִשְׂרָאֵל), near Kfar HaChashmonaim (כְּפַר הַחַשְׁמוֹנָאִים). Antiochus's people had set up altars to Zeus there, and Antiochus decreed public korbanos (קָרְבָּנוֹת) to Zeus, compelling the local Jews to attend. Mattisyahu approached the altar, drew a sword, and struck down the Misyaven (מִתְיַוֵּן) who was bringing the offering — a Jew who simply did not care. With his sons he fled into the forests around Modi’in, where they hid for a time.

The Rebellion in the Forests

There they gathered a small army — me’atim b’yad rabim (מְעַטִּים בְּיַד רַבִּים), as we say in davening (דַּאוֶענֶען). Perhaps a few hundred men. Against the mighty Greek army it was, by every standard of ancient warfare, ludicrous. And precisely in that lay its beauty.

X. The Rebellion and the Falls of Elazar and Yehudah

Strategy

Each son took a band of men to a different region of Eretz Yisrael (אֶרֶץ יִשְׂרָאֵל) and fought every local governor of Antiochus in turn.

Elazar and the Elephants

Antiochus, having campaigned to India, to North Africa, and eastward, had returned with armored elephants — the tanks of the ancient world. Elazar (אֶלְעָזָר), son of Mattisyahu (מַתִּתְיָהוּ), was a gibor (גִּבּוֹר): in one battle he ran between the elephants and killed them from below the belly with a spear. Megillas Antiochus (מְגִלַּת אַנְטִיוֹכוּס) describes a gruesome end — from the mass of tzo’ah (צוֹאָה), the toxic excrement of so many elephants, Elazar drowned. The Megillah (מְגִלָּה) here narrates from within the tradition; the details are grim.

The Origin of the Name “Maccabi”

Of Yehudah (יְהוּדָה) — the strongest of the brothers — the popular pshat (פְּשָׁט) takes Maccabi (מַכַּבִּי) as an acronym for Mi Kamocha Ba’eilim Hashem (מִי כָּמֹכָה בָּאֵלִים ה׳). Nevertheless the simple pshat is otherwise: Maccabi is an acronym for Mattisyahu Ben Yochanan Kohen — מ.ב.י.כ. — the family name. The popular reading is beautiful and speaks to the heart; but the historical pshat is the family name.

Mattisyahu's Charge

When Yehudah fell, his brothers returned to their father with the question: Yehudah was the strongest. He was the eldest. What do we do now? Mattisyahu — already an old man — took up his sword:

“I will fight in place of mourning my son's death. Now is the time to fight. A Jew must be proud to fight for the truth.”

He ran with his sons to the battlefield. Victory followed victory; the small Judean state outlasted, upon the field, one of the mightiest empires of the ancient world.

Antiochus the Fugitive

The mesorah (מְסוֹרָה) in Megillas Antiochus gives an account of Antiochus's end at variance with the ancient historians. According to the Megillah, as his army was slaughtered wave upon wave, Antiochus boarded a ship and fled; the Jews called him Antiochus HaBore’ach (אַנְטִיוֹכוּס הַבּוֹרֵחַ), “Antiochus the Fugitive,” and a bushah (בּוּשָׁה) was set upon his head. The Greek historians (Polybius; Sefer HaMaccabim I & II (סֵפֶר הַמַּכַּבִּים)) record instead that Antiochus died of illness on the Persian campaign in Elam.[18] Either way, his empire fell from him, and other Greek dynasts inherited what he had lost.

XI. The Story of Chanukah and the Menorah

The story of Chanukah itself may be treated briefly. They won the battles — k’mo she’omrim b’davening b’al ha’nissim (כְּמוֹ שֶׁאוֹמְרִים בְּדַאוֶענֶען בְּעַל הַנִּסִּים). They found a pach shemen (פַּךְ שֶׁמֶן). They lit the menorah (מְנוֹרָה).

Why the Menorah?

Why does the miracle of the menorah take center stage? Because with that lighting they reinstituted avodas ha’mikdash (עֲבוֹדַת הַמִּקְדָּשׁ). For a long stretch, no one could enter the Beis HaMikdash (בֵּית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ): a temple to Zeus stood within it; Antiochus had brought korbanos (קָרְבָּנוֹת) to avodah zarah (עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה) upon our own mizbe’ach (מִזְבֵּחַ). Now, at last, they returned to the Beis HaMikdash; they reclaimed it; it was theirs. The nes (נֵס) of the menorah stands in for the deeper inyan (עִנְיָן): this is chanukas ha’mizbe’ach (חֲנֻכַּת הַמִּזְבֵּחַ) — the rededication of the mizbe’ach and of the Beis HaMikdash. (The relation between the Al Ha’Nissim (עַל הַנִּסִּים) account, which emphasizes the battles, and the Gemara (גְּמָרָא)'s account, which emphasizes the pach shemen, is a sugya (סוּגְיָא) of its own; here we take them as complementary — the outer victory and the inner rededication as one nes with two faces.)

XII. Malchus Beis Chashmonai — The Great Question Returns

The Rambam's Striking Halachah

The Rambam (רַמְבַּ״ם) at the beginning of Hilchos Chanukah (הִלְכוֹת חֲנֻכָּה) writes — in a manner he adopts in almost no other halachah (הֲלָכָה) — that the Chashmonaim (חַשְׁמוֹנָאִים) seized the throne and became kings over kol Yisrael (כָּל יִשְׂרָאֵל). For centuries there had been no real kings, only Sanhedrin (סַנְהֶדְרִין), dayanim (דַּיָּנִים), Tana’im (תַּנָּאִים), chachamim (חֲכָמִים), yeshivos (יְשִׁיבוֹת). And now malchus Beis Chashmonai (מַלְכוּת בֵּית חַשְׁמוֹנַאי) decided: we are going to be kings. This is the point at which the framing kushya (קֻשְׁיָא) of the entire shiur (שִׁיעוּר) bites: Kohanim (כֹּהֲנִים), who — measured against Yaakov Avinu (יַעֲקֹב אָבִינוּ)'s blessing — have no business being kings. (The Rambam's own nusach (נֻסָּח) here is one to which we shall return in Section XV; for the moment we let the difficulty stand in its sharpest form.)

The Two-Hundred-Year Dynasty

For roughly the next two centuries of Bayis Sheni (בַּיִת שֵׁנִי), the Chashmonaim ruled. The line runs, in outline:

  • First — the sons of Mattisyahu (מַתִּתְיָהוּ): Shimon (שִׁמְעוֹן), and Yochanan (יוֹחָנָן).
  • Then — Alexander Jannaeus (Yannai (יַנַּאי)).
  • Then — Aristobulus.
  • And other descendants — many bearing Greek names.

It began nobly: they were k’doshim (קְדוֹשִׁים); we still call them al yedei kohanecha ha’kedoshim (עַל יְדֵי כֹּהֲנֶיךָ הַקְּדוֹשִׁים) in Al Ha’Nissim (עַל הַנִּסִּים). It descended, over the generations, into sibling rivalry within the Jewish royal court, and into constant strife through the rest of Bayis Sheni.

XIII. The Ramban — The Question Is the Answer

The Ramban on Vayechi

The Ramban (רַמְבַּ״ן) in Parshas Vayechi (פָּרָשַׁת וַיְחִי)[19] asks the same question: how did the Chashmonaim (חַשְׁמוֹנָאִים) take the throne for themselves?

His answer is severe: the question is the answer. It was wrong. It was a sin. It was a crime. The Ramban — a thousand years after the events — has the moral koach (כֹּחַ) to say it. Lo yasur shevet mi’Yehudah (לֹא יָסוּר שֵׁבֶט מִיהוּדָה) is a tzivui (צִוּוּי) from Yaakov Avinu (יַעֲקֹב אָבִינוּ); the Chashmonaim, though tzaddikim kedoshim u’tehorim (צַדִּיקִים קְדוֹשִׁים וּטְהוֹרִים), sat upon a throne that was not theirs.

The Anticipated Question — Shaul HaMelech

One might immediately protest: but Shaul HaMelech (שָׁאוּל הַמֶּלֶךְ) was the first melech (מֶלֶךְ), and he was of Shevet Binyamin (שֵׁבֶט בִּנְיָמִין). The Ramban's reading holds: Shaul (שָׁאוּל) preceded the establishment of Malchus Beis David (מַלְכוּת בֵּית דָּוִד). Once David HaMelech (דָּוִד הַמֶּלֶךְ)'s dynasty was selected, Lo yasur shevet mi’Yehudah applied l’doros (לְדוֹרוֹת). The very availability of Shaul under this reading concedes, however, that malchus (מַלְכוּת) from another shevet (שֵׁבֶט) is not l’chatchilah (לְכַתְּחִלָּה) impossible — a concession that will become important below when the alternative reading of Rav Yaakov (רַב יַעֲקֹב) presses in the same direction from the other side.

The Punishment — Hordus

This is why, says the Ramban, these tzaddikim (צַדִּיקִים) who saved Klal Yisrael (כְּלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל), who bequeathed us the nes (נֵס) of Chanukah — suffered so terribly during their reign. Hordus (הוֹרְדוֹס) killed them all. The Gemara (גְּמָרָא) at the head of Maseches Bava Basra (מַסֶּכֶת בָּבָא בָּתְרָא)[20] states it plainly. Hordus had been a slave of the Chashmonaim; he rebelled and slaughtered them.

Was this mikreh (מִקְרֶה) — coincidence? No, says the Ramban. You take what is not yours, you lose what you have. They desired a throne beyond what belonged to them; being Kohanim (כֹּהֲנִים) was not enough for them; for that, Hashem (הַשֵּׁם) caused them to be wiped out.

The Caveat About Shimon

The Ramban adds, almost in three words, a caveat: Shimon (שִׁמְעוֹן), one of the sons of Mattisyahu (מַתִּתְיָהוּ), was a shtikl Tzeduki (אַ שְׁטִיקְל צְדוּקִי) — somewhat influenced by the Tzedukim (צְדוּקִים). We praise the brothers together as tzaddikim kedoshim (צַדִּיקִים קְדוֹשִׁים); the Ramban hints that there was an additional cheit (חֵטְא) in Shimon's line. The Rishonim (רִאשׁוֹנִים) who examined this have records of Shimon's connection with the Tzedukim: perhaps not a Tzeduki (צְדוּקִי) himself, but influenced — and his descendants therefore deviated. Whether the Ramban's ikar cheit (עִיקַּר חֵטְא) is the malchus and Shimon's slide a secondary aggravation, or whether the Shimon line's specific cheit is what triggered the destruction upon the whole house, is a question that turns on the reader's ear for the lashon (לָשׁוֹן); both readings have been held.

The Last Chashmona’is

The Gemara records that Hordus killed them all — except for one girl, who survived. She climbed to the roof; just before casting herself down, she cried out (as a Chashmona’is (חַשְׁמוֹנָאִית)):

כָּל דְּאָמַר «מִבֵּית חַשְׁמוֹנַאי קָאָתִינָא», עַבְדָּא הוּא.
“Anyone who says, ‘I come from the House of the Chashmonaim’ — he is a slave [i.e., of Hordus, and not of the Chashmona’i line].”
Bava Basra 3b

Hordus, the mad tyrant, preserved her body in honey for seven years; the Gemara records a machlokes (מַחֲלוֹקֶת) as to what he did with it thereafter (v’ein kan ha’makom le’ha’arich (וְאֵין כָּאן הַמָּקוֹם לְהַאֲרִיךְ)).

XIV. The Sanhedrin's Silence — Rav Yaakov Kamenetsky in Emes L’Yaakov

The Real Question

If the Chashmonaim (חַשְׁמוֹנָאִים)'s malchus (מַלְכוּת) was a cheit (חֵטְא), why did the Sanhedrin (סַנְהֶדְרִין) remain silent? The Sanhedrin functioned and operated in full force throughout this era. They were enacting halachos (הֲלָכוֹת), gezeiros (גְּזֵרוֹת), takanos (תַּקָּנוֹת), siyagim (סְיָיגִים). The entire body of Mishnayos (מִשְׁנָיוֹת) across the shishah sidrei mishnah (שִׁשָּׁה סִדְרֵי מִשְׁנָה) was generated during this tekufah (תְּקוּפָה) — to be compiled centuries later by Rabbeinu HaKadosh (רַבֵּינוּ הַקָּדוֹשׁ), but generated here. A massive halachic body — and not a single mecha’ah (מְחָאָה) preserved?

Rav Yaakov Kamenetsky

Rav Yaakov Kamenetsky (רַב יַעֲקֹב קַמֶנֶצְקִי) in his sefer (סֵפֶר) Emes L’Yaakov (אֱמֶת לְיַעֲקֹב)[21] — one of the most illuminating and somewhat under-appreciated sefarim (סְפָרִים) of recent decades, embracing peirushim (פֵּירוּשִׁים) on Torah (תּוֹרָה), on the Rambam (רַמְבַּ״ם), and on Maseches Avos (מַסֶּכֶת אָבוֹת) — supplies a yesod (יְסוֹד) that reframes not only this question but the whole shape of Bayis Sheni (בַּיִת שֵׁנִי). Rooted in the Slabodka world and baki (בָּקִי) in Tanach (תַּנַ״ךְ) and Jewish history as few in his generation were, he reads the era structurally.

The Yesod — Bayis Sheni Was Never Meant to Be a Geulah Sheleimah

Once Nevuchadnetzar (נְבוּכַדְנֶצַּר) exiled us to Bavel (בָּבֶל), says Rav Yaakov (רַב יַעֲקֹב), it was over. We returned; we received permission to build a Beis HaMikdash (בֵּית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ) — but only as a tekufah arai (תְּקוּפָה אֲרַאי), an interim period. Chazal (חֲזַ״ל) understood that the Yiddishe structure of self-rule in Eretz Yisrael (אֶרֶץ יִשְׂרָאֵל) would not again be what it had been until Mashiach (מָשִׁיחַ) arrives.

The signs are laid out in the Gemara (גְּמָרָא)'s list of differences between the two Batei Mikdash (בָּתֵּי מִקְדָּשׁ):

  • No Aron (אָרוֹן)
  • No Keruvim (כְּרוּבִים)
  • No Hashra’as ha’Shechinah (הַשְׁרָאַת הַשְּׁכִינָה) in the same measure

Many of the giluyim (גִּלּוּיִים) that defined Bayis Rishon (בַּיִת רִאשׁוֹן) simply did not exist in Bayis Sheni.

Therefore — No Plans for a Melech

Chazal at the time were not planning to appoint a king. They knew this geulah (גְּאֻלָּה) was interim; they knew we would yet return to galus (גָּלוּת) until “ki yavo Shiloh” (כִּי יָבֹא שִׁילֹה); they knew Mashiach would bring the tikkun ha’shaleim (תִּיקוּן הַשָּׁלֵם). They feared that establishing a true malchus (מַלְכוּת) would cause Klal Yisrael (כְּלַל יִשְׂרָאֵל) to mistake the moment for the geulah ha’sheleimah (גְּאֻלָּה הַשְּׁלֵמָה) — to think it was over, the work was done, the days of David (דָּוִד) restored. So they davka refrained.

Why So Many Gezeiros and Takanos in Bayis Sheni?

Rav Yaakov presses the reading further. Why is it specifically in Bayis Sheni that Chazal generated the vast bulk of the gezeiros (גְּזֵרוֹת) and takanos (תַּקָּנוֹת)? Because Chazal knew this tekufah (תְּקוּפָה) would last 420 years — long, but not forever. They knew Klal Yisrael was on the way out, into a galus of a thousand years and more. The Jews would need fortification for what was coming. They needed siyagim (סְיָיגִים), gederim (גְּדָרִים), and gezeiros — protections that are not d’oraisa (דְּאוֹרַיְתָא) but d’rabbanan (דְּרַבָּנָן). This is why davka in Bayis Sheni, the Chashmonaim themselves, the Kohanim Gedolim (כֹּהֲנִים גְּדוֹלִים), the Sanhedrin, and the chachamim (חֲכָמִים) all multiplied gezeiros and takanos. Three otherwise-loose facts — no king, a proliferation of takanos, the Sanhedrin's silence — reduce to one explanatory arc.

The Chachamim's Silence Explained

And so when the Chashmonaim assumed the throne — the chachamim did not protest. They did not even hold that there should be a king in this tekufah. They wanted the status quo: Sanhedrin, dayanim (דַּיָּנִים), chachamim, and that alone. This was not a time for malchus.

Why then did the Chashmonaim seize it? Because they felt: “Im lo achshav, eimasai?” (אִם לֹא עַכְשָׁיו, אֵימָתַי?) — if not now, when? If we do not consolidate power, all the battles of the past decade will lose their effect. The unity that binds Klal Yisrael together against the Misyavnim (מִתְיַוְּנִים) will dissolve. Someone must run the malchus and the Beis HaMikdash (בֵּית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ) simultaneously. Therefore, says Rav Yaakov, it was not so grave a cheit. Perhaps they should have declared their reign expressly as hora’as sha’ah (הוֹרָאַת שָׁעָה) — a temporary malchus for the needs of the hour, without pretense of the malchus l’olam (מַלְכוּת לְעוֹלָם) reserved for Beis David (בֵּית דָּוִד).

And Zerubavel?

The reading is confirmed by another line altogether. Zerubavel (זְרֻבָּבֶל), in Ezra (עֶזְרָא) and Nechemiah (נְחֶמְיָה), was of Beis David and was to have served as king. He did not. For a temporary kingdom in the spirit of a hora’as sha’ah, then, even another shevet (שֵׁבֶט) can serve. Lo yasur shevet mi’Yehudah (לֹא יָסוּר שֵׁבֶט מִיהוּדָה), in this view, is a statement about malchus l’olam — the eternal kingship — not about every interim arrangement. And that, says Rav Yaakov, is exactly what happened with the Chashmonaim.

A Note on the Mechanics

The category of hora’as sha’ah malchus that Rav Yaakov quietly deploys carries real weight. Whether such a king carries the standard dinim (דִּינִים) of Melech Yisrael (מֶלֶךְ יִשְׂרָאֵל) — moredin (מוֹרְדִין), bidud kavod (בִּדּוּד כָּבוֹד), arba’ah amos (אַרְבַּע אַמּוֹת), transmission to his son, the parshah (פָּרָשָׁה) of Ha’Melech (הַמֶּלֶךְ) — is a discussion of its own, and lies beyond the scope of the present sugya (סוּגְיָא). What is essential here is only the meta-halachic claim: there exists a category of malchus less than the malchus l’olam of Beis David, which can be assumed l’sha’ah (לְשָׁעָה) by another shevet when the needs of the hour compel it. That is the door through which the Chashmonaim's reign passes, in the reading of Rav Yaakov.

XV. The Rambam vs. the Ramban — Tzivui or Havtachah?

The Rambam's Actual Lashon

If one returns to the Rambam (רַמְבַּ״ם) in Hilchos Chanukah (הִלְכוֹת חֲנֻכָּה) with this reading in mind, the lashon (לָשׁוֹן) itself begins to speak. The Rambam writes:

וְגָבְרוּ בְּנֵי חַשְׁמוֹנַאי הַכֹּהֲנִים הַגְּדוֹלִים... וְחָזְרָה מַלְכוּת לְיִשְׂרָאֵל יֶתֶר עַל מָאתַיִם שָׁנָה.
“And the sons of the House of Chashmonai, the great Kohanim, prevailed... and the kingship returned to Israel for more than two hundred years.”
Rambam, Hilchos Chanukah 3:1

The verbs are the language of praise, not of narration. Gavru (גָּבְרוּ), gedolim (גְּדוֹלִים), chazrah malchus le’Yisrael (חָזְרָה מַלְכוּת לְיִשְׂרָאֵל) — the kingdom returned. We were no longer under Greeks, Babylonians, or Persians. A king of our own — but only temporarily.

The Underlying Machlokes

Here lies the foundational machlokes (מַחֲלוֹקֶת):

  • The Ramban (רַמְבַּ״ן) — “Lo yasur shevet mi’Yehudah” (לֹא יָסוּר שֵׁבֶט מִיהוּדָה) is a tzivui (צִוּוּי), a command of Yaakov Avinu (יַעֲקֹב אָבִינוּ) to his descendants. Take the throne if you are not of Beis David (בֵּית דָּוִד), and you have transgressed the tzivui of your zaken (זָקֵן).
  • The Rambam — “Lo yasur” is a havtachah (הַבְטָחָה), a promise. For all eternity Malchus Beis David (מַלְכוּת בֵּית דָּוִד) will rule. But this leaves room for hora’as sha’ah (הוֹרָאַת שָׁעָה) kings from other shevatim (שְׁבָטִים) during the intervening ages.

A Parallel Machlokes — Yishuv Eretz Yisrael

The very same axis reappears between the same two Rishonim (רִאשׁוֹנִים) in a wholly different sugya (סוּגְיָא): yishuv Eretz Yisrael (יִשּׁוּב אֶרֶץ יִשְׂרָאֵל).

  • The Ramban: “V’horashtem osah v’yeshavtem bah” (וְהוֹרַשְׁתֶּם אֹתָהּ וִישַׁבְתֶּם בָּהּ)[22] — it is a mitzvah d’oraisa (מִצְוָה דְּאוֹרַיְתָא), binding in every generation.[23]
  • The Rambam: it is not counted among the taryag mitzvos (תַּרְיַ״ג מִצְווֹת) — it is a havtachah.

One may paskin (פַּסְקִין) as he wishes; many Rishonim hold with the Ramban, and in our own generation the ruling is certainly that it is a mitzvah (מִצְוָה). This is not a matter of politics but of halachah (הֲלָכָה). The same machlokes that decides whether the Chashmonaim (חַשְׁמוֹנָאִים) sinned in taking the throne decides also whether one who lives in Chutz La’Aretz (חוּץ לָאָרֶץ) thereby refrains from a mitzvah. Tzivui or havtachah — the axis is one.

On the Combination of Keser Kehunah and Keser Malchus

An adjacent question deserves at least a mention. The Torah (תּוֹרָה) keeps three kesarim (כְּתָרִים) — Kehunah (כְּהֻנָּה), Torah, and Malchus — largely separate; and a serving Kohen (כֹּהֵן) upon the throne raises a difficulty independent of the Yehudah (יְהוּדָה) question. Even were hora’as sha’ah malchus from another shevet (שֵׁבֶט) fully licit in principle, one might still ask whether the very family of the Kohanim Gedolim (כֹּהֲנִים גְּדוֹלִים) is the right family to receive it. The Ramban's severity may be reading not only from Lo yasur shevet mi’Yehudah alone but from the additional pressure of the kehunah/malchus combination; and this may be a further part of what he means when he writes that the destruction of the line was midah k’neged midah (מִדָּה כְּנֶגֶד מִדָּה).

XVI. Why Is There No Maseches Chanukah?

If the Chashmonaim (חַשְׁמוֹנָאִים)'s malchus (מַלְכוּת) was itself contested in principle, one might ask why the yom tov (יוֹם טוֹב) they gave us has no maseches (מַסֶּכֶת) at all. Rabbeinu HaKadosh (רַבֵּינוּ הַקָּדוֹשׁ) gave us a maseches for nearly every yom tov — Megillah (מְגִלָּה), Yoma (יוֹמָא), Rosh HaShanah (רֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה), Sukkah (סֻכָּה). Why no Maseches Chanukah (מַסֶּכֶת חֲנֻכָּה)?

The Chasam Sofer's Reported Explanation

The Chasam Sofer (חֲתַם סוֹפֵר)'s Sefer Chut Ha’Meshulash (סֵפֶר חוּט הַמְשֻׁלָּשׁ)[24] — compiled by his grandson — records an explanation, though not from the Chasam Sofer's own pen. The Chasam Sofer wrote more than nearly any other rav (רַב) in his generation, and this particular thing he did not write down; the grandson's testimony alone is our source, which should be weighed accordingly. The reported statement: Rabbeinu HaKadosh (רַבֵּינוּ הַקָּדוֹשׁ) came from Zera Beis David (זֶרַע בֵּית דָּוִד); he was the Nasi (נָשִׂיא), the Reish Galusa (רֵישׁ גָּלוּתָא) having his seat in Bavel (בָּבֶל).[25] Allegedly, Rabbeinu HaKadosh took the Chashmonaim's seizure of the throne as a family injury — “You Chashmonaim dared take away our throne? I will not write a maseches about your yom tov.”

Read on its face the statement is deeply hard: Rabbeinu HaKadosh lit the menorah (מְנוֹרָה) as we light the menorah. Other rabbanim (רַבָּנִים) who quote this Chasam Sofer reframe it accordingly: Rabbeinu HaKadosh did not withhold the maseches vindictively but l’sheim shamayim (לְשֵׁם שָׁמַיִם) — a withholding b’cheshbon (בְּחֶשְׁבּוֹן), because our aveiros (עֲבֵירוֹת) caused the Chashmonaim not to be zocheh (זוֹכֶה) to a maseches. Even so, treated as oral tradition, the account is best held at some distance.

The Vilna Gaon — The Lost Maseches

A second tradition — the more compelling — comes from the Vilna Gaon (וִילְנָא גָּאוֹן). His son writes in his father's name that there once was a Maseches Chanukah, and we lost it.[26] Many masechtos (מַסֶּכְתּוֹת) were lost across the length of galus (גָּלוּת).

Rav Hai Gaon (רַב הַאי גָּאוֹן) records a startling episode: one day in the yeshivah (יְשִׁיבָה) in Bavel, an old man — zaken v’chacham v’yashish ba’yeshivah (זָקֵן וְחָכָם וְיָשִׁישׁ בַּיְשִׁיבָה) — appeared with a long white beard, took out an entire maseches that they had never before seen, and read from it. Rav Hai Gaon begged him: “Stay! Let me copy this maseches! We have all shishah sidrei mishnah (שִׁשָּׁה סִדְרֵי מִשְׁנָה) — but we are missing this one!” The man said, “I must go,” and departed. Rav Hai Gaon lamented that he had lost that maseches forever.

The Gaon (גָּאוֹן)'s point was broader still. We have a Maseches Mezuzah (מַסֶּכֶת מְזוּזָה); what about tefillin (תְּפִלִּין)? Where is the dedicated maseches? The list of losses, said the Gaon, is one of the great tragadyos of galus.

The Sefas Emes — A Chassidic Reading

The Sefas Emes (שְׂפַת אֱמֶת)[27] takes the absence of Maseches Chanukah and makes a simchah (שִׂמְחָה) of it. The chiddush (חִדּוּשׁ) — an emblematic move of chassidus (חֲסִידוּת): Chanukah is entirely Torah she’b’al peh (תּוֹרָה שֶׁבְּעַל פֶּה). That is precisely why it remains b’al peh (בְּעַל פֶּה) — there is no written tradition because the very essence of Chanukah is the unwritten chelek (חֵלֶק) of Torah (תּוֹרָה). The absence is not an accident of galus but a formal siman (סִימָן) of the yom tov's own chelek in Torah. The gedolei chassidus (גְּדוֹלֵי חֲסִידוּת) elaborate the yesod (יְסוֹד) in many directions.

XVII. Closing

We began with a question so hard that the whole shape of Bayis Sheni (בַּיִת שֵׁנִי) had to be traversed to answer it. Kohanim (כֹּהֲנִים) ascended a throne that Yaakov Avinu (יַעֲקֹב אָבִינוּ)'s blessing reserved for the descendants of Yehudah (יְהוּדָה). And the Rishonim (רִאשׁוֹנִים), reading the same event, opened two paths.

For the Ramban (רַמְבַּ״ן), Lo yasur shevet mi’Yehudah (לֹא יָסוּר שֵׁבֶט מִיהוּדָה) is a tzivui (צִוּוּי). The Chashmonaim (חַשְׁמוֹנָאִים), though tzaddikim kedoshim u’tehorim (צַדִּיקִים קְדוֹשִׁים וּטְהוֹרִים), took what was not theirs; the destruction of their house by Hordus (הוֹרְדוֹס) is the measured accounting of that cheit (חֵטְא). For Rav Yaakov Kamenetsky (רַב יַעֲקֹב קַמֶנֶצְקִי) — reading with the Rambam (רַמְבַּ״ם)'s nusach (נֻסָּח) in Hilchos Chanukah (הִלְכוֹת חֲנֻכָּה) at his ear — the pasuk (פָּסוּק) is a havtachah (הַבְטָחָה): Bayis Sheni was never meant to be the geulah ha’sheleimah (גְּאֻלָּה הַשְּׁלֵמָה); the chachamim (חֲכָמִים)'s silence about the coronation is the tell; the massive fortification of gezeiros (גְּזֵרוֹת) and takanos (תַּקָּנוֹת) in the tekufah (תְּקוּפָה) is the preparation; and the Chashmonaim's malchus (מַלְכוּת) is a defensible hora’as sha’ah (הוֹרָאַת שָׁעָה). The axis between them — tzivui or havtachah — is the same axis that divides the two Rishonim on yishuv Eretz Yisrael (יִשּׁוּב אֶרֶץ יִשְׂרָאֵל), and it is the deeper structural inheritance of the whole discussion.

And the mussar (מוּסָר) the Ramban draws does not depend on which side of the machlokes (מַחֲלוֹקֶת) one takes. Even great tzaddikim (צַדִּיקִים) — even the kedoshim u’tehorim whose blood was shed al kiddush Hashem (עַל קִדּוּשׁ הַשֵּׁם), whose nes (נֵס) we still light every winter — even they must know the limit of what belongs to them. When the crown that Yaakov Avinu placed upon the head of Yehudah is not l’chatchilah (לְכַתְּחִלָּה) one's own, one may perhaps wear it l’sha’ah (לְשָׁעָה) for the needs of the hour; but the wearing of it as if it were one's own carries a cheshbon (חֶשְׁבּוֹן). The Chashmona’is (חַשְׁמוֹנָאִית) on the rooftop, crying out that no future claimant to her family is anything but an eved (עֶבֶד), is the closing image of that cheshbon. And precisely against that image the nes of the menorah (מְנוֹרָה) — the reclamation of the mizbe’ach (מִזְבֵּחַ), the rededication of avodah (עֲבוֹדָה) — shines its clean and unmixed light.

Bamidbar 33:53 — וְהוֹרַשְׁתֶּם אֶת הָאָרֶץ וִישַׁבְתֶּם בָּהּ. — The pasuk upon which the Ramban and Rambam disagree: the Ramban reads it as a mitzvah d’oraisa of yishuv Eretz Yisrael; the Rambam does not count it among the taryag mitzvos, treating it as a havtachah. The structural parallel to the malchus dispute on Bereishis 49:10. ↩
  23. Ramban, Hosafos l’Sefer HaMitzvos, Mitzvas Asei 4. — The Ramban's addition to the Rambam's Sefer HaMitzvos: yishuv Eretz Yisrael as a mitzvah d’oraisa binding in every generation. The structural disagreement with the Rambam runs across multiple parshiyos along the axis of tzivui versus havtachah. ↩
  24. Sefer Chut Ha’Meshulash — biographical compilation of the Chasam Sofer by his grandson. — The reported explanation that Rabbeinu HaKadosh, descended from Zera Beis David, withheld a Maseches Chanukah in protest of the Chashmonaim's seizure of the throne. The Chasam Sofer himself never wrote this; the grandson's testimony is our sole source, and is to be weighed accordingly. Reframed by some rabbanim as a l’sheim shamayim withholding b’cheshbon rather than personal grievance. ↩
  25. Sanhedrin 5a (and parallels). — On the Reish Galusa in Bavel (descended from Beis David) and the Nasi in Eretz Yisrael. Relevant to the Chut Ha’Meshulash tradition insofar as it identifies Rabbeinu HaKadosh, as Nasi, with the Davidic line whose malchus claim the Chashmonaim displaced. ↩
  26. Vilna Gaon (cited by his son R’ Avraham); cf. Ma’aseh Rav and writings of Rav Hai Gaon on lost masechtos. — The Gaon's tradition: there had been a Maseches Chanukah, and we lost it through galus. Rav Hai Gaon's startling account of an old man who appeared at the yeshivah in Bavel with a previously-unknown maseches, read from it, and departed before they could copy it. Many masechtos on individual inyanim (e.g., tefillin, paralleling Maseches Mezuzah) are similarly lost. ↩
  27. Sefas Emes, Likutim, Chanukah; cf. drashos on Chanukah throughout the Sefas Emes. — The Chassidic chiddush: the absence of a Maseches Chanukah is itself the essence of the yom tov. Chanukah is the essence of Torah she’b’al peh — the unwritten chelek of Torah — and so it is preserved b’al peh alone. The gedolei chassidus elaborate the yesod in many directions. ↩
