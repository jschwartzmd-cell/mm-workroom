כֵּיצַד נַעֲשׂוּ הַחַשְׁמוֹנָאִים לִמְלָכִים?

How Did the Chashmonaim Become Kings?
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Chanukah / Erev Shabbos Mikeitz 5785
Recorded 25 December 2024  ·  Sefer Edition · Revised 23 August 2026

A Chanukah shiur built around a single deep kushya: how did a family of Kohanim ascend a throne that Yaakov Avinu's blessing had reserved for the descendants of Yehudah? The path in is long: the historical arc from Alexander of Macedon through the Misyavnim, the Seleucid reversal under Antiochus, the corruption of the Kehunah Gedolah, the Jason-Menelaus civil war, and the rebellion of Mattisyahu and his sons. The halachic core rests on a foundational machlokes: the Ramban in Parshas Vayechi, for whom the seizure of the throne was a cheit punished by Hordus wiping out the line; and Rav Yaakov Kamenetsky in Emes L'Yaakov, whose structural reading — Bayis Sheni as an interim tekufah that was never meant to be the geulah ha'sheleimah — recasts the malchus as a defensible hora'as sha'ah. The tzivui-vs.-havtachah axis that emerges from this dispute recurs in the classical Rambam-Ramban disagreement on yishuv Eretz Yisrael, and the piece closes with the parallel question of why the yom tov that the Chashmonaim gave us has no maseches.

I. Framing the Question

The Rambam ends his entire Yad HaChazakah with Hilchos Melachim.[1] The structural choice is instructive: malchus is not a rhetorical figure for “leadership,” but a full parshah of halachos — with its own dinim, its own gedarim, and its own prerequisites for the establishment of a king. Throughout Tanach, two malchu’os alternated in Klal Yisrael:

  • Malchei Yehudah — descended from David HaMelech.
  • Malchei Yisrael — the breakaway northern kingdom.

Those who learn Maseches Sanhedrin are familiar with the numerous chilukim that follow between the two. But the Chashmonaim stand in neither column. They are Kohanim — Shevet Levi — and the Rambam at the head of Hilchos Chanukah writes that they seized the throne and ruled kol Yisrael for more than two centuries. How is that b'derech halachah possible? Yaakov Avinu in Parshas Vayechi[2] declared:

לֹא יָסוּר שֵׁבֶט מִיהוּדָה וּמְחֹקֵק מִבֵּין רַגְלָיו, עַד כִּי יָבֹא שִׁילֹה.
“The scepter shall not depart from Yehudah, nor the staff from between his feet, until Shiloh arrives.”
Bereishis 49:10

The malchus is not to depart from Shevet Yehudah; the mechokek — the lawgiver — must come from Yehudah. How, then, do Kohanim become melachim?

II. Historical Background — The Rise of Alexander of Macedon

The Beginning of Bayis Sheni

Roughly a generation after the Beis HaMikdash was rebuilt under Persian permission, an individual rose to power who would change the shape of the ancient world: Alexander of Macedon, son of Philip II. He ascended his father's throne in the year 336 BCE at the age of twenty, and by 332 BCE his armies had swept through Eretz Yisrael. The name entered the Jewish lexicon as a permanent Yiddishe nomen.[3]

Alexander's Education Under Aristotle

Alexander was a pampered child of the Macedonian ruler. His father hired the finest teachers of the age; chief among them was Aristotle. The two studied together for some eight years, beginning when Alexander was a boy of thirteen. The pairing of the great thinker with the great doer set a trajectory in divrei ha’yamim — and especially in Jewish history — from which Klal Yisrael would suffer greatly, until ultimately prevailing in the yom tov of Chanukah. Aristotle introduced his young pupil to Greek philosophy, the arts, and the whole architecture of Hellenistic ideology. They had their debates and their disagreements; but the imprint upon Alexander was profound and lasting.

Alexander's Conquests

Following his father's assassination — historians have long debated whether Alexander himself had a hand in it — the young king rapidly subdued South Asia, North Africa, the Mediterranean basin, the Middle East, and Eretz Yisrael, earning the title Alexander the Great: Alexander the Conqueror.

The Encounter with Shimon HaTzaddik

The Gemara in Yoma[4] records the famous account: when Alexander reached the border of Eretz Yisrael, Shimon HaTzaddik, then Kohen Gadol and leader of Klal Yisrael, went out to meet him. Alexander descended from his chariot and bowed. To his astonished soldiers and courtiers — who could not comprehend why the conqueror of nations would prostrate himself before a Jewish sage — he explained: whenever he went out to battle, he would see the demus of this very Shimon HaTzaddik before him, blessing him with victory.

The Ten Questions in Maseches Tamid

Alexander was not only a conqueror but a thinker. Talmud Bavli, Maseches Tamid,[5] preserves an entire daf of ten philosophical questions he put to the Chachmei Yisrael — fierce debates on philosophy and hashkafah. The chochmah of Macedon meets the chochmah of Chazal across a full page of the Talmud.

III. The Hellenistic Era and the Rise of the Misyavnim

What is “Hellenism”?

Hellas was the ancient name for Greece herself; Hellenists are those influenced by the Greeks. The Hellenistic era — some three centuries within the tekufah of Bayis Sheni — saw a major part of the civilized world fall under Greek empire and, more dangerously, Greek influence: in music, in architecture, in science and mathematics, in philosophy, in ideology. Architecture in particular was their special province, as the surviving monuments still attest.

Was It Good for the Jews?

Despite the warm encounter with Shimon HaTzaddik, the answer is nisht azoy gut. Alexander's declared policy was religious autonomy: every conquered nation retained its religion, paid its taxes, submitted to Greek political rule, and was introduced to Greek culture. (Aristotle himself dissented from this shitah, arguing for a more thoroughgoing Hellenization.) On the surface, Alexander was good for the Jews; Klal Yisrael even began naming children after him. But the cultural infiltration was relentless.

Gymnasia and the Rise of the Misyavnim

Many Jews were drawn — first by pressure, then willingly — into the gymnasia, the schools of Greek philosophy and physical culture. There they began to raise their children with Greek priorities and Greek hashkafos. The era of the Misyavnim — the Hellenized Jews — had begun.

Prushim and Tzedukim

The rabbinic Jews — the Prushim — fought to preserve the Torah, and especially Torah she’b’al peh, which at this time was not yet written down. Tanach alone was on the page; the rest was carried by the yeshivos and the rebbeim orally. Maintaining the mesorah was therefore a matter of survival.

Their opponents — the Tzedukim — trace to Tzaddok and Baisus, talmidim of Antignos Ish Socho,[6] whose followers misread their master's teaching on avdus mei’ahavah and broke from the mesorah. Alongside them stood the Beisusim. The Tzedukim rejected Torah she’b’al peh; the Prushim defended it.

Roughly a Century and a Half of Coexistence

For approximately the century and a half between Alexander's conquest and the accession of Antiochus IV, Yevanim, Misyavnim, and observant Jews lived in relative — if uneasy — peace. Schools and gymnasia operated; many Jews remained shomrei mitzvos. By the time we reach the days of the Chashmonaim, however, a different Greek dynasty has taken over: the Seleucids (Seleukidim). Under their rule, the pressure upon Jewish religious life began to intensify.

IV. Klal Yisrael Without Kings

Who was melech during all these generations? Nobody. Our last king was Tzidkiyahu HaMelech, dragged in shalsha’os shel barzel — chains of iron — to Bavel at the end of churban Bayis Rishon. After that, the malchus in Klal Yisrael ended.

Leadership devolved to the Kohanim Gedolim and to the Tana’im. This is the early tekufah of the Tana’im: Antignos Ish Socho — note the Greek name — at the head of Maseches Avos. Throughout this era a Sanhedrin of seventy or seventy-one dayanim functioned with a Nasi at its head. But there was no king.

No Navi → No Melech

The end of nevu’ah coincides with the beginning of Bayis Sheni: Chaggai, Zechariah, and Malachi are the last of the nevi’im. As the poskim note, one requires a navi to memaneh a king.[7] No prophet, no coronation. On its face this itself may seem sufficient to explain the absence of a melech throughout Bayis Sheni, and so also to complicate the Chashmonaim's claim to the throne. Yet the requirement is itself shanui b’machlokes among the Rishonim, and it does not by itself settle the deeper question that lies before us — for even a king appointed by prophecy would still have to belong to Shevet Yehudah if Yaakov Avinu's blessing is a binding tzivui. The absence of prophecy is a necessary condition of the whole conversation; the sufficient explanation lies elsewhere, and it is to that we return in Sections XIII–XV.

V. Antiochus Reverses Policy

The Wicked Antiochus IV

Antiochus IV — Antiochus HaRasha, “Antiochus the Wicked” of the Chanukah songs — was unlike his father, his uncle, and his grandfather, all of whom had been Greek emperors before him. He reversed the long-standing policy of religious autonomy. Where prior emperors had said: let them keep their Beis HaMikdash, their korbanos, their Sanhedrin, their laws — we will tax and govern them but leave their religion alone — Antiochus declared: I am going to get involved.

Corruption of the Kehunah Gedolah

Before the story of Chanukah, the Kohen Gadol held the highest position in Klal Yisrael: he ran the Beis HaMikdash, the spiritual epicenter of the people. But the Kohanim Gedolim of Bayis Sheni were largely not tzaddikim. Most had become deeply misyaven, deeply influenced by the Greeks. They cultivated political connections with local Greek governors and emperors, and — as recorded both in Chazal and in secular history — bribed their way into the Kehunah Gedolah, though they were neither bekiim in halachah nor particularly frum.

The Gemara in Yoma[8] records, in the context of the ketores in the Kodesh HaKodashim on Yom Kippur, that many such Kohanim Gedolim were Tzedukim — including the notorious case of the nisuch ha’mayim on Sukkos. They wanted the money, the power, the authority — and that was all.

The Tragic Cycle and Civil War

Historians have wondered how Kohanim Gedolim could bribe so heavily and fight so fiercely for an office in which many of them died during their first Yom Kippur avodah. The answer is that the cycle was constant: civil strife was the rule, not the exception, throughout most of the 420 years of Bayis Sheni. There was sibling rivalry among the Kohanim Gedolim, milchamos between Prushim and Tzedukim, and open conflict between shomrei mitzvos and the Misyavnim. Among Jews, two are enough for a good geshmak argument; in those years, the demographic minority of Jews in Eretz Yisrael compared with those in galus Bavel made the in-fighting all the more pronounced.

VI. The Sefarim Chitzonim and Megillas Antiochus

Certain books written in this era — the Sefarim Chitzonim, “external books” — sit outside the canon. They were never accepted into our sifrei kodesh, and some appear to have been authored by Misyavnim. Two are especially relevant here: Sefer HaMaccabim I & II.[9] We do not learn them as sifrei kodesh; their dates and details are debated. For general background of the era, however, they suffice.

A separate work — Megillas Antiochus, also called Megillas Bnei Chashmonai — is printed in many siddurim.[10] Tradition holds that it was written by Shammai and Hillel, transmitted directly from Mattisyahu ben Yochanan Kohen Gadol and his five sons; Rav Saadia Gaon was the first to print it in his siddur, attesting in his introduction that the mesorah traces back to Mattisyahu's family. It is a heavily abridged account of the events.

VII. Jason and Menelaus — Civil War in the Kehunah

Jason / Yeshua

The Kohen Gadol in the days approaching Antiochus was Jason — a Greek name. According to Yosef ben Matisyahu (Josephus Flavius),[11] his Hebrew name was Yeshua. There is an earlier Yeshua Kohen Gadol (without the heh) in Tanach; in our literature this later figure is called Yeshua or Jason. He had connections with the local Greek governor.

Menelaus's Coup

Jason's rival, Menelaus — a Jew and, according to Josephus's account, a brother of Jason and himself a Kohen — coveted the Kehunah Gedolah for himself. (The sources here diverge: Josephus makes Menelaus Jason's brother, while Sefer HaMaccabim II identifies him differently and disputes his priestly credentials.[12]) When a rumor spread that Antiochus had been killed in battle in Africa, Menelaus saw his moment: he took a thousand soldiers — a thousand fellow Jews — and attacked. Chaos overtook Yerushalayim: who was the legitimate Kohen Gadol? Even the rabbinic Jews who came to the Beis HaMikdash with their korbanos did not know.

VIII. Antiochus's Rage and the Desecration

The rumor was false. Antiochus was alive — and enraged. He returned in haste to Eretz Yisrael and plunged into local Jewish politics in a way no Greek emperor before him had ever attempted. He determined who would serve as Kohen Gadol; eventually he assumed control of the Beis HaMikdash himself.

The Pig in the Kodesh HaKodashim

The first act of the desecration was to bring a pig — a davar acher — into our holy Temple, slaughter it there, and sprinkle its blood in the Kodesh HaKodashim and on the mizbe’ach outside. He forced Kohanim to consume the flesh; those who refused were shechted on the spot. He moved through Yerushalayim in a boiling rage, killing anyone identified as a rabbinic Jew — the various accounts give figures on the order of tens of thousands slain, with many more exiled.[13] From this point, the milchamos with the Greeks turned existential.

The Gezeiros

The gezeiros Antiochus enacted focused upon milah, Shabbos, and Rosh Chodesh — three mitzvos the Misyavnim already targeted. The particular chochmah in singling out milah: the Hellenists worshipped the human body. They were narcissists; they had no bushah — none of the bayshanim, rachmanim, gomlei chasadim that the Gemara in Beitzah[14] identifies as the three defining simanim of Klal Yisrael. In their gymnasia, everyone trained naked. They could therefore see at a glance who had a bris — and put him to death.

The Rambam's Atypical Aside

The Rambam at the head of Hilchos Chanukah[15] does something almost unparalleled elsewhere in the Yad: he narrates the history. Not the halachos first, but the events. He does not enumerate each gezeirah, but he paints the picture of the depth of the crisis under malchus Yavan. The Rambam writing divrei ha’yamim at the head of a halachah is itself a limmud upon the chashivus of the events for the halachah that follows.

IX. Mattisyahu — The Unlikely Hero

The Irony of Yochanan Kohen Gadol

Mattisyahu ben Yochanan Kohen Gadol witnessed the destruction in Yerushalayim. He was a rabbinic Jew, a parush. The most piercing detail of his biography: his father, Yochanan Kohen Gadol, was in the end a Tzeduki. The Gemara in Berachos[16] records that for eighty years Yochanan was the most venerated Jew of his generation — and at the close of his life turned Tzeduki. From this the Mishnah in Avos[17] derives:

אַל תַּאֲמִין בְּעַצְמְךָ עַד יוֹם מוֹתְךָ.
“Do not trust in yourself until the day of your death.”
Avos 2:4

And the wonder is that Yochanan the Tzeduki produced Mattisyahu and the five sons whom we honor in davening as tzaddikim, kedoshim, u’tehorim. Something turned. That the same tree bore both the collapse at the end of eighty years and the flowering of the rebellion is itself among the quiet miracles of the era.

Modi’in

Mattisyahu fled to Modi’in — still today a place in Eretz Yisrael, near Kfar HaChashmonaim. Antiochus's people had set up altars to Zeus there, and Antiochus decreed public korbanos to Zeus, compelling the local Jews to attend. Mattisyahu approached the altar, drew a sword, and struck down the Misyaven who was bringing the offering — a Jew who simply did not care. With his sons he fled into the forests around Modi’in, where they hid for a time.

The Rebellion in the Forests

There they gathered a small army — me’atim b’yad rabim, as we say in davening. Perhaps a few hundred men. Against the mighty Greek army it was, by every standard of ancient warfare, ludicrous. And precisely in that lay its beauty.

X. The Rebellion and the Falls of Elazar and Yehudah

Strategy

Each son took a band of men to a different region of Eretz Yisrael and fought every local governor of Antiochus in turn.

Elazar and the Elephants

Antiochus, having campaigned to India, to North Africa, and eastward, had returned with armored elephants — the tanks of the ancient world. Elazar, son of Mattisyahu, was a gibor: in one battle he ran between the elephants and killed them from below the belly with a spear. Megillas Antiochus describes a gruesome end — from the mass of tzo’ah, the toxic excrement of so many elephants, Elazar drowned. The Megillah here narrates from within the tradition; the details are grim.

The Origin of the Name “Maccabi”

Of Yehudah — the strongest of the brothers — the popular pshat takes Maccabi as an acronym for Mi Kamocha Ba’eilim Hashem. Nevertheless the simple pshat is otherwise: Maccabi is an acronym for Mattisyahu Ben Yochanan Kohen — מ.ב.י.כ. — the family name. The popular reading is beautiful and speaks to the heart; but the historical pshat is the family name.

Mattisyahu's Charge

When Yehudah fell, his brothers returned to their father with the question: Yehudah was the strongest. He was the eldest. What do we do now? Mattisyahu — already an old man — took up his sword:

“I will fight in place of mourning my son's death. Now is the time to fight. A Jew must be proud to fight for the truth.”

He ran with his sons to the battlefield. Victory followed victory; the small Judean state outlasted, upon the field, one of the mightiest empires of the ancient world.

Antiochus the Fugitive

The mesorah in Megillas Antiochus gives an account of Antiochus's end at variance with the ancient historians. According to the Megillah, as his army was slaughtered wave upon wave, Antiochus boarded a ship and fled; the Jews called him Antiochus HaBore’ach, “Antiochus the Fugitive,” and a bushah was set upon his head. The Greek historians (Polybius; Sefer HaMaccabim I & II) record instead that Antiochus died of illness on the Persian campaign in Elam.[18] Either way, his empire fell from him, and other Greek dynasts inherited what he had lost.

XI. The Story of Chanukah and the Menorah

The story of Chanukah itself may be treated briefly. They won the battles — k’mo she’omrim b’davening b’al ha’nissim. They found a pach shemen. They lit the menorah.

Why the Menorah?

Why does the miracle of the menorah take center stage? Because with that lighting they reinstituted avodas ha’mikdash. For a long stretch, no one could enter the Beis HaMikdash: a temple to Zeus stood within it; Antiochus had brought korbanos to avodah zarah upon our own mizbe’ach. Now, at last, they returned to the Beis HaMikdash; they reclaimed it; it was theirs. The nes of the menorah stands in for the deeper inyan: this is chanukas ha’mizbe’ach — the rededication of the mizbe’ach and of the Beis HaMikdash. (The relation between the Al Ha’Nissim account, which emphasizes the battles, and the Gemara's account, which emphasizes the pach shemen, is a sugya of its own; here we take them as complementary — the outer victory and the inner rededication as one nes with two faces.)

XII. Malchus Beis Chashmonai — The Great Question Returns

The Rambam's Striking Halachah

The Rambam at the beginning of Hilchos Chanukah writes — in a manner he adopts in almost no other halachah — that the Chashmonaim seized the throne and became kings over kol Yisrael. For centuries there had been no real kings, only Sanhedrin, dayanim, Tana’im, chachamim, yeshivos. And now malchus Beis Chashmonai decided: we are going to be kings. This is the point at which the framing kushya of the entire shiur bites: Kohanim, who — measured against Yaakov Avinu's blessing — have no business being kings. (The Rambam's own nusach here is one to which we shall return in Section XV; for the moment we let the difficulty stand in its sharpest form.)

The Two-Hundred-Year Dynasty

For roughly the next two centuries of Bayis Sheni, the Chashmonaim ruled. The line runs, in outline:

  • First — the sons of Mattisyahu: Shimon, and Yochanan.
  • Then — Alexander Jannaeus (Yannai).
  • Then — Aristobulus.
  • And other descendants — many bearing Greek names.

It began nobly: they were k’doshim; we still call them al yedei kohanecha ha’kedoshim in Al Ha’Nissim. It descended, over the generations, into sibling rivalry within the Jewish royal court, and into constant strife through the rest of Bayis Sheni.

XIII. The Ramban — The Question Is the Answer

The Ramban on Vayechi

The Ramban in Parshas Vayechi[19] asks the same question: how did the Chashmonaim take the throne for themselves?

His answer is severe: the question is the answer. It was wrong. It was a sin. It was a crime. The Ramban — a thousand years after the events — has the moral koach to say it. Lo yasur shevet mi’Yehudah is a tzivui from Yaakov Avinu; the Chashmonaim, though tzaddikim kedoshim u’tehorim, sat upon a throne that was not theirs.

The Anticipated Question — Shaul HaMelech

One might immediately protest: but Shaul HaMelech was the first melech, and he was of Shevet Binyamin. The Ramban's reading holds: Shaul preceded the establishment of Malchus Beis David. Once David HaMelech's dynasty was selected, Lo yasur shevet mi’Yehudah applied l’doros. The very availability of Shaul under this reading concedes, however, that malchus from another shevet is not l’chatchilah impossible — a concession that will become important below when the alternative reading of Rav Yaakov presses in the same direction from the other side.

The Punishment — Hordus

This is why, says the Ramban, these tzaddikim who saved Klal Yisrael, who bequeathed us the nes of Chanukah — suffered so terribly during their reign. Hordus killed them all. The Gemara at the head of Maseches Bava Basra[20] states it plainly. Hordus had been a slave of the Chashmonaim; he rebelled and slaughtered them.

Was this mikreh — coincidence? No, says the Ramban. You take what is not yours, you lose what you have. They desired a throne beyond what belonged to them; being Kohanim was not enough for them; for that, Hashem caused them to be wiped out.

The Caveat About Shimon

The Ramban adds, almost in three words, a caveat: Shimon, one of the sons of Mattisyahu, was a shtikl Tzeduki — somewhat influenced by the Tzedukim. We praise the brothers together as tzaddikim kedoshim; the Ramban hints that there was an additional cheit in Shimon's line. The Rishonim who examined this have records of Shimon's connection with the Tzedukim: perhaps not a Tzeduki himself, but influenced — and his descendants therefore deviated. Whether the Ramban's ikar cheit is the malchus and Shimon's slide a secondary aggravation, or whether the Shimon line's specific cheit is what triggered the destruction upon the whole house, is a question that turns on the reader's ear for the lashon; both readings have been held.

The Last Chashmona’is

The Gemara records that Hordus killed them all — except for one girl, who survived. She climbed to the roof; just before casting herself down, she cried out:

כָּל דְּאָמַר «מִבֵּית חַשְׁמוֹנַאי קָאָתִינָא», עַבְדָּא הוּא.
“Anyone who says, ‘I come from the House of the Chashmonaim’ — he is a slave [i.e., of Hordus, and not of the Chashmona’i line].”
Bava Basra 3b

Hordus, the mad tyrant, preserved her body in honey for seven years; the Gemara records a machlokes as to what he did with it thereafter (v’ein kan ha’makom le’ha’arich).

XIV. The Sanhedrin's Silence — Rav Yaakov Kamenetsky in Emes L’Yaakov

The Real Question

If the Chashmonaim's malchus was a cheit, why did the Sanhedrin remain silent? The Sanhedrin functioned and operated in full force throughout this era. They were enacting halachos, gezeiros, takanos, siyagim. The entire body of Mishnayos across the shishah sidrei mishnah was generated during this tekufah — to be compiled centuries later by Rabbeinu HaKadosh, but generated here. A massive halachic body — and not a single mecha’ah preserved?

Rav Yaakov Kamenetsky

Rav Yaakov Kamenetsky in his sefer Emes L’Yaakov[21] — one of the most illuminating and somewhat under-appreciated sefarim of recent decades, embracing peirushim on Torah, on the Rambam, and on Maseches Avos — supplies a yesod that reframes not only this question but the whole shape of Bayis Sheni. Rooted in the Slabodka world and baki in Tanach and Jewish history as few in his generation were, he reads the era structurally.

The Yesod — Bayis Sheni Was Never Meant to Be a Geulah Sheleimah

Once Nevuchadnetzar exiled us to Bavel, says Rav Yaakov, it was over. We returned; we received permission to build a Beis HaMikdash — but only as a tekufah arai, an interim period. Chazal understood that the Yiddishe structure of self-rule in Eretz Yisrael would not again be what it had been until Mashiach arrives.

The signs are laid out in the Gemara's list of differences between the two Batei Mikdash:

  • No Aron
  • No Keruvim
  • No Hashra’as ha’Shechinah in the same measure

Many of the giluyim that defined Bayis Rishon simply did not exist in Bayis Sheni.

Therefore — No Plans for a Melech

Chazal at the time were not planning to appoint a king. They knew this geulah was interim; they knew we would yet return to galus until “ki yavo Shiloh”; they knew Mashiach would bring the tikkun ha’shaleim. They feared that establishing a true malchus would cause Klal Yisrael to mistake the moment for the geulah ha’sheleimah — to think it was over, the work was done, the days of David restored. So they davka refrained.

Why So Many Gezeiros and Takanos in Bayis Sheni?

Rav Yaakov presses the reading further. Why is it specifically in Bayis Sheni that Chazal generated the vast bulk of the gezeiros and takanos? Because Chazal knew this tekufah would last 420 years — long, but not forever. They knew Klal Yisrael was on the way out, into a galus of a thousand years and more. The Jews would need fortification for what was coming. They needed siyagim, gederim, and gezeiros — protections that are not d’oraisa but d’rabbanan. This is why davka in Bayis Sheni, the Chashmonaim themselves, the Kohanim Gedolim, the Sanhedrin, and the chachamim all multiplied gezeiros and takanos. Three otherwise-loose facts — no king, a proliferation of takanos, the Sanhedrin's silence — reduce to one explanatory arc.

The Chachamim's Silence Explained

And so when the Chashmonaim assumed the throne — the chachamim did not protest. They did not even hold that there should be a king in this tekufah. They wanted the status quo: Sanhedrin, dayanim, chachamim, and that alone. This was not a time for malchus.

Why then did the Chashmonaim seize it? Because they felt: “Im lo achshav, eimasai?” — if not now, when? If we do not consolidate power, all the battles of the past decade will lose their effect. The unity that binds Klal Yisrael together against the Misyavnim will dissolve. Someone must run the malchus and the Beis HaMikdash simultaneously. Therefore, says Rav Yaakov, it was not so grave a cheit. Perhaps they should have declared their reign expressly as hora’as sha’ah — a temporary malchus for the needs of the hour, without pretense of the malchus l’olam reserved for Beis David.

And Zerubavel?

The reading is confirmed by another line altogether. Zerubavel, in Ezra and Nechemiah, was of Beis David and was to have served as king. He did not. For a temporary kingdom in the spirit of a hora’as sha’ah, then, even another shevet can serve. Lo yasur shevet mi’Yehudah, in this view, is a statement about malchus l’olam — the eternal kingship — not about every interim arrangement. And that, says Rav Yaakov, is exactly what happened with the Chashmonaim.

A Note on the Mechanics

The category of hora’as sha’ah malchus that Rav Yaakov quietly deploys carries real weight. Whether such a king carries the standard dinim of Melech Yisrael — moredin, bidud kavod, arba’ah amos, transmission to his son, the parshah of Ha’Melech — is a discussion of its own, and lies beyond the scope of the present sugya. What is essential here is only the meta-halachic claim: there exists a category of malchus less than the malchus l’olam of Beis David, which can be assumed l’sha’ah by another shevet when the needs of the hour compel it. That is the door through which the Chashmonaim's reign passes, in the reading of Rav Yaakov.

XV. The Rambam vs. the Ramban — Tzivui or Havtachah?

The Rambam's Actual Lashon

If one returns to the Rambam in Hilchos Chanukah with this reading in mind, the lashon itself begins to speak. The Rambam writes:

וְגָבְרוּ בְּנֵי חַשְׁמוֹנַאי הַכֹּהֲנִים הַגְּדוֹלִים... וְחָזְרָה מַלְכוּת לְיִשְׂרָאֵל יֶתֶר עַל מָאתַיִם שָׁנָה.
“And the sons of the House of Chashmonai, the great Kohanim, prevailed... and the kingship returned to Israel for more than two hundred years.”
Rambam, Hilchos Chanukah 3:1

The verbs are the language of praise, not of narration. Gavru, gedolim, chazrah malchus le’Yisrael — the kingdom returned. We were no longer under Greeks, Babylonians, or Persians. A king of our own — but only temporarily.

The Underlying Machlokes

Here lies the foundational machlokes:

  • The Ramban — “Lo yasur shevet mi’Yehudah” is a tzivui, a command of Yaakov Avinu to his descendants. Take the throne if you are not of Beis David, and you have transgressed the tzivui of your zaken.
  • The Rambam — “Lo yasur” is a havtachah, a promise. For all eternity Malchus Beis David will rule. But this leaves room for hora’as sha’ah kings from other shevatim during the intervening ages.

A Parallel Machlokes — Yishuv Eretz Yisrael

The very same axis reappears between the same two Rishonim in a wholly different sugya: yishuv Eretz Yisrael.

  • The Ramban: “V’horashtem osah v’yeshavtem bah”[22] — it is a mitzvah d’oraisa, binding in every generation.[23]
  • The Rambam: it is not counted among the taryag mitzvos — it is a havtachah.

One may paskin as he wishes; many Rishonim hold with the Ramban, and in our own generation the ruling is certainly that it is a mitzvah. This is not a matter of politics but of halachah. The same machlokes that decides whether the Chashmonaim sinned in taking the throne decides also whether one who lives in Chutz La’Aretz thereby refrains from a mitzvah. Tzivui or havtachah — the axis is one.

On the Combination of Keser Kehunah and Keser Malchus

An adjacent question deserves at least a mention. The Torah keeps three kesarim — Kehunah, Torah, and Malchus — largely separate; and a serving Kohen upon the throne raises a difficulty independent of the Yehudah question. Even were hora’as sha’ah malchus from another shevet fully licit in principle, one might still ask whether the very family of the Kohanim Gedolim is the right family to receive it. The Ramban's severity may be reading not only from Lo yasur shevet mi’Yehudah alone but from the additional pressure of the kehunah/malchus combination; and this may be a further part of what he means when he writes that the destruction of the line was midah k’neged midah.

XVI. Why Is There No Maseches Chanukah?

If the Chashmonaim's malchus was itself contested in principle, one might ask why the yom tov they gave us has no maseches at all. Rabbeinu HaKadosh gave us a maseches for nearly every yom tov — Megillah, Yoma, Rosh HaShanah, Sukkah. Why no Maseches Chanukah?

The Chasam Sofer's Reported Explanation

The Chasam Sofer's Sefer Chut Ha’Meshulash[24] — compiled by his grandson — records an explanation, though not from the Chasam Sofer's own pen. The Chasam Sofer wrote more than nearly any other rav in his generation, and this particular thing he did not write down; the grandson's testimony alone is our source, which should be weighed accordingly. The reported statement: Rabbeinu HaKadosh came from Zera Beis David; he was the Nasi, the Reish Galusa having his seat in Bavel.[25] Allegedly, Rabbeinu HaKadosh took the Chashmonaim's seizure of the throne as a family injury — “You Chashmonaim dared take away our throne? I will not write a maseches about your yom tov.”

Read on its face the statement is deeply hard: Rabbeinu HaKadosh lit the menorah as we light the menorah. Other rabbanim who quote this Chasam Sofer reframe it accordingly: Rabbeinu HaKadosh did not withhold the maseches vindictively but l’sheim shamayim — a withholding b’cheshbon, because our aveiros caused the Chashmonaim not to be zocheh to a maseches. Even so, treated as oral tradition, the account is best held at some distance.

The Vilna Gaon — The Lost Maseches

A second tradition — the more compelling — comes from the Vilna Gaon. His son writes in his father's name that there once was a Maseches Chanukah, and we lost it.[26] Many masechtos were lost across the length of galus.

Rav Hai Gaon records a startling episode: one day in the yeshivah in Bavel, an old man — zaken v’chacham v’yashish ba’yeshivah — appeared with a long white beard, took out an entire maseches that they had never before seen, and read from it. Rav Hai Gaon begged him: “Stay! Let me copy this maseches! We have all shishah sidrei mishnah — but we are missing this one!” The man said, “I must go,” and departed. Rav Hai Gaon lamented that he had lost that maseches forever.

The Gaon's point was broader still. We have a Maseches Mezuzah; what about tefillin? Where is the dedicated maseches? The list of losses, said the Gaon, is one of the great tragadyos of galus.

The Sefas Emes — A Chassidic Reading

The Sefas Emes[27] takes the absence of Maseches Chanukah and makes a simchah of it. The chiddush — an emblematic move of chassidus: Chanukah is entirely Torah she’b’al peh. That is precisely why it remains b’al peh — there is no written tradition because the very essence of Chanukah is the unwritten chelek of Torah. The absence is not an accident of galus but a formal siman of the yom tov's own chelek in Torah. The gedolei chassidus elaborate the yesod in many directions.

XVII. Closing

We began with a question so hard that the whole shape of Bayis Sheni had to be traversed to answer it. Kohanim ascended a throne that Yaakov Avinu's blessing reserved for the descendants of Yehudah. And the Rishonim, reading the same event, opened two paths.

For the Ramban, Lo yasur shevet mi’Yehudah is a tzivui. The Chashmonaim, though tzaddikim kedoshim u’tehorim, took what was not theirs; the destruction of their house by Hordus is the measured accounting of that cheit. For Rav Yaakov Kamenetsky — reading with the Rambam's nusach in Hilchos Chanukah at his ear — the pasuk is a havtachah: Bayis Sheni was never meant to be the geulah ha’sheleimah; the chachamim's silence about the coronation is the tell; the massive fortification of gezeiros and takanos in the tekufah is the preparation; and the Chashmonaim's malchus is a defensible hora’as sha’ah. The axis between them — tzivui or havtachah — is the same axis that divides the two Rishonim on yishuv Eretz Yisrael, and it is the deeper structural inheritance of the whole discussion.

And the mussar the Ramban draws does not depend on which side of the machlokes one takes. Even great tzaddikim — even the kedoshim u’tehorim whose blood was shed al kiddush Hashem, whose nes we still light every winter — even they must know the limit of what belongs to them. When the crown that Yaakov Avinu placed upon the head of Yehudah is not l’chatchilah one's own, one may perhaps wear it l’sha’ah for the needs of the hour; but the wearing of it as if it were one's own carries a cheshbon. The Chashmona’is on the rooftop, crying out that no future claimant to her family is anything but an eved, is the closing image of that cheshbon. And precisely against that image the nes of the menorah — the reclamation of the mizbe’ach, the rededication of avodah — shines its clean and unmixed light.
