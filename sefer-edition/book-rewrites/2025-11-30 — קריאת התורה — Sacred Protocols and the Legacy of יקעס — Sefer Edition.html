קריאת התורה — בריך שמיה וירושת היקעס

Sacred Protocols and the Legacy of the Yekkes (יקעס)
Studies in the Tefillah (תפילה) Series — Brich Shmei and the Torah Service
10 Kislev 5786  ·  Sefer Edition

This discourse is dedicated לעילוי נשמת the lecturer’s grandfather, זכר צדיק לברכה, and to his great-grandfather Rav Asher Anshel, הי״ד, who was murdered in the Holocaust. [Names and wording to be confirmed with the Rav before printing.]

The lecturer opens with a personal account of a forgotten yahrzeit (יארצייט) and then takes up a question left over from the previous week: why do the Jews of Frankfurt not say Brich Shmei (בריך שמיה) when the ark is opened? The answer is a history. It runs from the two Geiger brothers and Rav Hirsch, through the long Ashkenazic suspicion of kabbalah (קבלה) after Shabsai Tzvi, to the Baal Shem Tov and the change in our own generation; it then weighs the two difficult phrases of the passage, בר אלהין and אנא עבדא, and the Chofetz Chaim’s private practice. The second half goes through the Torah service itself: the six words of גדלו לה׳ אתי and the six steps of the Ark, escorting the scroll, the Arizal’s gazing at the letters, why the Ashkenazim lift the scroll after the reading, an uncommon honor, the pesichah (פתיחה) of an expectant father, silence while the scroll is carried, the call הכל הבו גודל לאלהינו, and a proposal to divide the honors.

I. A Preface: Providence and a Yahrzeit

The discourse opens with a personal account, which the lecturer gives as his reason for sponsoring the morning’s breakfast and the discourse itself. His father had passed away some months earlier,[the recording is unclear here; see Open Questions, 2] and his father’s own yahrzeit (יארצייט) would fall shortly after Pesach (פסח); his paternal grandfather’s yahrzeit falls on the seventh of Kislev (כסלו). The lecturer was close to his grandfather, whom he counts himself a disciple (talmid (תלמיד)), and had spoken of him to the congregation on several occasions. Every year his father had telephoned him: “Light a candle for Saba (סבא). You were his talmid; learn a little from his sefer (ספר).” This year, for the first time, no call came, and the lecturer forgot the day entirely, until an hour before shkiah (שקיעה).

The Grandfather

The grandfather, born Weiss, was a great talmid chacham (תלמיד חכם). In his native land he studied in the Satmar yeshivah (ישיבה), where he was counted among its best students and learned in chavrusa (חברותא) with the Satmar Rav, the author of the Divrei Yoel (דברי יואל), with whom he prepared a shiur (שיעור). He then moved to Eretz Yisrael (ארץ ישראל), where he Hebraicized his name to Ephrati, changed his outlook (hashkafah (השקפה)), and became a professor of Talmud (תלמוד) at Bar-Ilan University; yet, the lecturer says, he always held himself a disciple of the Satmar Rav. The lecturer’s father went another way, to the Ponevezh yeshivah, and became a “burning” (farbrenter (פארברענטער)) fervent ben Torah (בן תורה). The lecturer recalls, as a child, attending Yom HaAtzmaut (יום העצמאות) services with his grandfather in the latter’s synagogue in Petach Tikva: a world very different from the one his father chose, yet a world of Torah (תורה) and yiras shamayim (יראת שמים) no less.

From the age of eighteen to twenty-one, during three years in Eretz Yisrael, the lecturer visited his grandfather nearly every Shabbos (שבת) and learned with him; before that, as a young child and as a boy, he had spent months in his home. The grandfather’s books, whose titles the lecturer gives as ועמודים לעמודים and תלמוד לתלמידים, are not popular, for he had a different method of study: he approached the Gemara (גמרא) as a researcher (mechkar (מחקר)), attending to variant readings (girsaos (גרסאות)) and texts (nuschaos (נוסחאות)) and to the identity of each authority, rather than in the manner of the yeshivos (ישיבות). He was, the lecturer adds, the trailblazer who gave the religious high schools (tichon (תיכון)) of Eretz Yisrael, in which students learn sacred and secular subjects together, a system for teaching Gemara.

The Reminder

An hour before shkiah on the day, a man whom the lecturer had never met came to his door. He had been sent by Rabbi Meyer Travis of the community’s adopted kollel (כולל) in Eretz Yisrael, and carried a newly published sefer for the beis medrash (בית מדרש). Rabbi Travis’s book is a controversial work on contemporary questions of Even HaEzer (אבן העזר), including some that touch on transgender issues; he had told the lecturer to expect that he would be excommunicated for it, and several brave rabbis, among them the community’s late rav (רב), had given their approbations (haskamos (הסכמות)). The messenger had met Rabbi Travis once, on an airplane ten years earlier. As he was leaving he asked: “You are Avishai Efrati’s son?” He had prayed with the lecturer’s father at the early (vasikin (ותיקין)) minyan (מנין) every Shabbos morning, and as the gabbai (גבאי) of that minyan, who kept the yahrzeit lists, he asked: “Isn’t today your grandfather’s yahrzeit?” The lecturer had forgotten. There was time to light a candle and to learn in his memory. “People always have stories of hashgachah pratis (השגחה פרטית),” he says; “I am always jealous that they happen to others. Thank God I have one to share.”

The discourse is dedicated to the memory of the grandfather, and of his father, Rav Asher Anshel (רב אשר אנשיל), השם ינקום דמו, the lecturer’s great-grandfather, who was murdered in the Holocaust, “a holy Jew, a distinguished Jew”; the lecturer stood at the crematoria two years earlier and felt connected to him.

II. Brich Shmei (בריך שמיה) and the Question of Frankfurt

The Question

The preceding discourse began the study of Brich Shmei, the passage recited as the aron kodesh (ארון קודש) is opened. Afterwards a member of the congregation observed, to the lecturer’s embarrassment, that in Frankfurt they did not say it at all; the lecturer investigated, and this discourse gives the result.

When Is It Said?

As noted the week before, some nuschaos say Brich Shmei only on Shabbos, Yom Tov (יום טוב) and Rosh Chodesh (ראש חודש). The reason is in its own words, בריך כתריך ואתרך, “blessed be Your crown”: the holiness of the keser (כתר), the crown, is invoked in the Kedushah (קדושה) only when one says כתר יתנו לך, and that is on those days. The Ashkenazic practice is to say Brich Shmei every time the aron (ארון) is opened, because, the lecturer says, the plain sense (pshat (פשט)) of the Zohar (זוהר) calls for its daily recital.

A Recent Addition to the Prayers

Brich Shmei is a quotation from the Zohar.[1] As the lecturer has said many times, much of the order of prayer (seder hatefillah (סדר התפילה)) derives from the Arizal (אריז״ל), even among those who do not regard themselves as students of kabbalah (קבלה); one could say that one has no connection to kabbalah and does nothing in accord with the Ari (אר״י), and still much of the siddur (סידור) would be his. The Zohar was known only to a select few (yechidei segulah (יחידי סגולה)) until it was first printed, in 1557 according to the lecturer, in Constantinople. Because the Arizal said Brich Shmei and others saw him say it, it spread in the Ashkenazic communities and throughout Europe and among the Sephardim. The prayer books printed from the early 1600s all contain it; the lecturer marvels that within a few decades it had entered the liturgy everywhere. By the mid-1600s it was standard practice. Except in Frankfurt.

III. The Divrei Koheles (דברי קהלת) and the Two Geiger Brothers

Frankfurt and Its Customs

Frankfurt was the bastion of German-Jewish (Yekkishe (יקעס)) custom, the central community from which the customs of German Jewry went out. A collection of its customs, called Divrei Koheles, was compiled by Rav Shlomo Zalman Geiger, a judge (dayan (דיין)) in Frankfurt for many years, who belonged to an established German rabbinic family; the lecturer places him in the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries.[2] He compiled it because he considered it essential that the next generation know its true, authentic customs. In it he writes plainly: אין אומרים בריך שמיה בפרנקפורט, “in Frankfurt we do not say Brich Shmei.” The reason: it comes from the Zohar, and in Frankfurt they stayed far from Zohar.

The Brother

The family stands for one of the sharpest divisions in German-Jewish history. Rav Shlomo Zalman had a brother, Abraham Geiger, the Dr. Abraham Geiger of the Reform movement; the two were sons of rabbis from a rabbinic family. Abraham Geiger was a brilliant talmid chacham who went to the University and began to apply Biblical criticism to the Torah. He deleted from the prayer book the petition ותחזינה עינינו בשובך לציון ברחמים, saying that Jerusalem was over and that Germany was Jerusalem. The Meshech Chochmah (משך חכמה) writes at the end of Parashas Bechukosai (פרשת בחקתי) that the Reform claimed Berlin as their Jerusalem.[3] The lecturer draws the lesson for his own community: Jerusalem, in the future when Mashiach (משיח) comes and the Beis HaMikdash (בית המקדש) is rebuilt, is the place of the ideal Jewish life; no diaspora city is Jerusalem; and “Torah goes out from Zion” (כי מציון תצא תורה).

This Abraham Geiger had been, at the University of Bonn, a chavrusa of Rav Shimshon Raphael Hirsch. Hirsch saw that his brilliant friend was moving to the left and tried to draw him back to the right; they remained close for a time, but at the end of their lives they fought bitterly. The lecturer will not recommend Geiger’s writings: “you should not have the book in your home.” His brother Rav Shlomo Zalman remained a traditional Jew, faithful to the customs of Frankfurt.

IV. Rav Hirsch and the Future of German Jewry

The Vision

A great controversy separated Rav Shimshon Raphael Hirsch from other German rabbis, among them Rav Geiger and Rav Bamberger, the Rav of Würzburg, over what German Jewry was to become. Hirsch saw that the Reform movement was tearing the soul from German Jewry: the organs introduced into synagogues, the altering of the liturgy and of halachah (הלכה), the assimilation. The Jews of Germany were going to universities and, as the verse says, ויתערבו בגוים וילמדו מעשיהם, “they mingled with the nations and learned their ways”; the more one has to do with the nations, the more they influence one’s Judaism. Hirsch’s answer was his doctrine of Torah im derech eretz (תורה עם דרך ארץ), for which he is often blamed, but by which he restored Jewish life to the German communities and made them more observant.

Among his innovations was the dancing of hakafos (הקפות) with the sefer Torah (ספר תורה) on Simchas Torah (שמחת תורה), which, the lecturer says, had not been done in Germany. Geiger and Bamberger, who preferred their established customs and distrusted anything new, even from good motives, were deeply troubled; to dance with a sefer Torah they thought a presumption. To this day, the lecturer notes, some communities will not do it; as to the congregation of Hirsch in Washington Heights, he assumes it follows Hirsch.

Secession from the Community

When the German government first allowed Jews to secede from the unified community, Hirsch decided that the time had come to separate completely from the Reform. He issued a proclamation (kol korei (קול קורא)) that every family must secede and not remain together with them, and founded the congregation Kehal Adas Yeshurun (קהל עדת ישורון), known as KAJ. Bamberger, Geiger and others opposed him. The Reform were then “part and parcel of the community,” a spectrum from the somewhat left to the somewhat right, “one big happy family,” and the objectors said: “Why do this? It will be a destruction for generations!” Hirsch was at first unsuccessful: only eighty families followed him, according to the historians as the lecturer reports. We speak of Hirsch as the leader of German Jewry, yet the truly observant Jews of his movement were that few; most of the rest assimilated, and many perished in the war. The Reform, the lecturer adds, are “gone, assimilated.” Yet German Jewry still carries Hirsch as its hero and its great man, and his influence on all of Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל) is immeasurable. His writings are read by the world, and the lecturer calls the Hirsch Chumash (חומש) the most beautiful book on the weekly portion; its recent five-volume English edition is rich in grammar and in the meaning of words.

V. The Yekkes and Kabbalah: Why Did We Begin to Say It?

The Real Question

The lecturer turns the question around. Why did Frankfurt not say Brich Shmei? The question is rather why we did. For a serious student of Jewish history it is necessary to understand the attitude of the Ashkenazim, those whose ancestors came from Europe, toward kabbalah, the “hidden wisdom” (chochmas hanistar (חכמת הנסתר)): they frowned upon its study and upon living by it, even though today the Arizal is in nearly all our prayers.

Shabsai Tzvi

The reason was Shabsai Tzvi in the seventeenth century. He rose up and declared that the secret of Judaism was not in Talmud and the poskim (פוסקים) but in kabbalah and the Zohar, and he led one of the most catastrophic false-messianic movements in Jewish history. He was one who “reveals aspects of the Torah not in accordance with halachah” (מגלה פנים בתורה שלא כהלכה), of whom the Sages say in the chapter Perek Chelek (פרק חלק) of Sanhedrin (סנהדרין) that he has no share in the World to Come;[4] to take the Torah and misinterpret it for one’s own benefit is to take the holy and make it profane. He ended by converting to Islam. After this, the leaders of the generation were prepared to remove the books of kabbalah and Zohar from the shelves as dangerous to Klal Yisrael, and the trauma created a deep distrust of kabbalah in Ashkenaz, especially among the Yekkes, who associated it with danger, with deviation from the solid ground of Gemara and halachah.

The Ban in Amsterdam

The lecturer cites as an example the ban (cherem (חרם)) issued in Amsterdam: whoever owned the Shelah HaKadosh (של״ה הקדוש), Shnei Luchos HaBris (שני לוחות הברית), a work we quote constantly today, or other books of kabbalah and Zohar, was to bring it to the community within three days to be buried (geniza (גניזה)). The decree is recorded in the communal ledger (pinkas hakehillah (פנקס הקהילה)), the notebook in which every community, as a whole genre of Jewish literature, set down its customs and decisions. Such was the fear of the influence of kabbalah. [The lecturer gives no date; see Open Questions.]

The Chasam Sofer (חתם סופר) and the Maharsha (מהרש״א)

The Chasam Sofer writes in a responsum — the lecturer calls it astonishing — that he does not approve of people learning kabbalah, Zohar in particular, and that he never heard a word about the holy Zohar from any of his three great teachers, the Haflaah (הפלאה), Rav Nosson Adler and Rav Zalman Chasid.[5] Perhaps they learned it in private; Rav Nosson Adler was a great kabbalist, but none taught it in public, because they understood the danger. One scarcely finds the Chasam Sofer citing the Zohar.

The danger was felt before Shabsai Tzvi. The lecturer reports that the Maharsha, whom he calls the cousin of the Rema (רמ״א) and at whose grave in Lublin he says the congregation stood the year before,[6] writes in a responsum: “I do not want to hear of Zohar. We follow Talmud and the poskim; once you go to Zohar, who knows where you will end up?”

The Noda BiYehuda (נודע ביהודה) Against Leshem Yichud (לשם יחוד)

Rav Yechezkel Segal Landau (רבי יחזקאל סגל הלוי לנדא), the Noda BiYehuda, the great man of the eighteenth century and the chief rabbi of Prague, asks in his responsa:[7] “I do not understand you. Every simple Jew says before each mitzvah (מצוה), לשם יחוד קודשא בריך הוא ושכינתיה: before tefillin (תפילין), before tallis (טלית), before lulav (לולב). Do you know what you are saying? The whole formula is from Zohar, from kabbalah. If you do not understand it, do not say it.” He wrote about it in two responsa and would not allow it, and his disciples, Rav Tzvi Hirsch among them, went to war against those who said it, “because it comes from kabbalah and Zohar, and we stay far from it.”

The Principle: Halachah Over Kabbalah

Much of this, though not all, stems from Shabsai Tzvi, who “ruined it for all of us”: he learned Zohar and kabbalah and then changed halachah on their basis, which is forbidden. The Rema, in his Darkei Moshe (דרכי משה), writes that wherever kabbalah and halachah conflict one follows halachah, never kabbalah.[8]

VI. What Changed: The Baal Shem Tov (בעל שם טוב) and Our Generation

Why does everyone learn Zohar today? A great part of the answer lies with the Baal Shem Tov, who spread the light of Chassidus (חסידות), which is wholly based on Zohar, and who taught that in our generation, for Mashiach to come, we need Zohar and the study of kabbalah. Simple Jews therefore read it. The Chofetz Chaim (חפץ חיים) himself told the students of his yeshivah in Radin: even if you do not understand what you say, read it; much of it is midrash (מדרש) and the words of the Sages, and the parts you understand are good, the parts you do not are still holy to say. Something changed in our generation. We accept the Zohar as the work of Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai (רבי שמעון בר יוחאי), although some passages may have been added by later scholars, in the twelfth or thirteenth century; every word is sacred to us today and it is a part of our Torah literature, canonized — the lecturer wonders by whom — perhaps by the Arizal. The Chasam Sofer, the lecturer adds in passing, is said to have been in agreement (maskim (מסכים)) with the Mitpachas Sefarim (מטפחת ספרים) discussed below, a point that is raised and not developed.

Frankfurt held fast through the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries to its refusal to learn Zohar, because of the danger it had seen, and never gave in. The Yekkes do not say Brich Shmei to this day. The lecturer is certain that Hirsch agreed: Hirsch became the Rav of Frankfurt and, in the end, came out on top and led Frankfurt Jewry triumphantly.

VII. The Two Difficult Phrases in Brich Shmei

“Bar Elahin (בר אלהין)” — a Son of God

There is a second reason why they omitted it. The text says לא על אנש רחיצנא ולא על בר אלהין סמיכנא, “I do not trust in man, nor do I rely on a son of God (bar elahin).” The rabbis of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries who objected said: this cannot be said in the synagogue. In that age every prayer, every blessing, every word uttered in the synagogue was scrutinized by the authorities, particularly the Christian ones: are we to say publicly, each time we take out a sefer Torah, that we do not rely on one whom they claim to be the son of God? We do not wish to utter those words, nor to incite the Church against the Jewish people; let us refrain from the whole passage.

The Yerushalmi

And there is a deeper reason. The Jerusalem Talmud in Shabbos tells that when Nebuchadnezzar threw Chananiah, Mishael and Azariah into the furnace and the Holy One made a miracle for them, Nebuchadnezzar saw them unharmed in the fire, and a fourth with them whom the verse describes in Aramaic as resembling a bar elahin — an angel, perhaps Gavriel, who saved them.[9] The Yerushalmi teaches that an angel came and struck Nebuchadnezzar on the mouth and said: take it back; you may not speak of angels in this way. Learn from this that we never refer to anyone as a bar elahin. In the Torah the word Elokim (אלהים) may be used of judges (dayanim (דיינים)) and anyone with halachic authority; the lecturer adds that the targum (תרגום) renders בני האלהים in the Torah as דיינא, “judges,” and that to call angels elaha (אלהא) is forbidden. [Onkelos does not read so at Bereishis 6:2; see note 9.] The phrase, though it speaks of angels and obviously not of Jesus, was dangerous.

“Ana Avda DiKudsha Brich Hu (אנא עבדא דקודשא בריך הוא)”

The lecturer had said the week before, without realizing the full context, that one needs great presumption to declare אנא עבדא דקודשא בריך הוא, “I am a servant of the Holy One.” He quoted his teacher, Rav Avraham Shor (as heard) in Flatbush, who taught that one should say “I want to become a servant,” changing the text. Then he realized that the words are close to a verse: אני עבדך בן אמתך, “I am Your servant, the son of Your maidservant” (Tehillim (תהלים) 116:16), which the lecturer recalls from the Hoshana Rabbah (הושענא רבה) service as אנא עבדך בן אמתך. In saying the passage we are quoting David HaMelech (דוד המלך).[10]

אָנָּה יְהוָה כִּי אֲנִי עַבְדֶּךָ אֲנִי עַבְדְּךָ בֶּן אֲמָתֶךָ
“Please, Hashem, for I am Your servant; I am Your servant, the son of Your maidservant.”
Tehillim 116:16

The Yaavetz (יעב״ץ) on the Authorship of the Zohar

Whether the Zohar was written by Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai is a dispute on which the lecturer prefers not to enter. Rav Yaakov Emden, the Yaavetz, wrote a whole book, Mitpachas Sefarim, seeking to show that the Zohar was not authored entirely by Rashbi (רשב״י).[11] These, the lecturer warns, are dangerous claims, not to be repeated in public. The point for the present discussion: if this passage is truly that of Rashbi, and Rashbi himself elsewhere says one may not say bar elahin, there is a real difficulty, and the Yaavetz uses it as proof that this part was not his. Whatever the case, we say it.

VIII. The Chofetz Chaim’s Private Practice

Concerning אנא עבדא, the lecturer learned something remarkable. In the Mishnah Berurah (משנה ברורה) the Chofetz Chaim writes two or three times that we say Brich Shmei (it was relatively new, though by his day already old).[12] But his grandson Rav Hillel Zaks, a rosh yeshivah (ראש ישיבה) in Chevron in Eretz Yisrael, whose father Rav Mendel Zaks, the Chofetz Chaim’s son-in-law, was a rosh yeshivah at Yeshiva University, testified that his grandfather, although he wrote in his book that one should, did not himself say Brich Shmei. It is not the only matter on which the Chofetz Chaim did not keep what he wrote: for all of us, everything is Mishnah Berurah, yet he held a higher private standard on a few matters. The explanation given is that he did not wish to utter the words אנא עבדא דקודשא בריך הוא. We, however, do say it, and should: the Baal Shem Tov taught that when Brich Shmei is said with intention (kavanah (כוונה)) it is the most beautiful thing in the world and can open the gates of mercy.

IX. Gadlu laShem Iti (גדלו לה׳ אתי): Six Words for Six Steps

The Count of the Avudraham (אבודרהם)

After the sefer Torah is taken from the aron the leader says:

גַּדְּלוּ לַיהֹוָה אִתִּי וּנְרוֹמְמָה שְׁמוֹ יַחְדָּו
“Exalt Hashem with me, and let us raise His name together.”
Tehillim 34:4

Why this verse? The Avudraham, who counted every word of the prayers, observed that the verse has precisely six words, and gives, in the name of the Machzor Vitry (מחזור ויטרי) and other Rishonim (ראשונים), a reason.[13]

David and the Ark

When David HaMelech brought the aron habris (ארון הברית) to the City of David from the house of Oved-Edom the Gittite, as is told in Shmuel II (שמואל ב׳),[14] the ark had been captured by the Philistines and had remained for some months with Oved-Edom. The Gemara at the end of Berachos (ברכות) says that he was greatly blessed: the women of his household bore six at a time, and within a year he had a large family, and everyone recognized that this was for the ark’s sake. The lecturer says that the Gemara speaks of his daughters; the text as checked names his mother-in-law and her eight daughters-in-law.

When David decided to bring the ark up to the City of David, he danced before it with all his might, leaping, far more than we dance on Simchas Torah. Michal bas Shaul (מיכל בת שאול) looked out of the window and said: is this how a king dances? This is how a prostak (פראסטאק), a peasant, dances! Because of this mockery, “Michal daughter of Saul had no child until the day of her death.” One must never mock a person who lowers himself in honor of the Torah; the greatest honor is to humble oneself, to dance before the ark and the tablets within it. The prophet tells that the procession was slow: every six steps the bearers put the ark down and offered sacrifices; the Gemara in Sotah (סוטה) 35 records different opinions on what was brought.

Says the Avudraham: when we take the sefer Torah from the aron and walk it to the bimah (בימה), we reenact that march of the ark, and the six words of the verse recall the six steps, celebrating the Holy One at each interval.

X. Greeting and Escorting the Sefer Torah

Walking with the Torah

Is there a law requiring that one come up to the aron to greet the sefer Torah? It is not a practical law (halachah lemaaseh (הלכה למעשה)) but a beautiful custom. What is practical law is that if the sefer Torah passes by one’s seat one must escort it; the Shulchan Aruch (שלחן ערוך), in the lecturer’s words, says one walks the scroll to the bimah. The Arizal used to walk with it from his seat; in a crowded synagogue this is not always possible.

Looking at the Letters at the Lifting

The Sephardim, following another practice of the Arizal, lift the scroll (hagbahah (הגבהה)) before the reading, whereas the Ashkenazim do so after it. The Shulchan Aruch follows Maseches Soferim (מסכת סופרים), a statement of the Sages, that one lifts it immediately before the reading.[15] The Arizal would walk the scroll to the bimah, pause, and during the lifting look carefully at the scroll to see if he could read a word or a letter; this, he said, brings “a great light upon your soul” (אור גדול על נשמתך), the language of the Eitz Chaim (עץ חיים).

There is a custom among the kabbalists: if one finds a word whose first letter is the first letter of one’s name, this is one’s connection with one of the “six hundred thousand” (shishim ribo (ששים רבוא)) souls of Israel. The lecturer notes that the Torah does not literally contain six hundred thousand letters, but the idea stands: there is a matter of the six hundred thousand, and each soul has a connection with a letter of the Torah. The custom continues; one sees people hurrying to the scroll after the lifting to look. It is a remedy (segulah (סגולה)) for guarding the eyes (shemiras einayim (שמירת עינים)) and brings light to the soul.

XI. Why the Ashkenazim Lift After the Reading

The source in the Sages (חז״ל) and the Shulchan Aruch has the lifting before the reading, yet the Ashkenazim lift afterward. The reason, the lecturer reports, given by the Magen Avraham (מגן אברהם) or by one of the other later authorities (he is not certain),[16] is that if a flaw (pasul (פסול)) were found in the scroll during the reading, one could not truthfully have said וזאת התורה אשר שם משה לפני בני ישראל, “this is the Torah that Moshe placed before the children of Israel.” So we wait, and only when the day’s reading has been completed without incident do we lift it and declare, with certainty: this is exactly the Torah, word for word, letter for letter, that the Holy One gave to Moshe Rabbeinu (משה רבינו). The lecturer adds that perhaps there was an actual incident (maaseh shehayah (מעשה שהיה)) in which a flaw was found after the verse had been said. A member of the congregation asked whether the scroll had not been flawed the week before also; the lecturer replied that this is so, and that nonetheless, each week that we say it, we affirm it of that reading.

XII. Uncommon Honors

There is an honor (kibud (כיבוד)) that the lecturer has never seen sold in any synagogue, and which would be welcome. The person who rolls and binds the scroll (golel (גולל), from gelilah (גלילה)) is handed the mantle (meil (מעיל)) and the belt (gartel (גארטל)) by someone standing by the bimah. The act of picking up the mantle from the bimah and handing it to the one who rolls is itself a great and distinguished honor (kibud muflag (כיבוד מופלג)); even if it is not sold, it should be given as an honor. The Or Zarua (אור זרוע), who teaches that opening the aron and removing the scroll are honors, teaches that this one also is.[17]

XIII. The Custom of Pesichah (פתיחה) for an Expectant Father

There is a custom to give the honor of opening the aron (pesichah) to a man whose wife is expecting a child. This is relatively new, and the lecturer says it comes from the Chida (חיד״א), who brings it explicitly.[18] The Chida writes that it is a remedy (segulah): just as the father opens the Holy of Holies, so may the Holy One open the womb and may all go smoothly. In a case of difficult labor (makshah leyaled (מקשה לילד)), someone once asked a great man what to say, and was told: say יזכור [the word as heard] — so that the children should come out. Our custom is to give pesichah. Rav Chaim Palagi, one of the great Sephardic authorities, rules that the person who opens the aron should also close it,[19] lest, Heaven forbid, the womb remain open.

XIV. The Sanctity of Silence at the Removal of the Scroll

The lecturer gives himself a rebuke first: in the interval between the opening of the aron and the saying of גדלו לה׳ אתי, there is conversation, while the scroll is carried, until the gabbai begins to call the aliyos (עליות). It is not supposed to be so. The Chida rebukes sharply those who talk while the scroll is being carried, placed on the bimah and opened, a most auspicious time. In strict law the lecturer cannot say that the Shulchan Aruch forbids speech here, but the Chida says: be careful, do not talk; say the words, לך ה׳ הגדולה, אב הרחמים הוא ירחם עמוסים and ויחון אותנו לפליטת עולמים, beautiful prayers. The Chida also holds that Brich Shmei should ideally be said before the scroll is taken out; some communities said it while walking, but our custom is before. In any case, do not talk.

There is even a question among the later authorities whether one may interrupt pesukei dezimrah (פסוקי דזמרה) to say Brich Shmei and the other verses in honor of the Torah. Rav Shimon Greenfeld, the Maharshag (מהרש״ג), one of the great authorities of Hungary, has a whole responsum on it.[20] In the end he holds that one should not interrupt, but so great is the importance of these verses, to greet and embrace the scroll as it comes to the bimah, that he takes the question seriously.

XV. The Meaning of Hakol Havu Godel (הכל הבו גודל לאלהינו)

When the gabbai calls, after ותגלה ותראה מלכותו עלינו בזמן קרוב, the words הכל הבו גודל לאלהינו, “all, give greatness to our God,” what is he doing? The Rishonim who explain the order of prayer, the Machzor Vitry, the Avudraham, Rabbi Yehudah ben Yakar and the Rokeach (רוקח), explain it as the call to the congregation to be silent.[21] Gentlemen, hush! We are about to have blessings: Barchu (ברכו) and the blessing over the Torah from the one called up. Be silent and answer “Amen.” That is the message each week, though we do not hear it. Moshe Rabbeinu, before the song of Haazinu (האזינו), said כי שם ה׳ אקרא הבו גדל לאלהינו, “for I proclaim the name of Hashem; give greatness to our God”: answer with me. So, when the one called up receives the call, the gabbai is saying: let us listen. The lecturer announces that Barchu and the blessing over the Torah will be taken up the following week.

XVI. A Proposed Custom: Dividing the Honors

The lecturer proposes, though it is the gabbai’s domain, a possible new custom. He found the night before, in one of the Rishonim, the Or Zarua, Rabbi Yitzchak of Vienna (“Wien”), a chief rabbi of Vienna and one of the later Tosafists, as brought down in the Mordechai (מרדכי),[22] a dispute in a synagogue. The gabbai had sold the honor of opening the aron and taking out the scroll, to be given to the cantor (chazan (חזן)), and the prayer leader protested: “What do you mean? I am the chazan, the shliach tzibbur (שליח ציבור); I pray Shacharis (שחרית); I must take the scroll from the aron and carry it to the bimah. Why are you giving it to someone else?” The Mordechai rules that each action done in honor of the Torah may be separated and assigned to a different person: one individual takes the scroll out of the aron and hands it to the shliach tzibbur, and the shliach tzibbur carries it to the bimah. That is the custom already practiced today, and that Mordechai is its source.

Closing

The lecturer closes the discourse with a wish for a good week and with the final mishnah (משנה) of Makkos (מכות):[23] “Rabbi Chananya ben Akashya says: the Holy One wished to make Israel meritorious; therefore He gave them much Torah and many commandments, as it is said, ה׳ חפץ למען צדקו יגדיל תורה ויאדיר, “Hashem desired, for the sake of its righteousness, to make the Torah great and glorious.”
