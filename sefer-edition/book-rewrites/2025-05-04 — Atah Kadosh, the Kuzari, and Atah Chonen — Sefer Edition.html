אַתָּה קָדוֹשׁ, הַכּוּזָרִי, וְאַתָּה חוֹנֵן

Atah Kadosh, the Kuzari, and Atah Chonen — The Third Berachah and the Opening of the Bakashos
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Sunday Morning Series on Tefillah
Recorded 4 May 2025  ·  Sefer Edition

Returning to the running track of שמונה עשרה after the pre-Pesach detour through קדושה. The opening triad of berachos closes; the body of bakashos opens; and between them stands the deepest question a mispallel asks in a lifetime of davening — what does it mean to call הקדוש ברוך הוא kadosh, and what does it mean for us.

I. The Return to the Running Track

Before Pesach of the previous year, this chaburah completed a sustained study of Kedushah (קדושה). That detour was structural, not accidental: in the chazaras ha’shatz (חזרת הש"ץ), Kedushah stands in the mouth of the tzibbur just before the third berachah of Shemoneh Esrei (שמונה עשרה), and no account of the third berachah can be given without first understanding what precedes it. That work having been done, the running track resumes: the third berachah itself, and then the body of the Amidah that follows.

The governing question of the entire series returns each time. Where does each berachah come from? Why did the Chachomim (חכמים) arrange them in this seder (סדר) and no other? Each answer, when it comes, illuminates not only the specific berachah at hand but the architecture of Jewish tefillah as a whole.

II. The Seder of the First Three Berachos

The Derashah of the Gemara in Megillah

The Gemara (גמרא) in Megillah[1] derives the seder of the first three berachos from a single pasuk in Tehillim,[2] the mizmor that opens Havu Lashem Bnei Elim:

הָבוּ לַה' בְּנֵי אֵלִים, הָבוּ לַה' כָּבוֹד וָעֹז. הָבוּ לַה' כְּבוֹד שְׁמוֹ, הִשְׁתַּחֲווּ לַה' בְּהַדְרַת קֹדֶשׁ.
Ascribe to Hashem, O sons of might, ascribe to Hashem glory and strength. Ascribe to Hashem the glory of His name; bow to Hashem in the majesty of holiness.
Tehillim 29:1–2

The three clauses of the pasuk map exactly onto the opening triad of the Amidah:

  • Bnei Elim (בני אלים) — the mighty ones, the strong ones. The Avos HaKedoshim (אבות הקדושים) are these bnei elim. From here the first berachah: Avos (אבות).
  • Havu LaShem Oz (הבו לה' עוז) — ascribe strength to Hashem. From here the second berachah: Gevuros (גבורות).
  • Havu LaShem Kevod Shemo (הבו לה' כבוד שמו) — ascribe to Hashem the glory of His name. From here the third berachah: Kedushas HaShem (קדושת השם).

The Chachomim did not compose an arbitrary sequence of praises and then hunt for scriptural support. The order of the berachos is embedded in a single pasuk; the Chachomim perceived it there and unfolded it into tefillah.

III. The Doubled Praise — Atah Kadosh V’Shimcha Kadosh

A Doubling Without Parallel

The third berachah contains a feature found nowhere else in Shemoneh Esrei: a doubled praise. Atah Kadosh V’Shimcha Kadosh (אתה קדוש ושמך קדוש). Every other berachah of the Amidah expresses one concept — Atah Chonen, Hashiveinu, Selach Lanu. Only here does the praise repeat: You are holy, and Your name is holy. The question presents itself immediately: what work does the doubling do?

The Reading of the Zohar HaKadosh

The Zohar HaKadosh (זוהר הקדוש)[3] develops the question at kabbalistic depth beyond the scope of this study; on the surface, however, its answer can be stated in a single line. The moment a finite, embodied human being opens his mouth to declare Hashem Kadosh, the very utterance is a species of presumption. Who is he who says this? How does the mispallel, still standing in his flesh and his limitations, presume to be makdish the Ribbono Shel Olam (רבונו של עולם)? The moment he says Kadosh, he must in the same breath push the recognition of separation further out: V’Shimcha Kadosh. You are so far above me that a single declaration of holiness is not enough — the very name is holy, at a further remove still. The doubling is the internal correction of the presumption of the utterance.

IV. The Meaning of Kadosh — Muvdal U’Mufrash

Beyond “Holy”

The English word holy renders kadosh (קדוש) so imperfectly that the translation obscures the concept. The peshat of kadosh is muvdal u’mufrash (מובדל ומופרש) — separated, set apart, transcendent. Every context in which the root קד"ש appears is illuminated by this lexical anchor.

The Pasuk of Kedoshim Tihyu

The pasuk at the head of Parshas Kedoshim[4] reads Kedoshim Tihyu Ki Kadosh Ani (קדושים תהיו כי קדוש אני). Rashi immediately supplies the operational gloss: Perushim Tihyu min HaArayos (פרושים תהיו מן העריות) — be separate from forbidden relations. The lexical equation kadosh = muvdal is not merely one connotation among many; it is the primary meaning that Rashi, drawing on the Sifra, brings to the surface as the working sense of the word.

The implication for man is significant. Every human being carries within himself the possibility of stepping out of the finite, of the ta’avos (תאוות) and the yetzer hara (יצר הרע), of becoming muvdal from his own lower gravities. The Ribbono Shel Olam, despite the intimacy of His communication with the world, remains ultimately separate from it. Man is charged with imitating that separation: Kedoshim Tihyu Ki Kadosh Ani.

The Faces of Kedushah in Halachah

The equation kadosh = muvdal is confirmed across three distinct halachic institutions:

  • The consecration of a korban. Makdish (מקדיש) an animal means separating it from one’s own possession; it is no longer chullin, no longer part of the owner’s reshus. To be hekdesh (הקדש) is to be structurally set apart from the domain of the everyday.
  • Mekadesh es ha’ishah — kiddushin. The Raavad (ראב"ד) in Hilchos Gerushin[5] develops the analytical position that kiddushin (קידושין) operates as a kinyan issur (קנין איסור): the wife is not property acquired, but is halachically separated from all other men. She is mekudeshes to her husband precisely because she is muvdeles from everyone else. Before the cherem d’Rabbeinu Gershom (חרם דרבינו גרשום) a man could marry more than one wife — Dovid HaMelech did so; today, baruch Hashem, the cherem reshapes the practical metzius.
  • The nazir. The Torah itself designates the nazir (נזיר) as kadosh. He is separated from society and its comforts — no wine, no contact with a mes, hair grown out uncut. His kedushah is his structural separation.

The Geography of Kadesh

The lexical sense of kadosh as muvdal is confirmed even in the geography of Tanach. The place-name Kadesh (קדש) appears more than once. Kadesh Barnea (קדש ברנע) sits at the southern border of Eretz Yisrael. Kedesh Naftali (קדש נפתלי) sits at the northern border, in Sefer Shoftim.[6] Every location in the Tanach that carries the name Kadesh stands on a border between regions or peoples. Kadesh is where one nation ends and another begins — the very meaning of the word rendered in geographic space.

V. The Sefer HaKuzari

The Author and His Era

The reading of the third berachah that follows in this study is the reading of one of the great Rishonim of Spain: Rabbeinu Yehudah HaLevi (רבי יהודה הלוי) in his Sefer HaKuzari.[7] The Kuzari is a foundational sefer machashavah of Klal Yisrael — and, notably, one that occupies a curious place in the world of Jewish learning.

Its Absence from Standard Yeshivos

Show a Lithuanian yeshiva that regularly learns Moreh Nevuchim or Kuzari as part of its seder ha’yom, and few if any will be found. The exception, and it is a decisive one, is Yeshivas Merkaz HaRav. When HaRav Kook, zt”l, established the yeshiva in Eretz Yisrael, he deliberately incorporated the Kuzari into the seder ha’limud, and from there it became a central text of the dati leumi beis midrash.[8] The precedent was, in the classical understanding, that of the Gra, who called the Kuzari kadosh v’tahor for those equipped to learn it — yod’ei da’as. The mainstream Litvish mashgiach would traditionally intercept a bachur discovered learning Kuzari with the concern that philosophical inquiry might disturb the direct simplicity of his emunah. In truth, the philosophical questions the Kuzari addresses trouble few bachurim today; but the sefer survives, and its reading of tefillah has entered the mainstream of the Rishonim and Acharonim who explicate the seder ha’tefillah.

VI. The Khazar Narrative

The Story as the Kuzari Tells It

The Kuzari opens with a narrative that for centuries was received as legend. In the eighth or ninth century, in the region of the Caucasus — the Kavkaz, in the mountains north of what is today Uzbekistan and its neighbors — a king named Yehosef (יהוסף), also called Yosef, ruled the kingdom of Khazar. The Kuzari presents him as a righteous and thoughtful goy gamur in earnest search of emes (אמת). A historical footnote of some interest: a substantial portion of the Kavkazi Jewry now living in Eretz Yisrael traces its ancestry to this region, and one school of historical opinion argues that a portion of Ashkenazi Jewry descends from the converted Khazars.

The Dream and the Investigation

The king dreamt of a malach (מלאך) who spoke to him: Your intent is good and righteous; your deeds are wrong. He awoke understanding: he had to find a different religion. He interviewed a philosopher and remained unmoved. He interviewed a Muslim scholar and remained unmoved. He then approached the Jews, and there met a chacham (חכם): Rav Yitzchak HaSangari (רבי יצחק הסנגרי). The Kuzari records the ensuing dialogue between king and chacham. The chacham’s answers convinced the king; he returned to his nation, retreated first to a cave together with a group of hidden Jewish chassidim and his chief of staff, underwent his own conversion, and only then persuaded his kingdom to follow.

The Historical Verification — The Cairo Genizah Letters

For centuries the narrative was received in the world of scholarship as a literary construction — a philosophical dialogue with a fictional frame. The Cairo Genizah[9] altered the picture. Among the manuscripts recovered by Solomon Schechter, three letters of the eighth or ninth century have come to light — letters either written by the king of Khazar himself or issued by his court and addressed to Rav Chasdai ibn Shaprut (רבי חסדאי אבן שפרוט), a major figure in the Jewry of Umayyad Spain. Rav Chasdai had heard of the Khazar report and had dispatched shluchim to verify it; the letters preserved in the Genizah are the responses that returned to him. The details vary from the Kuzari’s account — the conversion may have been partial rather than total, extending to the king and the noble families rather than to every citizen of the kingdom — but that something occurred is no longer in question. Independent corroboration comes from Islamic historical sources of the same period, which describe a Caucasus tribal kingdom that experimented first with Islam and then turned to Judaism. The Khazar episode is real.

The Composition of the Sefer

Rabbeinu Yehudah HaLevi wrote the Kuzari in Arabic. Rabbi Yehudah ibn Tibbon (יהודה אבן תיבון), the great translator of the Arabic works of the Rishonim, rendered it into Hebrew. The literal Arabic title translates as The Book of Disputations, Refutations, and Proofs on Behalf of the Despised Religion — the despised religion being, of course, our own. In a letter recovered from the Genizah, Rabbeinu Yehudah HaLevi himself refers to the work modestly as davar katan (דבר קטן); he was, apparently, unsatisfied with it. The world has held it otherwise. By the time of the Rambam, the chachomim of Lunel were writing to him: We have read the Kuzari; we have read the Chovos HaLevavos; now send us the Moreh Nevuchim. The Kuzari stands as one of the pillars of Jewish machashavah.

VII. The Kuzari’s Reading of the First Three Berachos

The Immanence of Gevuros

The second berachah of the Amidah — Gevuros — speaks of the Ribbono Shel Olam as Rofei Cholim, Matir Asurim, Mechayeh Meisim. Each of these declares an intimate engagement of Hashem with the world. When one is sick, He is involved. When one is bound, He is involved. When the dead will be raised, He is the one who raises them. The berachah is a sustained affirmation of the immanence of the Ribbono Shel Olam in the ordinary and extraordinary affairs of His creatures.

The Danger of Immanence Without Transcendence

The Kuzari observes that a mispallel who davens Gevuros without what follows could arrive at a distorted conception: Hashem is close, accessible, immanent — a buddy, one to whom one calls in familiar terms. The Kuzari teaches that the Chachomim immediately follow Gevuros with Atah Kadosh precisely to correct this potential distortion. Yes — He is close. And in the same breath: He is infinitely separate. Immanence without transcendence collapses the distance to an idolatry of familiarity. The pasuk in Parshas Kedoshim teaches kedushasi lema’alah mi’kedushaschem (קדושתי למעלה מקדושתכם); before any bakashah can be spoken, the mispallel acknowledges that Hashem’s kedushah is lema’alah from his own.

VIII. The Yehudi’s Capacity for Kedushah

The Assertion Concealed in the Praise

Beneath the Kuzari’s point lies a further one drawn by the Acharonim. The mispallel who declares Atah Kadosh is not merely praising; he is asserting his own capacity. The Mishnah in Avos[10] teaches: Chavivin Yisrael she’nikre’u banim laMakom (חביבין ישראל שנקראו בנים למקום) — beloved is Yisrael, called children to the Omnipresent. Every human being carries tzelem Elokim (צלם אלוקים); Klal Yisrael carries an additional relation of sonship. Into that sonship the Ribbono Shel Olam implanted the same koach He Himself possesses: the koach of separation, the koach of kedushah. Just as He is Kadosh, we have the capacity to be kedoshim: u’kedoshim b’chol yom yehalleluchah selah (וקדושים בכל יום יהללוך סלה).

Each morning, when the mispallel stands and says Atah Kadosh, he is thereby also saying — I have that capacity. No matter how heavy the daily ta’avos, no matter how insistent the yetzer hara, Klal Yisrael carries the koach of kedushah built in by the same Hand that built us. The daily declaration of Kadosh is a daily reminder that this capacity is not a distant aspiration but a native possession.

The Rizhiner on Kedoshim Tihyu

The Rizhiner Rebbe (רבי ישראל מריזשין)[11] reads the well-known Rashi on Kedoshim Tihyu Ki Kadosh Ani with an unexpected inflection. The standard reading of Rashi takes the phrase Talmud Lomar “Ki Kadosh Ani” (תלמוד לומר "כי קדוש אני") as a technical derashah: one might have imagined a boundless equivalence — that we should be kadosh in every sense as Hashem is Kadosh; talmud lomar “ki kadosh Ani” is the derashah’s corrective — kedushasi lema’alah mi’kedushaschem. The Rizhiner reads talmud lomar not as a derivational formula but as an imperative: teach yourself to say it. Talk to yourself every day: I am a kadosh. The act of sustained self-affirmation crystallizes over time into self-becoming. The daily Atah Kadosh is, in this reading, the mispallel’s own talmud lomar — the daily instruction to himself of what he is.

Rav Yonah of Vizhnitz — The Gap Between Lips and Nefesh

A story preserved in the chassidishe transmission of Rav Yonah of Vizhnitz[12] renders the avodah of Atah Kadosh in an unforgettable form. In Prague, a baal habayis who had not received the aliyah he had expected made a public shturem in shul on Shabbos. Rav Yonah approached him quietly and said: I do not understand. You sit by the mizrach; I heard you just now saying Yishtabach aloud. You said — V’nafshi k’afar la’kol tih’yeh — let my soul be like dust to all. How is it possible that a man who has just prayed for the effacement of the self makes a public fuss over an aliyah?

The work of Atah Kadosh is the closing of the gap between the words on the lips and the state of the nefesh. The mouth easily declares kadosh; the life must be arranged to match.

IX. The Closing of the Opening Unit

Halachah — The Three Berachos as One Unit

The third berachah is the seal of the first liturgical unit of Shemoneh Esrei — the unit of shevach. Halachah l’maaseh confirms this structurally. If a mispallel makes a critical error at any point in the first three berachos requiring him to return, he cannot simply resume from the beginning of HaKel HaKadosh; he must return to the beginning of Shemoneh Esrei altogether. The three berachos form a single indivisible unit of praise, and any repair must reconstitute the unit from its opening.

The Rema on the Amen After HaKel HaKadosh

A less-widely-known halachah gives the point additional weight. If a mispallel arrives at shul while chazaras ha’shatz is already in progress and cannot begin his Shemoneh Esrei with the chazan, the Rema[13] rules that he should wait. He should say Kedushah with the tzibbur; then continue waiting through the close of HaKel HaKadosh; answer Amen to that berachah specifically; and only then begin his own Shemoneh Esrei. That particular Amen — the Amen that seals the opening unit — is more important than any other Amen he might answer during chazaras ha’shatz. The Beis Yosef registers hesitation about extending the wait this far; the Rema’s point stands. It is not only the Kedushah that matters at this station of the davening, but the Amen that closes the triadic unit.

The Amen Within Krias Shema

The general principle is well known that one cannot interrupt Krias Shema for ordinary amenim. The exceptions traditionally taught are Kaddish and Kedushah. On the same logic, the Rema permits the Amen following HaKel HaKadosh to be answered mid-Krias Shema as well. Halachah l’maaseh, this is a pesak that many mispallelim pass their lifetime without encountering.

X. L’Dor VaDor Versus Atah Kadosh — The Nusach Story

The Machlokes

A well-known divergence separates the two great nusachos at this station of the Amidah. In the silent Shemoneh Esrei of the yachid, does one open the third berachah with L’dor VaDor Nagid Godlecha (לדור ודור נגיד גדלך) — the formulation of Nusach Ashkenaz — or with Atah Kadosh V’Shimcha Kadosh — the formulation of Nusach Sefard?

The Original Nusach

The Rambam in Sefer Ahavah,[14] in his seder ha’tefillah, records the third berachah in the L’dor VaDor formulation. This is a fact of considerable weight, since the Rambam is Sephardic, while the L’dor VaDor formulation is today identified as Nusach Ashkenaz. The Provencal Rishonim confirm the picture. The Orchos Chaim and the Kol Bo,[15] both cited extensively by the Beis Yosef, preserve the same early tradition: the original nusach ha’masorah of the yachid’s silent Amidah ran L’dor VaDor — flowing directly from the Kedushah conceptually and textually.

Why the Nusach Was Later Modified

Why then was the nusach subsequently reorganized in the Sephardic tradition to open with Atah Kadosh V’Shimcha Kadosh in the silent Shemoneh Esrei? The reason is structural. The third berachah requires three mentions of Kadosh, mirroring the malachim’s triple Kadosh Kadosh Kadosh.

In the chazaras ha’shatz, the three Kadosh’s are explicit in the Kedushah itself, as the tzibbur proclaims them. But the yachid davening alone has no Kedushah. His silent berachah, if it opened with L’dor VaDor, would be missing the requisite triple invocation. The Chachomim therefore reorganized the yachid’s nusach to insert three Kadosh mentions explicitly into the body of the berachah: Atah Kadosh, V’Shimcha Kadosh, u’Kedoshim b’chol yom yehalleluchah selah. Now the count is satisfied within the silent Amidah itself.

The Sephardic Atah Kadosh is therefore not the original formulation; it is a later structural takanah. L’dor VaDor is the original. In the chazaras ha’shatz, both nusachos say L’dor VaDor in agreement; only in the silent tefillah of the yachid do the traditions diverge, and the divergence is the mark of a later halachic accommodation of the three-Kadosh requirement.

XI. HaMelech HaKadosh on Aseres Yemei Teshuvah

The Substitution and Its Source

During the Aseres Yemei Teshuvah, the closing formula of the third berachah shifts. In place of HaKel HaKadosh, the mispallel says HaMelech HaKadosh. The Zohar HaKadosh grounds the substitution kabbalistically: HaKel is associated with chesed; HaMelech is associated with din and gevurah. When HaMelech yosheiv ba’din (המלך יושב בדין), the formulation shifts accordingly. The Gemara in Berachos[16] establishes the halachic weight of the substitution: forgetting HaMelech HaKadosh during the ten days is me’akev. One who erroneously said HaKel HaKadosh must return to the beginning of Shemoneh Esrei and re-daven.

Rav Avli Posweller’s Chiddush

Rav Avli Posweller (רבי אבלי פוסוועלר)[17] is a Lithuanian posek whose broader oeuvre is little studied today; his renown rests upon a single chiddush preserved in the Chayei Adam, around which the Acharonim have debated for two centuries. This is the kind of lomdishe pilpul that is traditionally brought forward as an Erev Rosh HaShanah devar Torah.

His chiddush: if one says HaKel HaKadosh in error on Rosh HaShanah day, he must indeed return and re-daven. But if he says HaKel HaKadosh in error on Rosh HaShanah night, he is yotzei and need not repeat.

The parallel that generates the chiddush is the halachah of Yaaleh V’Yavo on Rosh Chodesh. One who forgets Yaaleh V’Yavo at the Shacharis of Rosh Chodesh must return and repeat; but one who forgets Yaaleh V’Yavo at the Maariv of Rosh Chodesh does not repeat — because ein mekadshin es ha’chodesh ba’laylah (אין מקדשין את החודש בלילה). Rav Avli applies the same logic to HaMelech HaKadosh: the Aseres Yemei Teshuvah begin on Rosh HaShanah, which is bound to the sanctification of Chodesh Tishrei; at night the new chodesh is not yet formally mekudash. Hence HaMelech HaKadosh at night should not be me’akev.

The Rejection of the Chiddush

The Mishnah Berurah and most Acharonim reject the chiddush. Their objection is precise. On an ordinary weekday Maariv following Rosh Chodesh, the rest of the tefillah is a standard weekday Shemoneh Esrei; a missing Yaaleh V’Yavo creates no internal contradiction with the surrounding text. But on Rosh HaShanah night, the entire nusach of the tefillah is the full Yom Tov davening — Atah Vechartanu, HaMelech Mocheil, the entire liturgical apparatus of the day. Saying HaKel HaKadosh in the middle of such a davening is tarti d’sasri (תרתי דסתרי) — a head-on collision with the surrounding nusach. On this ground, HaMelech HaKadosh at night remains me’akev. The chiddush circulates as a classic lomdishe curiosity; the halachah l’maaseh follows the Mishnah Berurah.

XII. Birchas Atah Chonen — The Opening “Atah”

The Anomaly of the Opening Word

With the third berachah sealed, the body of bakashos now begins. A structural anomaly immediately arrests the attention of the careful mispallel. Birchas Atah Chonen opens with the word Atah (אתה). No other berachah in the twelve middle berachos of bakashah opens with Atah. Why does this one?

The Rishonim — A Transition from Shevach to Bakashah

The Rishonim supply the answer. The first three berachos are shevach; the middle twelve are bakashos; the Chachomim wove a transition. The first berachah of bakashah should still open in the register of shevach. Atah Chonen l’adam da’as (אתה חונן לאדם דעת) is itself a praise — an acknowledgment that the Ribbono Shel Olam is the source of da’as. Only then, in the second clause, does the request emerge: chonneinu me’itcha de’ah binah v’haskeil. The opening Atah is the hinge on which the whole Amidah pivots from praise into supplication, and the Chachomim arranged it so that even the pivot itself is a praise.

XIII. Why Da’as First?

The Gemara in Nedarim

Of all the things a mispallel could ask the Ribbono Shel Olam for, why does the sequence of bakashos begin with da’as? The answer is supplied by the Gemara in Nedarim,[18] which reads:

אִי דַּעַת קָנִיתָ, מַה חָסַרְתָּ? אִי דַּעַת חָסַרְתָּ, מַה קָנִיתָ?
If you have acquired da’as, what are you lacking? If you lack da’as, what have you acquired?
Nedarim 41a

The Gemara continues with the associated kelal: ein ani ela b’da’as (אין עני אלא בדעת) — the only true poverty is the poverty of da’as. The impoverished person is not the man standing at the door collecting tzedakah; the truly impoverished is the man without sechel. Chazal knew this Gemara long before it became a popular song. Da’as is the foundation of every other bakashah in the Amidah: parnassah, refuah, shalom bayis — each of these matters, and each of these functions, only if da’as is in place. Without da’as, the other requests are answered into a vessel that cannot hold them.

XIV. The Tragedy of Lost Da’as

What the Berachah Actually Guards Against

Anyone who has sat with a person who has lost his da’as — through Alzheimer’s, dementia, or severe amnesia — understands what the Chachomim installed at the head of the bakashos. The body remains; the person, in a sense, does not. The daily berachah of Atah Chonen is a request that the mispallel not take for granted the gift of waking each morning with a mind that thinks, understands, and functions as the tzelem Elokim the Ribbono Shel Olam installed in him. The pasuk V’al tashlicheinu l’eis ziknah (ואל תשליכנו לעת זקנה) — do not cast us off in the time of old age — anchors the request: let da’as remain until the end.

The Rebbe shared, in the course of this shiur, the passing of his grandfather three days earlier — a Jew of ninety-four, sharp as a knife to his final breath, who succumbed to COVID in a Queens hospital on the first of May 2025. His other grandfather — a distinguished תלמיד חכם, professor of תלמוד at Bar-Ilan University, author of sefarim — spent his last six years being fed by hand. The contrast, the Rebbe observed, is exactly what the mispallel davens about each morning when he says Atah Chonen: it is never too early and never too late to ask that the gift of da’as remain intact.

The full bakashah completes the point: Atah Chonen l’adam da’as... chonneinu me’itcha de’ah binah v’haskeil. Grant me da’as to the end of my days, that I may function as the human being You created me to be.

XV. Modern Adaptations of the Kuzari

Two notable later works follow the dialogic form of the Kuzari:[19]

  • Rabbi David Nieto (1654–1728), chacham of the Spanish and Portuguese congregation in London, composed a Kuzari HaSheini — updating the questions of the Kuzari for the concerns of his own era.
  • Rabbi Dr. Isaac Breuer (1883–1946), grandson of Rav Shimshon Refael Hirsch and a leading figure in Agudas Yisrael, composed a Sefer Kuzari Chadash — a twentieth-century treatment of the same enterprise.

Both works are read today primarily within their respective scholarly circles. The genius of the original Kuzari, which distinguishes it from an ordinary philosophy book, lies in its dialogic form — a sustained conversation between king and chacham, with give-and-take, argument and reply. That form pulls the reader in. The Rebbe does not urge that this chaburah take up the study of Kuzari as a formal seder; but its reading of the first three berachos has entered the mainstream Rishonim and Acharonim who explicate the seder ha’tefillah, and it deserves recognition on that account.

XVI. Practical Summary

  • The seder of the first three berachos is embedded in a single pasuk. Havu LaShem Bnei Elim yields Avos, Gevuros, and Kedushas HaShem in exactly that order.
  • The doubling of Atah Kadosh V’Shimcha Kadosh is the internal correction of the presumption of the utterance itself. A finite being who declares kadosh immediately pushes the recognition of separation further out.
  • Kadosh means muvdal u’mufrash. The lexical equation runs through hekdesh, kiddushin, nazir, and even geography.
  • The Kuzari’s reading is decisive. Gevuros establishes immanence; Atah Kadosh immediately corrects any collapse of the distance into familiarity. Before any bakashah, kedushasi lema’alah mi’kedushaschem.
  • The daily Atah Kadosh is also an assertion of the mispallel’s own capacity. The koach kedushah was installed by the same Hand that made him.
  • The Amen that closes HaKel HaKadosh is the seal of the opening unit — important enough to wait for at late arrival, important enough to answer even within Krias Shema.
  • L’dor VaDor is the original silent nusach; Atah Kadosh is a later structural takanah to supply the required three mentions of Kadosh where the yachid has no Kedushah.
  • HaMelech HaKadosh at night on Rosh HaShanah remains me’akev despite Rav Avli Posweller’s chiddush; the surrounding Yom Tov nusach creates tarti d’sasri.
  • Atah Chonen opens with Atah as a hinge from shevach into bakashah. Da’as is placed first because without it the other bakashos cannot function; ein ani ela b’da’as.

מראה מקומות  ·  Mareh Mekomos

Each note runs short citation first, then an expanded elaboration.

  1. Gemara Megillah 17b — seder of the berachos of Shemoneh Esrei derived from Tehillim 29. — The Gemara in Megillah presents the classical derashah deriving the seder of the eighteen (now nineteen) berachos of Shemoneh Esrei from various pesukim in Tanach. The opening triad is grounded in Havu LaShem Bnei Elim; the middle bakashos in verses of Tehillim and Divrei HaYamim; the closing three (Avodah, Hodaah, Shalom) in the closing structure of the Korban Tamid. This sugya is the principal source for understanding why Chazal ordered the berachos as they did. ↩
  2. Tehillim 29:1–2 — Havu LaShem Bnei Elim, Havu LaShem Kavod VaOz; Havu LaShem Kevod Shemo. — The opening pesukim of Mizmor 29 in Tehillim, recited as the closing mizmor of Kabbalas Shabbos and as part of the hotza’as sefer Torah, are read by Chazal as the textual ground for the structure of the opening unit of the Amidah. The three clauses (Bnei Elim, Oz, Kevod Shemo) map respectively onto Avos, Gevuros, and Kedushah. ↩
  3. Zohar HaKadosh on Atah Kadosh V’Shimcha Kadosh, and on HaMelech (din) versus HaKel (chesed). — The Zohar develops two relevant points across multiple passages. First, on the doubled Atah Kadosh V’Shimcha Kadosh: a finite, embodied mispallel who presumes to declare Hashem kadosh must immediately deepen the recognition of separation by adding V’Shimcha Kadosh. Second, on the substitution during Aseres Yemei Teshuvah: HaKel is associated kabbalistically with chesed, while HaMelech sits in gevurah and din; when the King sits in judgment, the formulation shifts to HaMelech HaKadosh. Not verified through Sefaria: an oral kabbalistic tradition drawn on by the Rebbe; preserved as cited. ↩
  4. Vayikra 19:2 with Rashi — Kedoshim Tihyu Ki Kadosh Ani; Perushim Tihyu min HaArayos. — The opening pasuk of Parshas Kedoshim. Rashi, drawing on the Sifra, glosses Kedoshim as Perushim — separated, particularly from forbidden relations — establishing the lexical equation kadosh = muvdal. Verified on Sefaria: Rashi on Leviticus 19:2 — “keep aloof from the forbidden sexual relations just mentioned and from sinful thoughts.” The same pasuk closes with Ki Kadosh Ani, the seifa on which the Rizhiner builds his reading of Talmud Lomar. ↩
  5. Hasagos HaRaavad on Rambam, Hilchos Gerushin — kiddushin as a kinyan issur. — The Raavad (Rabbi Avraham ben David, c. 1125–1198, Posquières), in his Hasagos on the Rambam’s Hilchos Gerushin, develops the analytical position that kiddushin operates as a kinyan issur — not (or not only) a property-style kinyan mamon in the wife, but a chalos of forbidden status excluding her from all other men. The Rebbe brings this to illustrate the lexical sense of kedushah as muvdal: kiddushin separates the ishah from the rest of the world, exactly as hekdesh separates a korban from chullin. ↩
  6. Kadesh Barnea (southern border of Eretz Yisrael) and Kedesh Naftali (northern border, Sefer Shoftim). — Kadesh Barnea appears repeatedly in Sefer Bemidbar and Sefer Devarim as a station in the wilderness journey and as a marker of the southern boundary. Kedesh Naftali appears in Sefer Yehoshua and Sefer Shoftim as a city of refuge in the northern territory of Naftali. Every place bearing the name Kadesh in Tanach sits at a border, confirming the lexical equation kadosh = muvdal even in the geography of the Land. ↩
  7. Sefer HaKuzari (Rabbeinu Yehudah HaLevi, c. 1075–1141) — composed in Judeo-Arabic in 1139 in Spain; Hebrew translation by Yehudah ibn Tibbon. — The full Arabic title translates as The Book of Refutation and Proof on Behalf of the Despised Religion. Translated into Hebrew by Yehudah ibn Tibbon in 1167 in Provence. The work is structured as a sustained dialogue between the king of Khazar and the chacham Rav Yitzchak HaSangari, developing Jewish theology, the covenantal status of Klal Yisrael, the structure of tefillah, and a sustained reading of the seder of the first three berachos of Shemoneh Esrei. Rabbeinu Yehudah HaLevi, in a letter recovered from the Cairo Genizah, refers to the work modestly as davar katan; the world has held it as one of the foundational sifrei machashavah of Klal Yisrael. ↩
  8. The Gra on the Kuzari as kadosh v’tahor; Rav Kook at Yeshivas Merkaz HaRav. — Rabbi Eliyahu of Vilna (the Gra, 1720–1797), as recorded in his students’ transmissions, called the Kuzari kadosh v’tahor for those equipped to learn it (yod’ei da’as). HaRav Avraham Yitzchak HaKohen Kook, zt”l (1865–1935, first Ashkenazi Chief Rabbi of Eretz Yisrael, founder of Yeshivas Merkaz HaRav in Jerusalem in 1924), drew on this assessment in deliberately incorporating Kuzari as a core element of the yeshiva’s seder ha’limud — establishing the work’s central place in the dati leumi beis midrash while remaining peripheral in standard Litvish yeshivos. ↩
  9. The Cairo Genizah — recovered 1896–97 by Solomon Schechter; three letters between the Khazar court and Rav Chasdai ibn Shaprut. — The Cairo Genizah (Ben Ezra Synagogue, Fustat, Old Cairo) was recovered to Cambridge University Library in 1896–97 by Solomon Schechter with the prior reconnaissance of Margaret Gibson and Agnes Smith Lewis. Among the recovered manuscripts: three letters between the Khazar court and Rav Chasdai ibn Shaprut (c. 915–970, Cordoba; physician and diplomat to Caliph Abd al-Rahman III; patron of Rabbi Dunash ben Labrat and Rabbi Menachem ben Saruk). The Khazar correspondence — including a letter purporting to be from King Joseph himself — provides independent corroboration of the conversion narrative that opens the Kuzari. Islamic chroniclers of the same period (al-Mas’udi, Ibn Fadlan, Ibn Rusta) describe a Caucasus tribal kingdom that had adopted Judaism. While the scope of the conversion (royal court versus full population) remains scholarly debated, the basic episode is corroborated. ↩
  10. Mishnah Avos 3:14 (Rabbi Akiva) — Chavivin Yisrael she’nikre’u banim laMakom. — The Mishnah of Rabbi Akiva in the third perek of Pirkei Avos establishes the structural chibah hierarchy: every human being is beloved as tzelem Elokim; Klal Yisrael is uniquely beloved as banim laMakom. The Mishnah grounds the Acharonim’s reading that Atah Kadosh is not merely a declaration about Hashem’s separation but also an assertion of Klal Yisrael’s capacity to be u’kedoshim b’chol yom yehalleluchah selah — the same koach kedushah implanted in us by virtue of our being His banim. ↩
  11. The Rizhiner Rebbe (Rabbi Yisrael of Rizhin, 1796–1850) on Talmud Lomar in Rashi to Kedoshim Tihyu. — Rabbi Yisrael of Rizhin (Ukraine; great-grandson of the Maggid of Mezritch; founder of the Rizhin-Sadigura dynasty). The standard reading of Rashi takes Talmud Lomar “Ki Kadosh Ani” as the derashah’s corrective to the suggested equivalence: kedushasi lema’alah mi’kedushaschem. The Rizhiner reads Talmud Lomar imperatively — teach yourself to say — practice declaring ani kadosh; the sustained self-affirmation crystallizes into avodah. Preserved in the oral mesorah of his descendants and in collected derashos; precise published source not given in the shiur. ↩
  12. Anecdote of Rav Yonah of Vizhnitz — the baal habayis making a shturem over an aliyah. — Attributed to Rav Yonah of the Vizhnitz dynasty (precise generation not specified in the shiur). Preserved in chassidishe oral transmission: a baal habayis in Prague who had not received the desired aliyah made a public shturem in shul on Shabbos; the Rebbe approached him and pointed out that he had just recited V’nafshi k’afar la’kol tih’yeh (in Elokai Netzor at the close of Shemoneh Esrei) — a prayer for radical self-effacement — entirely incompatible with making a shturem over an aliyah. The story illustrates the central avodah of Atah Kadosh the Rebbe develops: closing the gap between the words on the lips and the actual matzav of the neshamah. ↩
  13. Rema, Shulchan Aruch Orach Chaim 109:1 — waiting for the Amen after HaKel HaKadosh. — The Rema in his glosses on the Shulchan Aruch rules that a yachid arriving while chazaras ha’shatz is in progress should delay starting his own Shemoneh Esrei through both the Kedushah and the close of HaKel HaKadosh — answering Amen to that berachah specifically — before beginning. The Beis Yosef records hesitation about extending the wait this far; the Rema insists on the principle that the third berachah closes the unit of shevach and the Amen on its chasimah is structurally weighty. The Mishnah Berurah there elaborates the practical applications, including the parallel pesak that this Amen may be answered mid-Krias Shema. ↩
  14. Rambam, Sefer Ahavah, Seder Tefillah — the original nusach of the third berachah with L’dor VaDor. — The Rambam in Mishneh Torah, at the close of Sefer Ahavah, prints his nusach ha’tefillah in full. The third berachah in his nusach reads L’dor VaDor Nagid Godlecha — what is today identified as Nusach Ashkenaz. The fact that the Sephardic Rambam preserved this formulation is the strongest evidence for the claim that L’dor VaDor is the original nusach ha’masorah of the silent Amidah; the Atah Kadosh V’Shimcha Kadosh formulation now standard in Nusach Sefard represents a later structural takanah to supply three Kadosh mentions where the yachid has no Kedushah. ↩
  15. Orchos Chaim (Rabbi Aharon HaKohen of Lunel) and Kol Bo — confirm L’dor VaDor as the original nusach. — Rabbi Aharon HaKohen of Lunel (late thirteenth–early fourteenth century, Provence and Mallorca), in his halachic compendium Orchos Chaim, and the closely-related anonymous Kol Bo (likely an abridgment of the same work or by a closely related author), preserve the early Provencal-Sephardic nusach and confirm L’dor VaDor as the original silent-Shemoneh Esrei formulation. Beis Yosef on Siman 112 (and parallel discussions) cites both works heavily; their joint testimony alongside the Rambam establishes the historical priority of the L’dor VaDor nusach. ↩
  16. Gemara Berachos 12b — HaMelech HaKadosh as me’akev on Aseres Yemei Teshuvah. — The sugya in Berachos establishes that the substitution of HaMelech HaKadosh for HaKel HaKadosh during Aseres Yemei Teshuvah is not merely minhag but me’akev — one who said HaKel HaKadosh in error must return to the beginning of Shemoneh Esrei. The same din applies (at Minchah) to HaMelech HaMishpat in the eleventh berachah. The severity stems from the Zohar’s reading: HaMelech is the proper formulation of kedushah when the King sits in judgment, and the substitution structurally cannot be elided. ↩
  17. Rav Avli (Aybele) Posweller (c. 1764–1836, Vilna) — chiddush on HaMelech HaKadosh at night, brought by the Chayei Adam; rejected by the Mishnah Berurah. — Lithuanian posek known principally for the chiddush brought in his name by the Chayei Adam (Rabbi Avraham Danzig, 1748–1820, Vilna; author of the Chayei Adam compendium on Orach Chaim): the chakirah whether forgetting HaMelech HaKadosh on the night of Rosh HaShanah is me’akev like the day, given the parallel din with Yaaleh V’Yavo on leil Rosh Chodesh (where forgetting is not me’akev because ein mekadshin es ha’chodesh ba’laylah). The Mishnah Berurah and most Acharonim reject the chiddush: on a regular weekday Maariv after Rosh Chodesh, the rest of the tefillah is a standard weekday davening, so a missing Yaaleh V’Yavo creates no internal contradiction; on leil Rosh HaShanah, the entire nusach is the full Yom Tov davening, and a missing HaMelech HaKadosh creates tarti d’sasri. The chiddush circulates as a classic lomdishe devar Torah before Rosh HaShanah. ↩
  18. Gemara Nedarim 41a — i da’as kanisa, mah chasarta? i da’as chasarta, mah kanisa?; ein ani ela b’da’as. — The sugya in Nedarim on the comprehensive value of da’as: if a person possesses da’as, what could he be lacking? If he lacks da’as, what could he meaningfully possess? The sugya further establishes the kelal ein ani ela b’da’as — true poverty is the absence of intellectual capacity, not the absence of material wealth. Verified on Sefaria: Nedarim 41a records both statements — Abaye said: we have a tradition, a poor person is only one lacking in intelligence; and the teaching of the West (Eretz Yisrael): if he acquired this, what else is lacking? If he has not acquired this, what has he acquired? The sugya grounds the placement of Atah Chonen as the first bakashah: every other request — parnassah, refuah, selichah, geulah — matters and functions only if da’as is in place. ↩
  19. Modern adaptations of the Kuzari — Rabbi David Nieto, Kuzari HaSheini; Rabbi Dr. Isaac Breuer, Kuzari Chadash. — Rabbi David Nieto (1654–1728, Italian-born chacham of the Spanish and Portuguese congregation in London, Bevis Marks Synagogue), Mateh Dan / Kuzari HaSheini — published 1714 in London, structured as a series of dialogues updating the Kuzari’s questions for early-modern challenges including the rise of Haskalah, the scientific revolution, and Karaism. Rabbi Dr. Isaac Breuer (1883–1946, Frankfurt and later Jerusalem; grandson of Rav Shimshon Refael Hirsch; jurist and major hashkafah writer in Agudas Yisrael; lived briefly in Washington Heights), Sefer Kuzari Chadash / Der neue Kusari — a twentieth-century philosophical-political-theological treatise structured as a dialogue, addressing the rise of nationalism, Zionism, and modern hashkafah challenges. Both works are read primarily within their respective scholarly worlds today. ↩
