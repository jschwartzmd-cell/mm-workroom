סוֹף פְּסוּקֵי דְּזִמְרָה — מַהֲרַ״ם מֵרוֹטֶנְבּוּרְג, וַיְבָרֶךְ דָּוִד וְשִׁירַת הַיָּם

The Close of Pesukei d’Zimra: Maharam of Rothenburg, Vayivarech David, and Shiras HaYam
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Sunday Morning Series on Tefillah
Recorded 1 September 2024  ·  Sefer Edition · Revised 23 August 2026

The closing of Pesukei d’Zimra is not a single moment but a layered seal: the six Halleluyos at the end of Tehillim, the Baruch Hashem L’olam cluster of the Maharam of Rothenburg, the hodaah of David HaMelech and the shirah of Ezra HaSofer, and finally Shiras HaYam as mandated by the Zohar HaKadosh. Each stratum records a specific moment in the life of Klal Yisrael: the aspiration of Rabbi Yossi, the persecution of medieval Ashkenaz, the mobilization for the first Beis HaMikdash, the return from Bavel, and the mystical reading of the exodus. To trace the seal is to trace two thousand years of mesorah.

I. The Formal Endpoint: The Halleluyos of Tehillim

The essential chiyuv of Pesukei d’Zimra resides in the six closing chapters of Sefer Tehillim — from kapitel 145 (Ashrei) through kapitel 150. The Gemara in Shabbos[1] preserves the aspiration of Rabbi Yossi:

יְהִי חֶלְקִי מִגּוֹמְרֵי הַלֵּל בְּכָל יוֹם.
“May my portion be among those who complete Hallel every day.”

Rashi on that sugya clarifies that the Hallel intended here is not the festival Hallel but the closing Halleluyos of Tehillim. Rabbi Yossi’s wish shaped the daily practice of every Jew: to complete these six chapters each morning, culminating in the crowning verse of the Sefer:

כֹּל הַנְּשָׁמָה תְּהַלֵּל יָ-הּ, הַלְלוּ-יָ-הּ.
“Let every soul praise Yah — Halleluyah.”

The verse is recited twice, in the manner preserved among the older Sephardic minhagim, to mark the formal close of the unit. This is the structural endpoint of Pesukei d’Zimra. Everything that follows — the Baruch Hashem cluster, Vayivarech David, Shiras HaYam — is an addition whose history and rationale must be traced separately.

II. Two Halachic Cautions Concerning Repetition

Before the layered additions are examined, two halachos of the boundary itself require attention. Both concern the disciplined care with which the poskim guarded the received text against inadvertent alteration in the mouth of the daily mispallel.

The Prohibition of Joining Verses Across a Boundary

In certain nusachos there arose the practice of chaining together the closing Halleluyah of one kapitel with the opening Halleluyah of the next, producing a continuous phrase in the mouth of the reciter. The poskim caution against this practice. The concern is that words which the received text keeps apart should not be joined in a way that manufactures a phrase Moshe Rabbeinu did not compose. The principle stands in the same family as the klal — “kol pasuk d’lo paskei Moshe, anan lo paskinan lei”[2] — which forbids splitting a verse Moshe did not split. Whether the concern operates by joining or by splitting, its root is one: the received boundaries of the text are not the reciter’s to move.

The same principle underlies the well-known pause before the words Yom HaShishi in the Friday-night Kiddush: a brief hum or breath separates them from the preceding Vayehi erev vayehi voker, lest the reciter appear to have manufactured a continuous phrase where the verse-boundary belongs. Some Hassidic communities preserve the chained Halleluyah practice on Kabbalistic grounds; the mainstream psak follows the caution.

The Prohibition of Repetition That Implies Two Domains

The Mishnah in Berachos[3] rules that one who repeats the words “Shema, Shema” in the manner of an invocation is silenced — the repetition implies shtei rishuyos, two dominions. The concern was born in the era in which minim and apikorsim sought to disturb the batei midrash of Chazal. Out of this concern, Chazal imposed a broad caution against needless repetition during tefillah. The Rambam may confine the strict prohibition to Shema; the majority of the poskim extend the caution more broadly.

From this halachah arises the standing hesitation among the poskim concerning chazzanim who repeat words for musical effect. A well-known illustration is preserved from the invitation extended to Rabbi Yosele Rosenblatt to lead Yamim Noraim in Belz: the Belzer Rebbe accepted his service on the single condition that no word be repeated. The chazzan, unable to bind himself to that condition, declined, and led the tefillos in a community less exacting on that point. The story preserves both the standing of the halachah and the humility with which those who served the tzibbur engaged it.

III. The Baruch Hashem Cluster

Following Kol ha’neshamah, the siddur appends a sequence of four pesukim, each opening with the formula Baruch Hashem:

  • “Baruch Hashem l’olam, amen v’amen”[4]
  • “Baruch Hashem mi’Tziyon, shochen Yerushalayim, Halleluyah”[5]
  • “Baruch Hashem Elokei Yisrael, min ha’olam v’ad ha’olam”[6]
  • “U’varuch Sheim k’vodo l’olam, v’yimalei k’vodo es kol ha’aretz, amen v’amen”[7]

These are drawn from four different kapitlach of Tehillim — 89, 135, 106, and 72. Their assembly here is neither incidental nor mikra’i: it is the enactment of a specific gadol in Ashkenaz, in response to a specific historical crisis. The figure is Rabbeinu Meir of Rothenburg, the Maharam, and the crisis is the persecution of German Jewry in the thirteenth century.

IV. The Maharam of Rothenburg

His Standing in the Mesorah

Rabbeinu Meir ben Baruch of Rothenburg — the Maharam — was born in the German lands about the year 1220 and died in captivity in the fortress of Ensisheim in Alsace in the year 1293.[8] Few figures in the Ashkenazic mesorah are more foundational. His teshuvos, his peirushim, and his kinos shape the halachic and liturgical inheritance of Ashkenaz. His rebbe was Rabbeinu Yechiel of Paris, a talmid of the Or Zarua; his own talmidim and the circle around him include the Rosh (Rabbeinu Asher ben Yechiel) and much of the next generation of Ashkenazic Rishonim. Through those channels his psakim descended into the Tur, the Beis Yosef, and the received body of Ashkenazic halachah.

The Maharam himself was among those who stood against the burning of the Talmud in Paris in the 1240s. His kinah “Sha’ali Serufah Ba’eish”[9] — sung on the morning of Tishah B’Av across Ashkenaz — is his own lament for that burning.

The Attempted Aliyah and Its Halting

In the decades following the burnings, the Maharam resolved to leave Germany and lead his family and community up to Eretz Yisrael. He joined a wider Ashkenazic movement of Ba’alei Tosafos already ascending to Yerushalayim, intending to strengthen the yishuv there. He had reached northern Italy when the journey was arrested. A Jewish apostate informed Emperor Rudolf I of Habsburg (whose reign extended from 1273 to 1291) that the Maharam was leading Jewish families out of the German lands in defiance of the imperial prohibition on Jewish emigration. Rudolf construed the departure as an act of revolt. The Maharam was seized, returned to the Empire, and imprisoned in the fortress of Ensisheim in Alsace, portions of which still stand.

Seven Years in Captivity — the Tashbatz and the Migdal Poreach

The Maharam remained a captive for the last seven years of his life. His sefarim were denied him. He obtained one concession: that a single talmid be permitted to come daily to the fortress, stand outside the window of his cell, and receive by dictation the chiddushei Torah and teshuvos which the Maharam continued to produce. That talmid was Rabbeinu Shimshon ben Tzaddok, who compiled the received material into the Sefer Tashbatz (also styled Tashbetz Katan, and not to be confused with the later Sephardic Tashbetz of Rabbeinu Shimon ben Tzemach Duran).[10] The Tashbatz stands as one of the foundational sources of Ashkenazic minhag and halachah.

In his peirush on Maseches Oholos,[11] on the perek whose theme is a migdal ha’poreach ba’avir — a tower suspended in the air — the Maharam wrote of himself with a candor rarely permitted to a gadol: he was himself a migdal poreach ba’avir, suspended in the air, cut off from his sefarim. If a Rishon were to be found disputing what he had written, he was mevatel his da’as, for he was writing without books.

The Refusal of Ransom and the Long Halachic Victory

The Maharam’s talmidim, headed by the Rosh, raised an extraordinary sum for his ransom — the tradition preserves the figure as twenty thousand marks of silver — and negotiated with the imperial authorities for his release. The Maharam refused the ransom. Were he to be redeemed at such a price, he ruled, the authorities would come to regard Jewish leaders as a source of profit and would seize and hold every rav in the Empire in expectation of ransom. He chose to remain in captivity, and died there.

His ruling entered Ashkenazic halachah as normative — a specific application of the Mishnah in Gittin forbidding ransom above the going price of captives, extended by the Maharam’s own example to the case of the gadol ha’dor whose captivity would otherwise become a standing pretext for future extortion. The historical judgment has vindicated him: after the Maharam, the German authorities did not again succeed in extorting a great rav in this manner. The war was won from within the cell.

Fourteen Years Above Ground

Even in death the Maharam did not swiftly leave the fortress. The authorities refused to release his remains for Jewish burial, and the body lay unburied within Ensisheim for many years — the tradition preserves the figure most commonly as fourteen years, though earlier accounts vary.[12] The release was finally purchased by Rabbi Alexander ben Shlomo Wimpen of Frankfurt, a wealthy Jew who dedicated an enormous sum to the ransom of the body on one personal condition: that when his own time came, he be buried beside the Maharam. The condition was honored. The Maharam of Rothenburg and Rabbi Alexander Wimpen rest today side by side in the beis ha’chaim of Vermayza (Worms).

V. The Maharam’s Takanah: Baruch Hashem L’olam

The Halachic Problem

The generation of the Maharam was one of ceaseless persecution. Imperial officers and local magistrates entered batei midrash in the middle of the shiur and mid-tefillah; refusing to answer the summons of a monarch or governor exposed the tzibbur to danger of life. The framework of halachah already accommodated such moments. The Shulchan Aruch, following the Gemara, permits and in certain cases requires hefsek within Pesukei d’Zimra for kavod ha’melech and for moreh malchus, and permits interruption to prevent hefsed meruba.[13] The two heterim are not of one order: the first is a dechuyah concern, in which danger and honor of monarchy suspend the norm; the second is a permitted response to substantial economic loss.

Yet a structural difficulty attached to any such interruption. Pesukei d’Zimra is a bracketed unit: Baruch She’amar at its head, Yishtabach at its close. To rupture the middle without a seal is to leave the brachos that frame it hanging on either side of a broken text. A new brachah could not be composed — the era of the composition of brachos had long closed. Some other closure was required.

The Mechanism of the Seal

The Maharam instituted a brief, dignified sequence of four verses drawn from Sefer Tehillim, each opening with a Baruch Hashem formulation. The sequence is not a brachah; the reciter is not enacting a new liturgical composition. Its function is more precisely defined: it is a hazkaras Sheim Shamayim — a concentrated invocation of the Divine Name in the mode of praise — assembled from the received text of Tehillim and configured to occupy the halachic position of a closing seal. The Baruch formulations mimic the register of a brachah-closure without laying claim to its formal status. On this basis the assembled verses function as a me’ein brachah: a quasi-brachah which, without violating the received body of composed brachos, provides the structural closure that the interrupted tefillah requires.

The Maharam’s ruling, therefore, is that before permissibly interrupting Pesukei d’Zimra, the reciter recites this cluster; upon returning, he recites it again as the reopening. The seal operates in both directions.

From Emergency Device to Fixed Liturgy

The Beis Yosef in Orach Chaim 51 records the Maharam’s practice as the source of the Baruch Hashem L’olam cluster preserved at the close of Pesukei d’Zimra.[14] What arose as an emergency device under medieval persecution entered the siddur as a permanent fixture. Its retention even in the absence of hefsek reflects the principle that a takanah instituted for the general community, once received, is not lightly removed; and further, that the daily recitation preserves the mechanism in readiness, so that when the moment of interruption arrives — as the Mishnah Berurah observes it may still arrive today, in response to kaddish, kedushah, or personal necessity — the reciter can complete the seal by advancing to Baruch Hashem L’olam and, if need be, resuming from there.

The Spread of the Takanah into Sephardic Practice

The Maharam’s takanah was in origin an Ashkenazic institution. It entered Sephardic practice through the mediation of the Arizal. Rabbeinu Yitzchak Luria was orphaned in his early years and taken by his mother’s family to Egypt, where he was raised in the household of his maternal uncle, Rabbi Mordechai Frances, whose daughter he later married. His father’s house was Ashkenazic (the family name Luria descends from the Rhineland); his mother’s house was Sephardic. His siddur, accordingly, drew on both streams, and the enactments of Ashkenazic gedolim which he found consistent with the teaching of the Zohar passed through his authority into Nusach Sfard. The Maharam’s cluster reached universal practice by this route.

VI. Vayivarech David

The Setting of the Passage

The verses of Vayivarech David es Hashem l’einei kol ha’kahal are drawn from Divrei HaYamim Aleph, perek 29.[15] They are not part of Sefer Tehillim. The meaning of the passage depends upon the historical moment it records.

The Foundation Waters

David HaMelech longed to build the Beis HaMikdash. He was not himself zocheh to the building — that zechus was reserved for Shlomo — but he prepared it in every particular. The Gemara in Sukkah[16] records that when David began to dig the foundations of the Mikdash, the tehom — the cosmic underground waters — rose from the depths and threatened to inundate the world. David inscribed the Sheim Hashem upon a shard of pottery and cast it into the rising waters; the tehom subsided to its place. Only then could the foundation be laid. Every stone of the Mikdash rests upon that subdual, and the words the tzibbur speaks every morning in Vayivarech David stand upon that ground.

The Successful Appeal

David then convened the leadership of Israel and conducted the appeal for the materials of the Mikdash — gold, silver, copper, cedar, and the craftsmen whose labor would join them. The response was overwhelming. Though the fulfillment of his dream had been deferred to his son, David poured his soul into the preparation, and the campaign closed with the entire nation having offered generously and joyfully. Vayivarech David is his public hodaah in the moment of that closure: “Baruch Hashem Elokei Yisrael avinu mei’olam v’ad olam … lecha Hashem ha’gedulah v’ha’gevurah v’ha’tiferes v’ha’netzach v’ha’hod…”

The passage entered the daily siddur as the archetype of tefillah-after-tefillah: the response to the moment of a completed appeal is to turn first to HaKadosh Baruch Hu in praise, before every other response. David’s example is set as the daily pattern for a nation still in galus, still praying for the return of its house.

The Minhag of Tzedakah

From this understanding of the passage arises the widespread minhag of giving tzedakah at the recitation of Vayivarech David. The Arizal, cited in the Mishnah Berurah, gave three prutos at this point in the tefillah. The Shelah HaKadosh[17] elaborates the depth of the minhag: in the recitation of Vayivarech David the reciter is not merely recalling David’s appeal but reliving it — and the reliving is completed by the pairing of word with deed. Every morning, in the age of galus, the giving of tzedakah at these verses constitutes a small daily echo of David’s national mobilization for the house of Hashem. The tzedakah is not tefillah-adjacent; it is tefillah-constitutive of the kavanah the passage demands.

VII. The Composite Passage: Divrei HaYamim and Nechemiah

The Seam in the Text

The daily unit that opens with Vayivarech David is not, in fact, a single passage. It is a composite. The opening verses are from Divrei HaYamim Aleph, but partway through the recitation the text shifts to a wholly separate passage: “Atah hu Hashem l’vadecha; atah asisa es ha’shamayim …”[18] — the opening of the shirah of the Levi’im in Nechemiah, perek 9. Behind the seam sits a second episode entirely.

The Crisis Ezra Encountered

Approximately seventy years after the Churban Bayis Rishon, the first aliyos from Bavel had begun. Ezra HaSofer remained in Bavel for a further span — the tradition preserves that his own rebbe was still teaching there, and the obligation of learning from one’s rebbe took precedence even over the mitzvah of ascending to Eretz Yisrael. When Ezra finally ascended, he found a spiritual crisis of grave dimension. The Jews who had returned had entered widely into intermarriage with the surrounding nations. The count preserved in Ezra, perek 10, records roughly 113 individuals who had taken foreign wives, and the ranks are the graver part of the account: among the intermarried were kohanim, levi’im, and sarim. Had the pattern continued unbroken, the return from Bavel would have dissolved into the surrounding population within a generation.

The Convocation and the Mass Teshuvah

Ezra had been appointed by the Persian king as the recognized Jewish authority, with full royal backing for his enactments. He convened the entire tzibbur in Yerushalayim and decreed that whoever failed to appear would forfeit his property. The assembly gathered. Ezra rose before them with a Sefer Torah, tore his garments in mourning, and wept, and the tzibbur wept with him. In the course of a single afternoon — the first mass teshuvah movement preserved in the sources — those who had entered into intermarriage separated from their non-Jewish wives, and the covenant was renewed.

Ezra then commanded the Levi’im to sing. The teshuvah gave birth immediately to shirah. The pesukim the Levi’im sang are preserved in Nechemiah, perek 9, and it is those pesukim which the tzibbur recites daily, embedded within the framework of Vayivarech David.

The Two Pillars

The stitching of the two passages — whose editorial precedent is traceable already to the earliest arrangements of the tefillah preserved in Ashkenaz and among the Ba’alei Tosafos — is not setam. It joins the two archetypal moments in the life of Klal Yisrael: David’s mobilization for the building of the Mikdash, and Ezra’s separation from that which corrodes it. Building and boundary. Both are preconditions of geulah; neither suffices without the other. Every morning the tzibbur reattaches itself to both, in a daily covenantal renewal of the two impulses that make the return possible.

VIII. The Five Sefarim of Tehillim and the Anomaly at the Close

The Midrash teaches[19] that Sefer Tehillim is composed of five sefarim, corresponding to the five chumashim of the Torah:

כְּשֵׁם שֶׁנָּתַן מֹשֶׁה חֲמִשָּׁה חוּמְשֵׁי תּוֹרָה לְיִשְׂרָאֵל, כָּךְ דָּוִד נָתַן חֲמִשָּׁה סְפָרִים בְּתְהִלִּים.
“Just as Moshe gave Israel five Books of Torah, so David gave Israel five Books in Tehillim.”

Certain siddurim print, after the recitation of Kol ha’neshamah, four further verses whose purpose is to complete symbolically the five books of Tehillim in the course of a single morning tefillah. Since Kol ha’neshamah is itself the closing verse of Sefer 5, the addition of the closing verses of Sefarim 1 through 4 would complete the arc.

Several Rishonim observed a difficulty in the received arrangement: only two of the four appended verses are, in fact, the closing verses of their respective books. The four verses commonly printed are the closing verse of Sefer 2 (Tehillim 72:19), the closing verse of Sefer 3 (Tehillim 89:53), the closing verse of Sefer 4 (Tehillim 106:48), and a fourth verse of similar cadence — but the verse offered in place of the closing verse of Sefer 1 is a similar-sounding verse from elsewhere in Tehillim, not the true closing verse of that book (Tehillim 41:14). The confusion appears to derive from the closeness in wording of the two verses, both of which conclude with a doubled amen v’amen.

Some Rishonim held the substitution to be genuinely incorrect; others accepted the received arrangement, holding that the symbolic completion is achieved by the number of the verses and their liturgical placement rather than by strict textual identity. The mesorah has retained the received arrangement without emendation — a reflection of the general principle that the received text of tefillah, once fixed by generations of use, is not lightly corrected, even where a difficulty is acknowledged. The very persistence of the anomaly is a small witness to the caution with which the poskim approached the received liturgical text.

IX. Shiras HaYam

The Structural Question

The recitation of Shiras HaYam after Baruch Hashem L’olam and before Yishtabach presents, strictly, a structural difficulty. Pesukei d’Zimra is a bracketed unit sealed by Yishtabach; the introduction of an extended passage from the Chumash before that closure is at least on the surface a hefsek. The Rishonim divided on the question.

The Geonim

The siddurim of the Geonim — both Siddur Rav Amram Gaon and Siddur Rav Saadia Gaon[20] — do not include Shiras HaYam in the daily tefillah. Both write expressly that a community wishing to recite it on Shabbos and Yom Tov may do so, but the recitation is not required, and one who omits it is not to be protested. The two great yeshivos of Sura and Pumbedisa — whose leadership constituted the halachic backbone of the Geonic period — preserved the tefillah in its stricter, unadorned form.

The Rambam

The Rambam, in Hilchos Tefillah,[21] rules that Shiras HaYam may be recited daily as part of one’s morning devotions, but should be positioned after Yishtabach rather than before, in order to preserve the integrity of the brachah-frame of Pesukei d’Zimra. The Rambam’s halachic instinct is precisely the structural one: the concern of hefsek within the frame is real, and the natural resolution is to move the addition outside the frame. No community, however, follows the Rambam’s ordering in practice.

The Zohar and the Arizal

The Zohar HaKadosh, in its treatment of Parshas Beshalach,[22] requires the daily recitation of Shiras HaYam al pi sod — and requires its recitation in the position preceding Yishtabach, not following it. The Arizal systematized this psak into universal daily practice.

The Rambam’s structural objection is answered on its own terms rather than merely overridden. The Kabbalistic poskim hold that Shiras HaYam, once mandated daily by the Zohar, is not a foreign body inserted into Pesukei d’Zimra but constitutes part of the shevach unit itself — a further layer of the very praise of which Pesukei d’Zimra consists. On this understanding no hefsek arises, since the recitation is tzorech ha’tefillah: an extension of the closing praise, not an interruption of it. The Rambam and the Zohar are not merely two competing rulings; they are two characterizations of what Shiras HaYam is. Once the Zohar’s characterization was received — through the authority of the Arizal in matters of the siddur, whose psak Klal Yisrael extended a degree of trust exceeding the ordinary protocols of pesika — the structural objection ceased to apply.

The Daily Zechirah of Yetzias Mitzrayim

A further consideration reinforces the practice on its own nigleh terms. There is a daily mitzvah of zechiras yetzias Mitzrayim, whose primary fulfillment occurs at Kriyas Shema. Kriyas Yam Suf is the climax of the exodus. The recitation of Shiras HaYam in the morning tefillah, before Shacharis proper begins, deepens and reinforces that daily zechirah.

X. Koach HaDimyon: Rabbeinu Avraham ben HaRambam on the Sanctified Imagination

The Question of Kavanah

The Mishnah Berurah, drawing on the Chida[23] and, behind him, on the Arizal, records that the daily recitation of Shiras HaYam with proper kavanah is a segulah for parnasah. Yet the nature of the required kavanah is not self-evident. The Hebrew of Shiras HaYam contains some of the most difficult verses in the entire Chumash; even with Rashi, full comprehension of every word requires hours of study, and the daily mispallel cannot be presumed to have achieved it. If the segulah depends upon linguistic mastery, its promise is closed to nearly all who recite the shirah. Some other understanding of kavanah must be sought.

Rabbeinu Avraham ben HaRambam

The Rambam’s son, Rabbeinu Avraham ben HaRambam, was a talmid chacham and posek of the first rank, whose stature is often obscured by the immensity of his father’s. His principal work, HaMaspik L’Ovdei Hashem,[24] is a systematic treatise on avodas Hashem. In it Rabbeinu Avraham develops a teaching almost unique in classical machshavah: the disciplined use of the imagination — koach ha’dimyon — as an instrument of avodas Hashem.

The imagination is not, in his account, a wandering faculty to be suppressed; it is a spiritual capacity to be trained. He gives practical examples:

  • The moments before sleep, in which the mind wanders unbidden, are to be given over to the visualization of the Beis HaMikdash — the Kohanim bringing the korbanos, the Levi’im singing upon the duchan, Klal Yisrael gathered in Yerushalayim in ma’amados. The imagination fills with sanctified images what would otherwise be filled with idle ones.
  • The arrival of Mashiach is to be visualized in its detail — not as fantasy but as avodah that fortifies hope.
  • For the recitation of Shiras HaYam, the reciter is to place himself — in the eye of the imagination — upon the dry seabed, with the walls of water towering on either side, the Egyptian host drowning behind him, the gold and silver of Egypt washing up upon the shore, and Klal Yisrael emerging toward Har Sinai.

The Kavanah Unlocked

This is the kavanah to which the Chida and the Arizal point. The segulah of Shiras HaYam is not unlocked by linguistic mastery but by sanctified visualization. The daily mitzvah of reliving the exodus is not fulfilled by the intellect alone; it is fulfilled by the whole faculty of the soul, of which the imagination is a proper part. Shiras HaYam is placed in the morning tefillah precisely because the moment of daily tefillah is the moment given to that fulfillment.

An Application to the Present Age

The principle of Rabbeinu Avraham has a particular urgency in a time saturated with external input. The discipline of silence — of a mind not immediately given over to voice, music, or podcast — is presupposed by the exercise of koach ha’dimyon. The first act of the sanctified imagination is the closing of the input that would otherwise crowd it out. That first act is available to every Jew, in the interval before davening and in the moments before sleep, without further preparation.

XI. Synthesis

The close of Pesukei d’Zimra is a seal composed of layers, and each layer records a specific moment in the life of Klal Yisrael. The Halleluyos close the formal unit and fulfill the aspiration of Rabbi Yossi to be counted among those who complete Hallel every day. The Baruch Hashem L’olam cluster is the imprint of the Maharam of Rothenburg’s captivity upon the daily tefillah of every Jew — a me’ein brachah assembled to seal the tefillah against the violence of medieval Ashkenaz, and preserved in the siddur long after the immediate necessity had passed. Vayivarech David attaches the daily tefillah to David HaMelech’s appeal for the Mikdash and to Ezra HaSofer’s shirah of renewed covenant — building and boundary, the two pillars of every geulah. Shiras HaYam, mandated daily by the Zohar HaKadosh and received into universal practice through the authority of the Arizal, transforms the morning tefillah into a daily reliving of yetzias Mitzrayim.

Running through the whole is the teaching of Rabbeinu Avraham ben HaRambam: that tefillah is not the recitation of correct words alone, but the disciplined labor of the sanctified imagination — a faculty largely forgotten in the present age, which the daily tefillos, in their assembled depth, were designed to restore.

  2. Berachos י״ב ע״ב (12b); Ta’anis 27b. — כָּל פָּסוּק דְּלֹא פַסְקֵיהּ מֹשֶׁה, אֲנַן לֹא פַסְקִינַן לֵיהּ. The primary formulation concerns not splitting a verse Moshe did not split; the extension to not joining across a verse-boundary is drawn from the same principle of fidelity to the received textual boundaries. The pause before Yom HaShishi in Friday-night Kiddush is the practical analogue. ↩
  3. Berachos 33b — Mishnah: the silencing of one who repeats “Shema, Shema”. — The structural anti-shtei rishuyos halachah. The Rambam may confine the strict prohibition to Shema; the majority of poskim extend the caution more broadly, and on its basis discourage chazzanim from repeating words for musical effect. ↩
  4. Tehillim 89:53. — בָּרוּךְ ה׳ לְעוֹלָם, אָמֵן וְאָמֵן. Closing verse of Sefer 3 of Tehillim; the first of the four Baruch Hashem verses in the Maharam’s cluster. ↩
  5. Tehillim 135:21. — בָּרוּךְ ה׳ מִצִּיּוֹן שֹׁכֵן יְרוּשָׁלָיִם הַלְלוּ־יָהּ. The second of the four. ↩
  6. Tehillim 106:48. — בָּרוּךְ ה׳ אֱלֹהֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל מִן הָעוֹלָם וְעַד הָעוֹלָם. Closing verse of Sefer 4; the third of the four. Note that the received text and the pasuk read min ha’olam, not mei’olam. ↩
  7. Tehillim 72:19. — וּבָרוּךְ שֵׁם כְּבוֹדוֹ לְעוֹלָם וְיִמָּלֵא כְבוֹדוֹ אֶת כֹּל הָאָרֶץ אָמֵן וְאָמֵן. Closing verse of Sefer 2; the fourth of the four. ↩
  8. Historical sources on Rabbeinu Meir of Rothenburg, Emperor Rudolf I of Habsburg (r. 1273–1291), and the fortress of Ensisheim in Alsace (general historical reference). — The biographical narrative of the Maharam — the apostate informer, the imperial decree against Jewish emigration, the arrest in northern Italy, the imprisonment at Ensisheim, the death in captivity in 1293, and the eventual ransom of the body by R’ Alexander ben Shlomo Wimpen of Frankfurt — is reconstructed from medieval chronicles and modern historical scholarship rather than from a single textual siman. Standard treatments are found in the introductions to the Shu’t Maharam in the modern editions. ↩
  9. Maharam of Rothenburg, Sha’ali Serufah Ba’eish — kinah recited on Tishah B’Av. — The Maharam’s lament for the burning of the Talmud in Paris (1242), composed in the wake of his own active defense against the burnings. Sung universally in Ashkenaz on the morning of Tishah B’Av; standard kinah 41 in most printings. ↩
  10. Rabbeinu Shimshon ben Tzaddok, Sefer Tashbatz (Tashbetz Katan). — The compilation of the Maharam’s teshuvos, minhagim, and chiddushim, received at the Ensisheim window during the years of the Maharam’s captivity. A foundational source of Ashkenazic halachah. Not to be confused with the later Sephardic Tashbetz of Rabbeinu Shimon ben Tzemach Duran (post-1391). ↩
  11. Maharam of Rothenburg, peirush on Maseches Oholos, on the perek “Migdal HaPoreach Ba’avir” (general reference). — The Maharam’s peirush survives in fragments; the self-identification with a migdal poreach ba’avir — suspended in air, cut off from his sefarim, and preemptively mevatel his da’as to any Rishon who might disagree — is preserved in that peirush and cited widely in the later literature. ↩
  12. Duration of the Maharam’s unburied captivity (historical variance). — The historical sources preserve the interval between the Maharam’s death and R’ Alexander Wimpen’s ransom of his remains variously as seven, eight, or fourteen years. The figure of fourteen years is the most common in the received tradition and is retained here; the variance is a genuine feature of the historical record and not a matter of halachic psak. ↩
  13. Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chaim 51, 53, 65 — laws of hefsek in Pesukei d’Zimra; Mishnah Berurah ad loc. — The framework of permitted interruptions in Pesukei d’Zimra: kavod ha’melech, moreh malchus, hefsed meruba, and the responses of kaddish and kedushah. The two categories differ in halachic register — kavod-and-moreh as a dechuyah concern, hefsed as a permitted response to loss — and the Maharam’s takanah of Baruch Hashem L’olam arose against the backdrop of both under the conditions of medieval persecution. ↩
  14. Beis Yosef, Orach Chaim 51, citing the Maharam; Shulchan Aruch ad loc.; Mishnah Berurah. — The codification of the Maharam’s takanah: the Baruch Hashem L’olam cluster as a me’ein brachah structural seal against permitted hefsek in Pesukei d’Zimra. What began as an emergency device entered the siddur as a permanent fixture. The Mishnah Berurah preserves the operative psak for the contemporary reciter: at a permitted hefsek mid-Halleluyos, advance to Baruch Hashem L’olam before interrupting. ↩
  15. Divrei HaYamim Aleph 29:10–13. — David HaMelech’s hodaah at the public moment of the successful national appeal for the materials of the Beis HaMikdash: בָּרוּךְ ה׳ אֱלֹהֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל אָבִינוּ מֵעוֹלָם וְעַד עוֹלָם … לְךָ ה׳ הַגְּדֻלָּה וְהַגְּבוּרָה. The archetype of tefillah-after-tefillah. ↩
  16. Sukkah נ״ג ע״א-ב (53a–b). — David HaMelech at the digging of the foundations of the Beis HaMikdash; the rising of the tehom and the threat of world-flooding; the inscription of the Sheim Hashem upon a shard of pottery and its casting into the waters to subdue them. The aggadeta upon which the very ground of the Mikdash — and the words of Vayivarech David — rest. ↩
  17. Shelah HaKadosh, Shnei Luchos HaBris; cf. Pri Etz Chaim, Sha’ar HaTefillah; Mishnah Berurah 51:19. — The minhag of giving tzedakah at Vayivarech David. The Arizal gave three prutos; the Shelah explains the depth of the minhag as a pairing of word with deed — a reliving of David’s communal mobilization for the Mikdash that becomes tefillah-constitutive rather than tefillah-adjacent. ↩
  18. Nechemiah 9:6 ff. — אַתָּה הוּא ה׳ לְבַדֶּךָ, אַתָּה עָשִׂיתָ אֶת הַשָּׁמַיִם. The shirah of the Levi’im following Ezra’s mass teshuvah: the convocation of Klal Yisrael in Yerushalayim, the separation from intermarriage among kohanim, levi’im, and sarim, and the immediate outbreak of shirah. The second pillar of the daily Vayivarech David composite. ↩
  19. Midrash Tehillim 1:2. — The teaching that Sefer Tehillim is structured into five sefarim paralleling the five chumashim: כְּשֵׁם שֶׁנָּתַן מֹשֶׁה חֲמִשָּׁה חוּמְשֵׁי תּוֹרָה לְיִשְׂרָאֵל, כָּךְ דָּוִד נָתַן חֲמִשָּׁה סְפָרִים בְּתְהִלִּים. The five books close respectively at Tehillim 41:14, 72:19, 89:53, 106:48, and 150:6. ↩
  20. Siddur Rav Amram Gaon; Siddur Rav Saadia Gaon. — The two foundational Geonic siddurim, neither of which includes Shiras HaYam in the daily tefillah. Both write expressly that a community wishing to recite it on Shabbos and Yom Tov may do so, that the recitation is not required, and that one who omits it is not to be protested. ↩
  21. Rambam, Hilchos Tefillah, perek 7 (Seder HaTefillah). — The Rambam’s psak: Shiras HaYam may be recited daily as part of one’s morning devotions, but should be positioned after Yishtabach, not before, in order to preserve the integrity of the brachah-frame of Pesukei d’Zimra. No community follows this ordering in practice. ↩
  22. Zohar, Parshas Beshalach (general reference; Zohar II in standard editions). — The Zohar HaKadosh’s mandate of the daily recitation of Shiras HaYam al pi sod, in the position preceding Yishtabach. The Arizal systematized this psak into universal daily practice. The reference is general to the treatment of the parshah in the Zohar and not to a single pinned daf. ↩
  23. Chida, Moreh Ba’Etzba; Mishnah Berurah 51:17. — The teaching that Shiras HaYam recited with kavanah is a segulah for parnasah. Rooted in the Arizal, codified by the Chida, brought into general practice by the Mishnah Berurah. The kavanah in question is unlocked not by linguistic mastery but by Rabbeinu Avraham ben HaRambam’s koach ha’dimyon. ↩
  24. Rabbeinu Avraham ben HaRambam, HaMaspik L’Ovdei Hashem (Judeo-Arabic original; standard Hebrew translations). — The sefer in which Rabbeinu Avraham develops koach ha’dimyon as a discipline of avodas Hashem: the visualization of the Beis HaMikdash before sleep, the picturing of the arrival of Mashiach, the placing of oneself upon the dry seabed at Kriyas Yam Suf. A teaching almost unique in classical machshavah, and the practical key to the kavanah of Shiras HaYam. ↩
Sefer Edition of Rabbi Ephrati’s Sunday-morning shiur on Tefillah, recorded 1 September 2024. This edition incorporates revisions drawn from the Shiur Review and Editorial Review of 23 August 2026: the mechanism of the Maharam’s me’ein brachah seal has been articulated; the Rambam / Zohar tension on Shiras HaYam has been resolved on its own halachic terms; the two-and-two anomaly in the closing-pesukim of the five sefarim has been specified; the received text of Tehillim 106:48 has been corrected to min ha’olam; the ransom figure has been corrected to twenty thousand marks silver; the Arizal’s upbringing has been corrected to his maternal uncle’s household in Egypt; the Ezra intermarriage figure has been given as roughly 113 per Ezra 10; the Maharam’s death has been dated to 1293; and the “Rottenberg” variant has been normalized.
