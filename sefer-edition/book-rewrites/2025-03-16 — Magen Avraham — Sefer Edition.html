מָגֵן אַבְרָהָם — נִשְׁמַת הַבְּרָכָה הָרִאשׁוֹנָה

Magen Avraham: The Soul of the First Berachah
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Sunday Morning Series on Tefillah
Recorded 16 March 2025 (Purim Katan)  ·  Sefer Edition · Revised 30 August 2026

A single word stands at the head of the שמונה עשרה (Shemoneh Esrei) — “מגן אברהם” — and behind it three ranges of Jewish thought converge: the חסידישע (chassidishe) reading of the last generation, the halakhic-eschatological vision of the future avodah, and the messianic universalism of the uncensored Rambam. What follows is an attempt to draw them together.

I. The Puzzle of the Chasimah

The Source in Pesachim

The Gemara (גמרא) in Pesachim (פסחים) 117b[1] derives the entire structure of Birkas Avos (ברכת אבות) — the first berachah (ברכה) of the Shemoneh Esrei — from the four clauses of Hashem’s opening promise to Avraham Avinu (אברהם אבינו) at the beginning of Parshas Lech Lecha (פרשת לך לך):

וְאֶעֶשְׂךָ לְגוֹי גָּדוֹל, וַאֲבָרֶכְךָ, וַאֲגַדְּלָה שְׁמֶךָ, וֶהְיֵה בְּרָכָה.
I shall make you a great nation; and I shall bless you; and I shall make your name great; and be a blessing.
Bereshis 12:2, cited by Rashi (רש"י) ad loc.[2]

The Gemara reads each clause as a future tefillah (תפילה):

  • “ואעשך לגוי גדול” — this is the “אלקי אברהם” of the berachah.
  • “ואברכך” — this is “אלקי יצחק.”
  • “ואגדלה שמך” — this is “אלקי יעקב.”
  • “והיה ברכה” — through this you, Avraham, shall become a berachah.

The natural expectation is that the chasimah (חתימה) — the sealing formula — would name all three Avos: “ברוך אתה ה' מגן אברהם, יצחק, ויעקב.” The Gemara, however, rules otherwise:

וּבְךָ חוֹתְמִין, וְלֹא בְּכֻלָּם.
With you we seal — and not with all of them.
Pesachim 117b

The Question Behind the Ruling

Why? Why does the sealing of the first berachah single out Avraham alone, when the three Avos are named in a single breath two words earlier? This chapter offers three mehalchim (מהלכים) — three angles of approach — each drawn from a distinct stream in the tradition: the chassidishe reading of the Kotzker (קאצקער) with its antecedent in the Megaleh Amukos (מגלה עמוקות); the halakhic-eschatological reading of the Tif’eres Banim (תפארת בנים) of Munkács, riding on a face-value Radak (רד"ק); and the messianic-universal reading of the Maharam Schick (מהר"ם שיק), anchored in the uncensored Rambam (רמב"ם) at the close of Hilchos Melachim (הלכות מלכים). Each reading widens the frame of the previous one; together, as will be argued in the closing chapter, they converge on a single reading of the word “ובך” — with you.

II. Body and Soul of Tefillah

Before entering the three mehalchim, a brief reminder of the framework within which they operate. The words of the Shemoneh Esrei, established by the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah (אנשי כנסת הגדולה), are the guf (גוף) — the body — of tefillah. Kavvanah (כוונה) is the neshamah (נשמה). The Zohar HaKadosh (זוהר הקדוש)[3] repeatedly warns that tefillah without kavvanah is “כגוף בלי נשמה” — a body without a soul; the mekubalim (מקובלים), notably the Ramak (רמ"ק) in Pardes Rimonim (פרדס רימונים), explain the Zohar’s frightening image of empty tefillos being cast to the chitzonim (חיצונים) and kelipos (קליפות).

The Gemara in Rosh HaShanah (ראש השנה) 18a[4] gives the practical corollary: two people daven “רפאנו”; one is healed, the other is not. Why? Tefillah shleimah (תפילה שלמה) versus tefillah she’einah shleimah — a complete tefillah versus an incomplete one; Rashi: with kavvanah versus without. Kavvanah is decisive. But the focus of this chapter is on the words themselves — what they mean, from what depths of the tradition they were drawn.

III. Mahalach A — The Kotzker: A Generation of Chesed

The famous vort from the Kotzker Rebbe, Rabbi Menachem Mendel of Kotzk (רבי מנחם מנדל מקאצק, 1787–1859), whose antecedent is found already in the Megaleh Amukos of Rabbi Nosson Nota Shapiro of Kraków (רבי נתן נטע שפירא, 1585–1633)[5]: there is a remez (רמז) — a hint — embedded in the Gemara’s ruling.

The three Avos are the three amudim (עמודים) — the three pillars on which the spiritual life of Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל) rests:

  • Avraham Avinu — the pillar of chesed (חסד).
  • Yitzchak Avinu (יצחק אבינו) — the pillar of gevurah (גבורה).
  • Yaakov Avinu (יעקב אבינו) — the pillar of tif’eres (תפארת), the integration of the two, the pillar of Torah.

Different generations of Klal Yisrael have leaned on different pillars — ages of martyrological gevurah, ages of towering Torah scholarship, ages of open-handed chesed. In the Kotzker’s reading, Chazal (חז"ל) are intimating something specific about the final generation, the generation that ushers in the coming of Mashiach (משיח): it will be the dor (דור) of Avraham Avinu, the dor ha-chesed (דור החסד). “ובך חותמין” — with you, Avraham, with your middah of chesed, will we close the galus (גלות).

If one had any awareness of the volume of tzedakah (צדקה) given in the two or three days surrounding Purim (פורים) — hundreds of millions of dollars across the Jewish communities of the world, in a single burst — the sheer scale is staggering. The generation living today experiences chesed on a scale unprecedented in Jewish history: institutions of tzedakah and chessed, medical societies, gemachs, hospitality networks that reach into every corner of the Jewish world. This, the Kotzker teaches, is the matbe’a (מטבע) — the currency — of the final geulah (גאולה) promised to Avraham Avinu.

IV. Mahalach B — The Tif’eres Banim: Only Shacharis in the Future

The Author

The Tif’eres Banim[6] is the work of the Munkatcher Rebbe, Rabbi Tzvi Hirsh Shapira of Munkács (רבי צבי הירש שפירא ממונקאטש, 1850–1913), a grandson of the Bnei Yissaschar (בני יששכר, Rabbi Tzvi Elimelech of Dinov, 1783–1841 — one of the foundational sifrei chassidus). Munkács, in what is today western Ukraine, was the seat of a chassidus dynasty whose rebbes were not only great chassidim and mekubalim but also major poskim (פוסקים). Rabbi Tzvi Hirsh authored the encyclopedic Darchei Teshuvah (דרכי תשובה) on Yoreh De’ah (יורה דעה) — the primary reference for any bachur (בחור) who has learned basar be-chalav (בשר וחלב) or ta’aroves (תערובות). His Tif’eres Banim on Chumash contains a chiddush (חידוש) on Parshas Lech Lecha that stands among the most striking readings of “ובך חותמין” in the entire literature.

The Pesukim in Yechezkel

The navi (נביא) Yechezkel (יחזקאל) was given the great vision of the Bayis Shelishi (בית שלישי), the future Beis HaMikdash (בית המקדש); the vision is read as the haftarah of Parshas HaChodesh (פרשת החודש).[7] The nevuah (נבואה) contains many sesirot (סתירות) with the arrangements of the first and second Temples — how the kohanim (כהנים) dress, how they conduct themselves; the Rishonim (ראשונים) labor at length to reconcile.

One pasuk stands out. In describing the daily korban (קרבן), Yechezkel 46:13–15[8] mentions the tamid shel shachar (תמיד של שחר) — “בבקר בבקר”, morning after morning — and does not mention a tamid shel bein ha’arbayim (תמיד של בין הערבים).

The Radak’s Reading

Most mefarshim (מפרשים) assimilate the pasuk to the familiar structure of korban tamid (קרבן תמיד) — some speak of korbanos milu’im (קרבנות מילואים) at the inauguration of the future Beis HaMikdash, others suggest textual ellipsis. The Radak, Rabbi David Kimchi (רבי דוד קמחי, 1160–1235, Narbonne in Provence)[9] — one of the great mefarshei Nach (מפרשי נ"ך) among the Rishonim — breaks with this. He reads the pasuk at face value: in the Bayis Shelishi, there will be only a tamid shel shachar, and no tamid shel bein ha’arbayim.

The Implication for Tefillah

The Gemara in Berachos (ברכות) 26b[10] teaches the great principle:

תְּפִלּוֹת כְּנֶגֶד תְּמִידִין תִּקְּנוּם.
The daily tefillos were instituted parallel to the temidin.
  • Shacharis (שחרית) corresponds to the tamid shel shachar.
  • Minchah (מנחה) corresponds to the tamid shel bein ha’arbayim.
  • Ma’ariv (מעריב) corresponds to the eivarim and pedarim (אברים ופדרים) consumed on the mizbe’ach (מזבח) through the night.

Follow the implication. If in the future there is no tamid shel bein ha’arbayim, there is no anchor for Minchah and no anchor for Ma’ariv — only Shacharis.

Why? “Ha-Laylah Ka-Yom Ya’ir”

The reason is disclosed in the pesukim. David HaMelech (דוד המלך) declares in Tehillim (תהילים) 139:12[11]:

גַּם חֹשֶׁךְ לֹא יַחְשִׁיךְ מִמֶּךָּ, וְלַיְלָה כַּיּוֹם יָאִיר; כַּחֲשֵׁכָה כָּאוֹרָה.
Even darkness does not conceal from You; night shines like day; darkness is as light.

Yeshayahu (ישעיהו) 60:19–20[12] extends the vision to the future world itself: “לא יבוא עוד שמשך” — your sun shall no longer set. If in the future world the sun does not set, there is no transition between day and night, no bein ha’arbayim, no zeman (זמן) for an evening korban. Therefore only Shacharis.

The Remez in Pesachim

Here is the Tif’eres Banim’s chiddush. Chazal record in Berachos:

  • Avraham Avinu instituted Shacharis.
  • Yitzchak Avinu instituted Minchah.
  • Yaakov Avinu instituted Ma’ariv.

“ובך חותמין” — in the future, only Avraham’s tefillah will remain. Yitzchak’s Minchah and Yaakov’s Ma’ariv are the tefillos of galus; the tefillah of geulah, of the age of the Bayis Shelishi, is Avraham’s alone.

Confirmation in the Verbal Tense

The Sforno (ספורנו) and other Rishonim notice a precise pattern in the pesukim establishing each of the three tefillos:

  • Of Avraham in Parshas Vayera (פרשת וירא): “ויהי עמד לפני ה'”[13]. Rashi: “אין עמידה אלא תפילה.” The form “עמד” is a participle — a continuous, eternal present. Standing, always.
  • Of Yitzchak: “ויצא יצחק לשוח בשדה לפנות ערב”[14] — the vav ha-hipuch (וו ההיפוך) narrative past.
  • Of Yaakov: “ויפגע במקום, וילן שם”[15] — again the narrative past.

Minchah and Ma’ariv are placed grammatically in the past — they are the tefillos of the historical unfolding of galus. Avraham’s Shacharis alone is set in the eternal present. The very grammar of the Chumash (חומש) confirms the chiddush.

Reconciling with “Lo Sishtaneh Ha-Torah”

The chiddush as stated confronts a principle of first order: “לא תשתנה התורה” — the Torah does not change. The Rambam establishes this as one of the Ani Ma’amin; the Ra’avad (ראב"ד)[16] disputes with the Rambam over whether nature itself will change le’asid la’vo (לעתיד לבוא) — but even according to the Rambam, the Torah stands unaltered. How can the Tif’eres Banim’s chiddush hold?

The stronger reconciliation is by way of the Ramban (רמב"ן) and the well-known drasha (דרשה) that “למה נקרא שמו חזיר? שעתיד הקדוש ברוך הוא להחזירו לישראל”[17]. The Ramban’s reading is not, chas ve-shalom (חס ושלום), that the Torah’s rules of kashrus (כשרות) will change. The rule is stable: the simanei kashrus (סימני כשרות) of maflis parsah u-ma’aleh gerah (מפריס פרסה ומעלה גרה) remain. The animal develops. Applied to Yechezkel 46: the halakhos (הלכות) of tefillah and the correspondence to temidin remain intact; the world changes — the daily cycle of light and dark reconfigures — and with the disappearance of bein ha’arbayim as a zeman, the second tamid has no metzius (מציאות) in which to be brought. The Torah has not changed; the world’s daily clock has.

A supplementary reconciliation follows the Rambam’s own count. In Sefer HaMitzvos (ספר המצוות) and at the head of Hilchos Temidin U-Musafin (הלכות תמידין ומוספין)[18], the Rambam counts korban tamid as one mitzvah, not two. In Bayis Rishon and Bayis Sheini, the one mitzvah was discharged twice daily; in Bayis Shelishi, it will be discharged once. The mitzvah is the same; only its frequency of execution shifts.

V. Mahalach C — The Maharam Schick: A Tikkun for All of Zera Avraham

The third mehalach comes from Rabbi Moshe Schick (רבי משה שיק, 1809–1879), the Maharam Schick[19], foremost talmid of the Chasam Sofer (חתם סופר) and rosh yeshivah (ראש ישיבה) in Huszt of a yeshiva of eight hundred talmidim. It is a profound and difficult reading, and it takes some patience to absorb; but for anyone who cares about kevod Shamayim (כבוד שמים) and the long arc of tikkun ha-olam (תיקון העולם), it repays close attention.

The Arithmetic of Zera Avraham

Trace the lineage of Avraham Avinu. He was the rosh ha-ma’aminim (ראש המאמינים), the one who — as the Rambam describes in Hilchos Avodah Zarah (הלכות עבודה זרה)[20] — traveled town to town and family to family proclaiming the emes (אמת) of the one Creator. He had two sons:

  • Yishmael (ישמעאל) — a whole branch of Avraham’s descendants, later the seed of much of the Islamic world.
  • Yitzchak — and Yitzchak too had two sons:
    • Eisav (עשו) — Chazal: “ישראל מומר”, raised in the house of Yitzchak, and yet lost to the mission.
    • Yaakov — the “מטה שלמה”, from whom emerged the y’"b shivtei Kah (י"ב שבטי י-ה) and the Klal Yisrael we know.

Considered by branches, the majority of Avraham’s line departed from his mission within two generations: three of the four branches at the level of the grandchildren. If one had approached Avraham Avinu at his bris (ברית) and disclosed to him this outcome, would he have wanted to marry at all?

Chizkiyahu HaMelech (חזקיהו המלך) — the Gemara in Berachos 10a records — refused to marry, having seen through ruach ha-kodesh (רוח הקודש) that he would father wayward children. Yeshayahu HaNavi (ישעיהו הנביא) had to prevail upon him with the ruling that a person is not the master of divine calculations, and must fulfill his obligation. Chizkiyahu married Yeshayahu’s daughter; his son Menasheh did indeed go astray. The reluctance was real; the halakhah still required the marriage.

The Promise to Avraham

The Maharam Schick, drawing on the Zohar HaKadosh, teaches: Avraham Avinu received from HaKadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא) a promise that ultimately all of his descendants will be gathered back to the truth of the one God. The tikkun (תיקון) is not for the surviving Yaakov mateh shleimah alone. This is what Yeshayahu 2:2–3 envisions when he prophesies “ונהרו אליו כל הגוים” — that all the nations shall stream to the mountain of Hashem; and it is what Klal Yisrael davens for on Rosh HaShanah when we ask that the entire world recognize the sovereignty of the one God.

The Mechanism — Gilgulim of Eisav and Yishmael

The Arizal (האר"י ז"ל), through his foremost talmid Rabbi Chaim Vital (רבי חיים ויטאל, 1542–1620), teaches in Etz HaDa’as Tov (עץ הדעת טוב) at the close of Parshas Re’eh (פרשת ראה)[21] a kabbalistic mesorah (מסורה):

  • The founder of Christianity is spiritually the gilgul (גלגול) of Eisav. The two names differ by only a single letter (י in place of ע) — a mnemonic pointer to the deeper spiritual identification.
  • The founder of Islam is spiritually the gilgul of Yishmael.

Rabbi Chaim Vital further preserves the tradition that Islam’s founder had a Jewish teacher, and records the dream of a great tree growing from his hand and giving shade to all the world — a dream interpreted for him by a Jewish sage as the summons to prophethood. Whatever historical form the tradition takes, its theological point is this: influences of Torah passed, through unexpected conduits, into the two great non-Jewish monotheisms.

Shituf and the Radbaz

The Radbaz (רדב"ז), Rabbi David ibn Zimra (רבי דוד בן זמרא, 1479–1573, Egypt and Tzfas)[22], develops the halakhic-theological category of shituf (שיתוף): belief in the one God paired with another power. In the Radbaz’s formulation, Christianity for non-Yehudim (יהודים) is shituf — not the full issur (איסור) of avodah zarah (עבודה זרה). Christians are chatzi kofrim (חצי כופרים), but they are half: they retain emes, they retain monotheism, and they revere the Hebrew Tanach (תנ"ך). Islam is more clearly monotheistic still, without any Trinitarian element. Neither, in this framework, stands on the same footing as the ovdei avodah zarah of Chazal’s era, who believed in nothing but their idols.

Whether the Radbaz’s classification represents halakhic consensus is not simple; the Rema (רמ"א), the Noda Bi-Yehudah (נודע ביהודה), the Chasam Sofer, and others weigh in with varying positions, and there is genuine machlokes (מחלוקת) on the scope and application of the ruling.[23] What matters for the present mahalach is that within a serious halakhic tradition, the descendants of Yishmael and (in the Radbaz’s reading) of Eisav are seen as bearing something of the emunah (אמונה) of Avraham, not as fully outside it.

The story is told of Rav Baruch Ber Leibowitz of Kamenitz (רב ברוך בער ליבוביץ מקאמניץ), talmid of Rav Chaim Soloveitchik of Brisk[24]. Traveling by horse and wagon driven by a Christian, they passed a church. Christians customarily cross themselves at a church; this driver did not — evidently an atheist. Rav Baruch Ber at once called out, Stop the wagon. Asked why, he answered in his Yiddish: “איינער וואָס גלייבט נישט אין גאָט” — one who does not even believe in his own false god — even if it is sheker sheker ve-chazav (שקר שקר וכזב) — if he does not care about that either, he is chashud (חשוד) even for murder. There are no moral boundaries left. The story, told in the yeshiva world for decades, illustrates the point that even a false theology, taken seriously, retains a moral floor that pure unbelief does not.

The Rambam in Hilchos Melachim — The Censored Passage

The Rambam, at the very end of Hilchos Melachim (רמב"ם הלכות מלכים י"א ה"ד), wrote a passage that was excised by Christian censors from printed editions for centuries. Only with the Frankel edition, working from Yemenite kisvei yad (כתבי יד), was it restored to standard printings.[25]

The Rambam’s teaching, in substance, runs as follows. The founder of Christianity thought he was Mashiach; a Jewish court executed him. From him, and from the founder of Islam who arose after him, emerged an irbuvya gedolah (ערבוביה גדולה) — a great confusion. The Crusades, the persecutions, the pogroms across centuries flowed in their wake, and Klal Yisrael paid the price. And yet — the Rambam writes — “מחשבות בורא עולם אין כח באדם להשיגם”, the thoughts of the Creator of the world are beyond human comprehension. The unfolding of history was designed to bring the world to a consciousness of Mashiach. Klal Yisrael is a messianic people, in a world that had never heard the concept. Across centuries, in every country, the two great non-Jewish religions saturated the world’s consciousness with the categories of a coming redeemer, of a single God, of a great final reckoning. When the real Mashiach arrives, the world is prepared to receive him.

The Yaavetz (יעב"ץ), Rabbi Yaakov Emden (רבי יעקב עמדן, 1697–1776, Altona)[26], returns to this Rambam repeatedly in his writings on emunah, emphasizing exactly this point: humans are small-minded and see only their own suffering; the Rambam asks us to lift our eyes to the arc of tikkun ha-olam, an arc whose length is measured in millennia and whose meaning is not immediately legible.

The Chiddush on “Magen Avraham”

This is what the Maharam Schick brings into the Shemoneh Esrei. When we recite “אלקי אברהם, אלקי יצחק, ואלקי יעקב,” we are naming the three Avos in their specific paternity of Yaakov mateh shleimah. When we then close with “מגן אברהם” alone, one might naturally suppose the singling out of Avraham is a contraction — that only Yaakov’s line made it through, and the branches of Yishmael and Eisav are written off.

No. The Maharam Schick teaches the opposite. The chasimah on Avraham alone is precisely the chasimah that reaches beyond the specific paternity of the three Avos to the wider paternity described in Bereshis 17:5[27]: “כי אב המון גוים נתתיך” — I have made you the father of a multitude of nations. Avraham alone among the Avos carries this title. Yitzchak and Yaakov are the Avos of Klal Yisrael; Avraham is both the Av of Klal Yisrael and the av hamon goyim.

This is the linguistic pivot that the Maharam Schick’s reading requires, and it has textual footing: the Torah itself uses two names for the same person — “אברם” narrowed to a single lineage, “אברהם” expanded to av hamon. In the chasimah, the wider name is what is invoked. “מגן אברהם” is the sealing on the promise that HaKadosh Baruch Hu shielded Avraham not for a partial zera but for the whole zera — every branch, in the fullness of time, gathered back.

VI. Synthesis — Three Faces of “U-vecha”

The three mehalchim can be laid out and left as three. But there is a synthetic reading available that is worth stating explicitly, because it lifts the reading of “ובך חותמין” from an accumulation of divrei Torah to a single vision.

Read the Gemara’s “ובך” — with you — as pointing outward and forward. Avraham in the chasimah is Avraham in the sense in which he is not identical with Yitzchak and Yaakov: Avraham as the wider figure that the two subsequent Avos did not become.

  • His middah is chesed — and chesed is the outward-facing middah, the one that goes beyond the boundaries of the self to include the other. The dor ha-chesed is the dor in which Avraham’s outward-facing middah becomes the axis of the age.
  • His tefillah is Shacharis — and Shacharis is the tefillah of the eternal present, the one grammatical form that persists into the age when “לא יבוא עוד שמשך”. The Bayis Shelishi is the Beis HaMikdash in which Avraham’s tefillah alone continues.
  • His fatherhood is av hamon goyim — and this is the fatherhood that reaches beyond the specific lineage of Yaakov to gather in, in the fullness of the messianic unfolding, the whole of zera Avraham.

All three mehalchim converge on the reading of Avraham in the chasimah as the outward, eternal, universal face of the promise — the face that is not diminished in the sealing but expanded. The single chasimah is at once historical (the Kotzker), liturgical (the Tif’eres Banim), and universal (the Maharam Schick). When we close Birkas Avos tomorrow morning, the word “אברהם” at the seal carries all three layers at once.

A Kavvanah in the Chasimah

A kabbalistic siddur (סידור)-kavvanah tradition transmitted in the name of the Mateh Tov (מטה טוב)[28] encapsulates the synthesis in practice: at the closing words “מגן אברהם,” have in mind that this chasimah reaches toward the eternal tefillah of le’asid la’vo — the tefillah of the dor ha-chesed, of the Bayis Shelishi, of the whole of zera Avraham gathered under the shield of the one God. The kavvanah is offered as an enhancement; the fundamental halakhic requirement of kavvanah in the first berachah, established in Shulchan Aruch (שולחן ערוך) Orach Chaim 101[29], remains the meaning of the words as they stand. For one who can lift the kavvanah higher, this is the direction in which the mehalchim of the Kotzker, the Tif’eres Banim, and the Maharam Schick together point.

Be’ezras Hashem (בעזרת השם), may we merit to see the fulfillment of the chasimah in our own days — the chesed that seals the galus, the Shacharis that opens the age of the Bayis Shelishi, and the shield of Avraham extended over all of his children.

Each note runs short citation first (for quick lookup), then an expanded elaboration. Footnotes run sequentially with the body text.

  1. Pesachim 117b — “ובך חותמין ולא בכולם.” — The sugya in Arvei Pesachim derives the structure of Birkas Avos from the four clauses of Hashem’s opening promise to Avraham in Lech Lecha. Each clause becomes a phrase in the berachah, but only the first — “ואעשך לגוי גדול”, mapped to “אלקי אברהם” — receives the chasimah. ↩
  2. Bereshis 12:2, with Rashi ad loc.; cf. Bereshis 17:5. — Rashi brings the Pesachim drasha in his commentary on the pasuk, signaling its centrality to the pshat (פשט) of Lech Lecha. The later pasuk in Bereshis 17:5 — “כי אב המון גוים נתתיך” — provides the textual basis for the Maharam Schick’s reading in Mahalach C. ↩
  3. Zohar HaKadosh across Parshas Terumah, Vayakhel, and Balak; Pardes Rimonim (Ramak) on the chitzonim. — The Zohar repeatedly likens tefillah without kavvanah to “גוף בלי נשמה” and develops the frightening image that empty words of tefillah are seized by the chitzonim. Rabbi Moshe Cordovero (Ramak, 1522–1570, Tzfas) in Pardes Rimonim systematizes the doctrine of the kelipos and the sitra achra (סטרא אחרא) on which the Zohar’s warnings rest. ↩
  4. Rosh HaShanah 18a — tefillah shleimah versus tefillah she’einah shleimah. — The Gemara’s account of two who davened “רפאנו”, one healed and one not; Rashi identifies the difference as kavvanah. The sugya is the classical ma’amar establishing the halakhic weight of kavvanah in the internal berachos of Shemoneh Esrei. ↩
  5. The Kotzker Rebbe (Rabbi Menachem Mendel of Kotzk, 1787–1859) on “ובך חותמין” as dor ha-chesed; antecedent in Megaleh Amukos of Rabbi Nosson Nota Shapiro of Kraków (1585–1633). — The Kotzker’s vort circulates in chassidishe tradition; the antecedent remez in the Megaleh Amukos — a foundational kabbalistic-drush (דרוש) work on the Torah — identifies the three-amudim mapping of the Avos as the key to the ruling. The Kotzker brings it forward in chassidishe form. The three-amudim structure (chesed, gevurah, tif’eres) is standard kabbalistic mapping. ↩
  6. Tif’eres Banim on Parshas Lech Lecha (Rabbi Tzvi Hirsh Shapira of Munkács, 1850–1913). — Rabbi Tzvi Hirsh Shapira — grandson of the Bnei Yissaschar (Rabbi Tzvi Elimelech of Dinov, 1783–1841), one of the foundational sifrei chassidus — authored both the encyclopedic Darchei Teshuvah on Yoreh De’ah (a primary reference for any lamdan in basar be-chalav or ta’aroves) and the Tif’eres Banim on Chumash. The chiddush on Lech Lecha, building on the Radak’s face-value reading of Yechezkel 46, identifies “ובך חותמין” with the future cessation of tamid shel bein ha’arbayim. ↩
  7. On Yechezkel’s vision as Bayis Shelishi: the standard reading; alternative Rishonim. — The dominant reading among Rishonim and mefarshei Nach is that Yechezkel’s vision from ch. 40 onward describes the future Beis HaMikdash. A minority of Rishonim, however, read the vision as descriptive of the Bayis Sheini. The Tif’eres Banim’s chiddush depends on the majority reading; readers who follow the minority position must reframe the chiddush as bearing on the arrangements of Bayis Sheini, an arrangement that in fact included tamid shel bein ha’arbayim and thereby weakens the chiddush considerably. The section proceeds on the majority reading. ↩
  8. Yechezkel 46:13–15 — the future tamid shel shachar without corresponding tamid shel bein ha’arbayim. — The perek in Yechezkel’s vision of the Bayis Shelishi in which the daily korban is described. The pasuk mentions only “בבקר בבקר”. The chapter is read as the haftarah of Parshas HaChodesh. Most mefarshim harmonize the omission with various tirutzim (תירוצים) — korbanos milu’im, textual ellipsis; the Radak takes it at face value. ↩
  9. Radak on Yechezkel 46 — chiddush: only tamid shel shachar in the future. — Rabbi David Kimchi (1160–1235, Narbonne in Provence), one of the great mefarshei Nach among the Rishonim, breaks with the harmonizing approach. He reads Yechezkel 46 at face value: Bayis Shelishi will have only a tamid shel shachar, not a tamid shel bein ha’arbayim. This is the foundational chiddush on which the Tif’eres Banim builds his reading of “ובך חותמין.” ↩
  10. Berachos 26b — “תפילות כנגד תמידין תקנום.” — The Gemara’s formulation of the principle that the daily tefillos were instituted parallel to the daily temidin: Shacharis against tamid shel shachar, Minchah against tamid shel bein ha’arbayim, Ma’ariv against the eivarim and pedarim consumed through the night. The sugya presents an alternative view (tefillos instituted by the Avos) and resolves that both are correct — the Avos originated the practice; the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah gave it formal structure parallel to the korbanos. ↩
  11. Tehillim 139:12 — “ולילה כיום יאיר; כחשכה כאורה.” — David HaMelech’s declaration that before HaKadosh Baruch Hu there is no distinction between night and day. Read by the Tif’eres Banim as a vision of the future state — le’asid la’vo the chiluk (חילוק) between day and night dissolves, and with it the metzius of an evening korban. ↩
  12. Yeshayahu 60:19–20 — “לא יבוא עוד שמשך”; cf. Yeshayahu 2:2–3 — “ונהרו אליו כל הגוים.” — Two nevu’os (נבואות) of the future geulah. Yeshayahu 60 describes the unending light of the or ha-ganuz (אור הגנוז) replacing the natural sun and moon — textual support for the Tif’eres Banim’s reading. Yeshayahu 2 — all the nations shall stream — is the textual support for the Maharam Schick’s reading of the universal tikkun of zera Avraham. ↩
  13. Bereshis 18:22 — “ויהי עמד לפני ה'”; Rashi: “אין עמידה אלא תפילה”; cf. Sforno. — Avraham’s tefillah for Sedom (סדום) is described with the participial “עמד” — standing, in continuous present. Rashi identifies this as the mekor (מקור) for tefillas Shacharis. The Sforno and other Rishonim note that the verbal form chosen for Avraham’s tefillah differs from those used for Yitzchak and Yaakov — eternal present versus narrative past — supporting the Tif’eres Banim’s reading. ↩
  14. Bereshis 24:63 — “ויצא יצחק לשוח בשדה לפנות ערב.” — The pasuk describing Yitzchak’s prayer at Minchah time (“לפנות ערב”) — the mekor for tefillas Minchah as instituted by Yitzchak Avinu. The verbal form “ויצא” is vav ha-hipuch narrative past. ↩
  15. Bereshis 28:11 — “ויפגע במקום, וילן שם.” — The pasuk describing Yaakov’s prayer on the way to Charan — the mekor for tefillas Ma’ariv as instituted by Yaakov Avinu. The form “ויפגע” is again narrative past, completing the grammatical contrast that supports the Tif’eres Banim’s reading. ↩
  16. Hasagos HaRa’avad on Rambam — whether nature changes le’asid la’vo. — Rabbi Avraham ben David of Posquières (Ra’avad, c. 1125–1198), in his hasagos on the Mishneh Torah, takes positions across several sugyos on the relationship between the Torah’s eternal stability and the natural world’s potential for transformation in the messianic era. The Rambam’s general view is that nature does not change; the Ra’avad and many mekubalim hold that nature itself transforms. Both positions accommodate the Tif’eres Banim’s chiddush within the principle of “לא תשתנה התורה.” ↩
  17. Ramban on the future kashrus of the chazir (חזיר) — the well-known drasha “למה נקרא שמו חזיר? שעתיד הקדוש ברוך הוא להחזירו לישראל”. The drasha is widely cited in Ramban’s name in chassidishe literature and appears in printed collections associated with him, though the precise textual location is not straightforward and parallel formulations are attributed also to Rabbeinu Bachye (רבינו בחיי) and to Midrashic collections. The Ramban’s reading — that the pig itself will develop to satisfy the simanei kashrus of maflis parsah u-ma’aleh gerah, while the Torah’s rules remain unchanged — is the structural parallel the Tif’eres Banim invokes: nature changes, the Torah does not.
    ↩
  18. Rambam, Hilchos Temidin U-Musafin ch. 1; Sefer HaMitzvos, Mitzvas Aseh 39. — The Rambam counts the daily korban tamid as a single mitzvah, discharged twice each day. This is the structural support for the secondary reconciliation with “לא תשתנה התורה”: in Bayis Shelishi, the same one mitzvah is discharged once instead of twice — the mitzvah has not changed, only its frequency of execution. ↩
  19. She’eilos u-Teshuvos Maharam Schick and his drashos — chiddush on “מגן אברהם” as the chasimah on the tikkun of all of zera Avraham. — Rabbi Moshe Schick (1809–1879), foremost talmid of the Chasam Sofer and rosh yeshivah in Huszt with a yeshiva of eight hundred talmidim. In his drashos and teshuvos he develops the reading that the singular chasimah “מגן אברהם” is not a contraction excluding Yishmael and Eisav but an inclusion: Avraham as “אב המון גוים”, with the promise of Hashem’s shielding extended over the whole zera. The reading interlocks with the Rambam’s long-arc framing of Yishu and the founder of Islam. ↩
  20. Rambam, Hilchos Avodah Zarah ch. 1 — Avraham Avinu spreading the emunah. — The Rambam’s opening perek in Hilchos Avodah Zarah is a brief history of emunah: humanity’s slide into idolatry, and Avraham’s solitary recognition of achdus ha-Bore (אחדות הבורא). The Rambam describes Avraham’s indefatigable travel from town to town and family to family proclaiming emes, gathering followers, and seeding the future umah (אומה). This portrait stands behind the Maharam Schick’s emphasis that Avraham’s mission was universal, not parochial. ↩
  21. Etz HaDa’as Tov (Rabbi Chaim Vital, 1542–1620) at the close of Parshas Re’eh, drawing on the Arizal — the kabbalistic mesorah that identifies the founders of Christianity and Islam as spiritual gilgulim of Eisav and Yishmael. Rabbi Chaim Vital was the foremost talmid of the Arizal and the chief transmitter of his Kabbalah. The two names of the founder of Christianity and of Eisav differ by only a single letter (י in place of ע); the letter-play is a mnemonic pointer, not a proof, and the substantive tradition is the kabbalistic gilgul attribution itself. The tradition that Islam’s founder had a Jewish teacher — and the dream of the tree growing from his hand, interpreted by a Jewish sage as a summons to prophethood — is preserved in Etz HaDa’as Tov as kabbalistic mesorah; the historical form of the tradition should be understood in that register.
    ↩
  22. She’eilos u-Teshuvos HaRadbaz on shituf; status of Christianity and Islam relative to avodah zarah. — Rabbi David ibn Zimra (Radbaz, 1479–1573, Egypt and Tzfas) develops the halakhic-theological category of shituf: belief in one God paired with another power. For non-Yehudim, shituf is not the full issur of avodah zarah; Christians thereby retain a partial emes. Islam, with no Trinitarian element, is more clearly monotheistic. The Radbaz’s framework grounds the Maharam Schick’s and Rambam’s view that the descendants of Yishmael and Eisav hold residual elements of Avraham’s legacy. ↩
  23. On the scope of the Radbaz’s shituf ruling and the wider machlokes. The Rema (Orach Chaim 156), the Noda Bi-Yehudah, the Chasam Sofer, and later poskim weigh in with differing positions on the precise scope of the leniency — whether shituf is fully permitted to non-Yehudim, or is prohibited but not classified as full avodah zarah; and whether Christianity in particular falls within the category. The Maharam Schick’s reading does not require the strongest formulation of the leniency; it requires that within a serious halakhic tradition, the descendants of Yishmael and Eisav retain something of the emunah of Avraham. That much is well-founded across the range of shittos (שיטות).
    ↩
  24. Oral mesorah from the talmidim of Rav Baruch Ber Leibowitz of Kamenitz (1862–1939), rosh yeshivah of Knesses Beis Yitzchak in Slabodka and later Kamenitz; talmid of Rav Chaim Soloveitchik of Brisk; author of Birkas Shmuel (ברכת שמואל). The story of the wagon driver who did not cross himself — “איינער וואָס גלייבט נישט אין גאָט”, one with no fear of any God, even a false one, has no moral floor — circulates in the yeshiva world; the shiur did not identify a published source.

  25. Rambam, Hilchos Melachim 11:4 (uncensored, Frankel edition) — the founders of Christianity and Islam in the long arc of tikkun ha-olam. — The closing halakhah of the Mishneh Torah in which the Rambam discusses the founder of Christianity and the founder of Islam (whom he names elsewhere as “המשוגע”) as having played, despite the immense suffering they caused, an instrumental role in seeding world consciousness with the concept of Mashiach in preparation for the actual Mashiach. The passage was excised by Christian censors from printed editions for centuries. The Frankel edition of the Mishneh Torah, working from the original Yemenite kisvei yad, restored it to printed form in our generation. The Rambam’s language is compact; the paraphrase in the body text renders his substance in expanded English rather than a verbatim translation. ↩
  26. The Yaavetz (Rabbi Yaakov Emden, 1697–1776, Altona) — emphasis of the Rambam’s Hilchos Melachim passage on the long arc of tikkun ha-olam. — The Yaavetz, son of the Chacham Tzvi (חכם צבי), in his writings on emunah and in his polemics returns repeatedly to the Rambam’s long-arc framing in Hilchos Melachim. He emphasizes that the historical role of Christianity and Islam in seeding world consciousness with the messianic idea is, in the Rambam’s reading, providential — an aspect of tikkun ha-olam beyond human comprehension. ↩
  27. Bereshis 17:5 — “כי אב המון גוים נתתיך.” — The pasuk in which Avram is renamed Avraham, with the explicit expansion of his paternity to “a multitude of nations.” This is the textual pivot on which the Maharam Schick’s reading rests: Avraham, unlike Yitzchak or Yaakov, carries both a narrow paternity (of the Yaakov mateh shleimah) and a wide one (of the av hamon goyim). The chasimah that names Avraham alone reaches for the wider paternity that only he bears. ↩
  28. A kabbalistic siddur-kavvanah tradition transmitted in the name of the Mateh Tov (מטה טוב) — recorded in the shiur under the name Minkud Matav; the precise attribution and edition were not identified. The kavvanah tradition recorded under this name overlaps with the kabbalistic siddurim of the Arizal school. The synthesis offered in the closing chapter can be received on the strength of the three mehalchim themselves, independent of the pinned attribution of the kavvanah.

  29. Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chaim 101 — the halakhic requirement of kavvanah in the first berachah of Shemoneh Esrei. — The Shulchan Aruch rules that kavvanah in Birkas Avos is me’akev (מעכב) — one who did not have kavvanah in the first berachah is required, be-di’avad (בדיעבד), to repeat the Shemoneh Esrei. The kabbalistic kavvanah offered in the closing chapter is an enhancement layered upon the halakhic requirement of understanding what one is saying; the halakhic obligation is fulfilled by the ordinary meaning of the words, and the kabbalistic overlay is for the ba’al nefesh (בעל נפש) who can reach it without disturbing the base kavvanah. ↩
