שַׁחֲרִית שֶׁל שַׁבָּת — מִזְמוֹר לְתוֹדָה, תְּפִלִּין, וְהַכָּנָף הַשְּׁבִיעִית

Entering Shabbos Shacharis: The Omitted Mizmor, the Missing Tefillin, and the Seventh Wing
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Sunday Morning Series on Tefillah
Recorded 9 August 2026  ·  Sefer Edition

Sponsored by Rav Eli and Mrs. Goldstein לְעִלּוּי נִשְׁמָתוֹ — that the study of the order of the morning prayer, in the day upon which the King is Himself present, should serve as a merit for the soul whose entire life was ordered toward that Presence.

The morning prayer of the seventh day is not the weekday prayer with additions. It is a distinct avodah, with its own additions and its own omissions, its own voice and its own tempo. What follows sets out, in orderly fashion, the several respects in which Shacharis of Shabbos departs from the weekday, and the reasons that stand behind each departure. The trail runs from an omitted mizmor, to the absence of tefillin, to the timing of the prayer, to its very volume — and rises at last to the teaching of the Zohar HaKadosh that Klal Yisrael, on the day of the covenantal sign, is itself the seventh wing of the ministering angels.

I. The Two Faces of Shabbos Shacharis

The order of the weekday Shacharis — the framework of Pesukei d’Zimra (פסוקי דזמרא), the blessings surrounding Kriyas Shema, and the Shemoneh Esrei — forms the substrate upon which the Shabbos-morning prayer is built. Upon this substrate the seventh day imposes two kinds of alteration: it adds and it omits. Both movements have reasons; both are the fruit of the halakhic and mystical tradition working upon the received liturgy.

The most visible addition falls at the front of Pesukei d’Zimra. Every nusach inserts additional kapitlach of Tehillim before, or immediately after, Baruch She’amar (ברוך שאמר). Nusach Ashkenaz adds relatively few; Nusach Sfard and the chassidishe nuscha’os add many more. The reason for these particular additions — not merely their number but their specific content — is the deep question to which this chapter builds. The most conspicuous omission, addressed at once, is that of Mizmor l’Todah.[1]

II. The Omission of Mizmor l’Todah

Rashi’s Reading: The Mizmor as Verbal Korban

The one hundredth chapter of Tehillim — מִזְמוֹר לְתוֹדָה — is recited each weekday morning as part of Pesukei d’Zimra. On Shabbos, in the practice of most Ashkenazic communities, it is omitted. The source of the omission is a ruling attributed to Rashi in the siddur preserved in his name.[2] The Mizmor l’Todah, on Rashi’s reading, is not simply a song of praise; it is the verbal substitute for the korban todah — the individual thank-offering brought by one saved from grave danger, as enumerated by Chazal: the traveler who crossed the sea, the traveler who crossed the desert, the one released from prison, the one who recovered from illness.[3]

The korban todah is a korban yachid. On Shabbos, only korbanos tzibbur — the tamid and the mussaf — are offered in the Beis HaMikdash. No yachid brings his private thank-offering on the seventh day. And if the offering itself is not brought, its verbal analog should not be recited either. That, in Rashi’s teaching, is why the Mizmor l’Todah is omitted from Shabbos-morning Pesukei d’Zimra.

The Structural Remez: Four Verses, Four Loaves

The internal structure of the mizmor sharpens Rashi’s reading. After its opening title-verse, the chapter contains exactly four content-verses. The meforshim observe that this is not accidental. The korban todah was accompanied by forty loaves in four categories — ten of challos matzos, ten of rekikin, ten of challos revuchah, and ten of chametz.[4] Four categories of loaves and four verses of the mizmor: the recitation mirrors the offering in its very literary architecture. For Rashi, this remez is precisely the point — the mizmor is a textual todah, and the omission on Shabbos follows the physical omission of the korban.

The Tur’s Objection

Against Rashi stands the Tur.[5] The Tur holds that reciting a chapter of Tehillim is not itself an act of chillul Shabbos. Nor is there any real concern that when the Beis HaMikdash is rebuilt, a Jew will infer from the morning recitation that he is obligated to bring an actual thank-offering that day. The Tur therefore rules that Mizmor l’Todah may — and should — be said on Shabbos as on any other day. The Sephardic tradition preserved this ruling, and many Sephardic communities recite it.

The Rambam’s Silence

A third position must be recorded. The Rambam, in his Seder haTefillah appended to Sefer Ahavah, does not include Mizmor l’Todah in his outline of the daily Pesukei d’Zimra at all.[6] The picture is thus not one of Rashi against the Tur alone; it is a three-way division of the Rishonic tradition. For the Rambam, the mizmor is not part of the fixed morning framework in the first instance, and the question of its Shabbos-status does not arise as such. Later authorities, in weighing the two named positions, are effectively choosing between Rashi and the Tur within an Ashkenazic and Sephardic framework in which the mizmor has already been received as a fixed daily recitation.

Confirmation from Erev Pesach and Pesach

The Levush (Rabbeinu Mordechai Yaffe, 1530–1612) offers a supporting observation for Rashi’s reading.[7] Mizmor l’Todah is omitted not only on Shabbos, but also on Erev Pesach and throughout Pesach. The korban todah included ten loaves of chametz, and one does not bring such an offering when chametz is prohibited or in danger of leaven. That the mizmor is likewise omitted in this season indicates that the recitation is bound tightly to the physical structure of the offering — strengthening Rashi’s position and the Ashkenazic practice.

The Regional Fault-Line

The dispute mapped itself onto the geography of medieval Jewish life. In Ashkenaz, and especially in the France of Rashi’s authority, the omission became fixed. In Sefarad, the Tur’s ruling prevailed and the recitation continued. Between the two lay Provence, whose kehillos were drawn now in one direction, now in the other, and which became a genuine battleground of the dispute across the centuries. Later authority, above all the Vilna Gaon, sharpened the Ashkenazic side: the Gaon, who treated Pesukei d’Zimra as a fixed and closed unit rather than as a body open to accretion, gave the omission additional structural weight in his siddur.[8]

III. The Absence of Tefillin on Shabbos

The Primary Sugya: Shabbos as Ois

The Gemara in Menachos establishes the halakhic foundation: שַׁבָּתוֹת וְיָמִים טוֹבִים לָאו זְמַן תְּפִלִּין נִינְהוּ, שֶׁכֵּן אִיקְּרוּ אוֹת — Shabbos and Yom Tov are not times for tefillin, since they themselves are called ois, a sign.[9] Three mitzvos are identified in the Torah with the term ois: milah, whose verse reads “וְהָיָה לְאוֹת בְּרִית בֵּינִי וּבֵינֵיכֶם”; tefillin, of which the Torah says “וְהָיָה לְךָ לְאוֹת עַל־יָדְךָ”; and Shabbos, which is called “אוֹת הִיא לְעוֹלָם”. Each of the three testifies, from a different angle, to the same covenantal reality between the Holy One and His people.

The Framework of the Sma”g and the Maharsha: Two Witnesses Suffice

The Sma”g (Rabbi Moshe of Coucy, mid-13th c.), as cited by the Maharsha in his Chidushei Aggados on Berachos,[10] gives this teaching a striking legal frame. The Torah’s principle for evidentiary sufficiency is stated in Devarim: “עַל־פִּי שְׁנֵי עֵדִים אוֹ עַל־פִּי שְׁלֹשָׁה־עֵדִים יָקוּם דָּבָר” — two witnesses suffice for a matter to stand; a third is not required. Applied to the covenantal signs, each day of a Jew’s life requires two eidim to the covenant with HaKadosh Baruch Hu. On weekdays, those two witnesses are milah upon his body and tefillin upon his arm and head. On Shabbos, the two witnesses are milah and Shabbos itself. To add tefillin as a third would be halakhically superfluous.

Not Merely Superfluous — A Diminishment of Shabbos

The Sifrei HaKedoshim press the matter further. To put on tefillin on Shabbos is not merely unnecessary; it is a zilzul b’kedushas Shabbos (זלזול בקדושת שבת) — a diminishment of the sanctity of Shabbos itself. It implies that the ois of the day is insufficient testimony, that another sign must be added to complete the record. On this understanding, tefillin on Shabbos are not merely bittul mitzvah; they carry an active offense against the day. They are treated as muktzeh and their use is forbidden outright.[11]

A Parable Preserved in the Name of the Ksav Sofer

A well-known parable, circulated in the tradition in the name of one of the great rabbanim of Central Europe in the nineteenth century, illustrates the point.[12] The kehillah was informed that Emperor Franz Joseph of Austria-Hungary would visit the shul on Shabbos morning. A rival, resentful that the emperor had chosen the traditional synagogue rather than the reform temple, entered the building the night before and removed the imperial portrait that customarily hung in the lobby as a token of allegiance. He then sent word to the imperial court that the Jews had removed the portrait as an act of rebellion.

The emperor arrived. He looked at once for the portrait and, finding it absent, turned to the rav for explanation. The rav, without hesitation, replied that Jewish practice contains a corresponding halakhah: on every day of the week, the Jew wears tefillin as a sign of his covenant with his King; on Shabbos he does not, because on Shabbos the King is Himself present, and a sign of the King’s presence is superfluous — indeed a diminishment — when the King is Himself in the room. Just so, the rav explained, when the emperor was absent, his portrait hung as a token of allegiance; when the emperor was Himself present, the portrait had been put away, for a portrait of the King is not what one displays in the presence of the King.

The parable is homiletic and its precise attribution varies in the telling; what it captures — and what its endurance in the tradition has served to make vivid — is the underlying svara. Shabbos is not merely a day when a witness is unnecessary because a second suffices; it is a day when a witness would insult the presence to which it purports to testify.

The Depth of the Prohibition: Rishonic Positions

The scope of the prohibition is itself a matter of Rishonic dispute. The Ba’al HaMaor holds that the prohibition of tefillin on Shabbos is d’oraisa — the Torah itself excludes Shabbos from the days of the mitzvah. The Ramban, in his hasagos, and the Rashba, argue for a d’rabbanan framework in which the exclusion is a rabbinic derivation from the ois principle rather than a Torah-level prohibition.[13] The practical consequence — that tefillin are not worn on Shabbos — is uniform; the dispute concerns the character of the exclusion and its evidentiary weight in cases of doubt. The ois framework of the Sma”g/Maharsha functions primarily as a hashkafic overlay that helps the practical halakhah feel coherent rather than arbitrary; the analytic sugya of the Rishonim runs deeper.

Yom Tov and Chol HaMoed

A related question is whether Yom Tov shares Shabbos’s ois-status, since it too is called shabbaton. The Zohar, in Parshas Pinchas, treats Yom Tov as similar to Shabbos in this regard. The practical field of dispute is the status of Chol HaMoed. The Shulchan Aruch in Orach Chaim records the machloikes. The Vilna Gaon, following the Zohar, ruled not to wear tefillin on Chol HaMoed, and most Litvish yeshivos as well as the great majority of chassidishe communities follow this practice; other Ashkenazic communities, following the Rambam and earlier Ashkenazi Rishonim, put on tefillin on Chol HaMoed without a berachah.[14]

IV. The Time of Shabbos Shacharis

The Ramo’s Ruling

The Ramo, in his gloss on Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chaim siman reish-pei-alef, records the minhag to daven Shacharis somewhat later on Shabbos than during the week.[15] The Ramo does not specify how much later, nor does he give a reason in the siman itself. The reasoning is developed by earlier and later authorities in three distinct directions.

The Mordechai and the Tradition of Rav Hai Gaon

The Mordechai, in his commentary on Shabbos, records a tradition traced through Rome to Rav Hai Gaon, the last of the Geonim.[16] The tradition rests on a subtle textual point. Concerning the daily tamid of morning, the Torah says “אֶת־הַכֶּבֶשׂ הָאֶחָד תַּעֲשֶׂה בַבֹּקֶר” — more emphatically still, בַּבֹּקֶר בַּבֹּקֶר — imposing an early-morning obligation. Concerning Shabbos, the Torah opens: “וּבְיוֹם הַשַּׁבָּת”, without the qualifier ba’boker. The tradition of Rav Hai reads the omission as intentional: on Shabbos, the pressure of early-morning offering is relaxed.

The Textual Difficulty and the Maharil’s Rejoinder

The obvious difficulty is that the verse “וּבְיוֹם הַשַּׁבָּת” introduces the mussaf of Shabbos, not the morning tamid; that the pasuk about mussaf omits ba’boker is unsurprising, since mussaf in any case follows the morning offering. The distinction cannot easily rest on this omission alone. The Maharil, building on the Mordechai, defends the tradition with a substantive claim: even the morning tamid of Shabbos was itself brought later than the weekday tamid, for the kohanim in the Beis HaMikdash slept later on Shabbos morning.[17] The claim is disputed, and no chazal passage is adduced in its direct support; it functions as an interpretive move to preserve the Rav Hai tradition against the textual objection.

The Radvaz: A Defense, and a Personal Repudiation

The Radvaz, chief rabbi of Egypt in the sixteenth century, took up the question in a famous teshuvah.[18] His argument is that since Rav Hai Gaon stands at the near end of the shalsheles of received tradition, his words must be treated as divrei kabbalah: even if the reasoning does not fully persuade, the ruling must be preserved out of the honor due to the transmitter. The teshuvah mounts an eloquent defense of the late-davening minhag on precisely this ground.

And then, at the close of the same teshuvah, the Radvaz delivers a personal repudiation whose force is not diminished by the passage of centuries. Let the reader not think, he writes, that because he has defended the minhag at length he therefore relies upon it himself. Chas v’shalom that he should sleep late on Shabbos morning and daven late. He calls the late-davening practice not a minhag yafeh, not a minhag hagun, and states plainly that he himself davens vasikin every Shabbos. The passage stands as one of the great instances in the responsa literature of a posek defending a tradition he does not personally accept.

The Gemara in Megillah: A Countervailing Reading

Working against the Rav Hai tradition altogether is the Gemara in Megillah 23a.[19] There it is taught that on Yom Tov, the community davens late — because Yom Tov permits cooking, and the household rises early to prepare the seudah. On Shabbos, when all cooking is prohibited and preparations are made in advance, this distraction does not exist, and Rashi comments explicitly that on Shabbos one should daven vasikin. On this reading, the seventh day pushes prayer earlier, not later, than the weekday — precisely the reverse of the tradition traced through the Mordechai.

The Simpler Reasons

Later acharonim commonly prefer a simpler account of the observable practice. Two rationales are widely invoked.

The first is oneg Shabbos. The dictum “שֵׁינָה בְּשַׁבָּת תַּעֲנוּג” — sleep on Shabbos is a delight — carries its own halakhic weight; the community allows itself a later beginning to honor the character of the day. On this reading, the pasuk-based argument of Rav Hai is an asmachta, a textual peg for a rationale that already stood on its own ground.[20]

The second, preserved in the name of certain Rishonim, reverses the picture entirely. The community wakes at the ordinary hour, but uses the intervening time not for sleep but for the weekly obligation of Shnayim Mikra v’Echad Targum — reading each verse of the parsha twice in the Hebrew and once in Onkelos. On this reading, the later davening is a gift only to those who fill the added hour with Torah study; it is not a license for sleep.[21]

A third rationale, brought in the Beis Yosef, is practical and communal: the delay allows latecomers to reach the tzibbur before it advances too far into the tefillah, ensuring that tefillah b’tzibbur is realized more fully than would be the case with an earlier start.[22]

The Halakhic Guardrail

Whatever the theoretical basis, one halakhic gader stands beyond dispute. A late start — whether at nine o’clock in the modern American norm or somewhat later in the summer months — can run against sof zeman Kriyas Shema and, on the longest summer days, even against sof zeman Tefillah. If the pace of the shaliach tzibbur threatens to carry the community past these limits, the individual must be prepared to interrupt his own progress through Pesukei d’Zimra and recite Kriyas Shema for himself. The Mishnah Berurah writes that at a minimum, one should say the first verse of Shema with kavvanah before the zeman; the preferable course is to recite the entire Kriyas Shema.[23]

V. The Voice of Tefillah on Shabbos

The Arizal’s Distinction

The Mishnah Berurah, citing the Arizal, distinguishes between the volume at which Pesukei d’Zimra is recited during the week and on Shabbos.[24] During the week the recitation is b’lachash — quietly, under the breath, in the mode of the individual’s internal preparation. On Shabbos the recitation is aloud, with niggun, expansively and joyfully. In the Arizal’s kabbalistic framework the two registers are tied to distinct yichudim: the weekday lachash accompanies specific kavvanos of concealment, and the Shabbos song accompanies the expanded da’as of the day, in which the inner life of prayer is drawn outward. Any who has entered a chassidishe shul on Shabbos morning has heard the distinction embodied: the whole of Pesukei d’Zimra is sung.

The Protection of the Ba’al Tefillah

Precisely because Shabbos allows — indeed demands — a slower and fuller Pesukei d’Zimra, the Mishnah Berurah issues a striking guard against the impatient congregant.[25] Should the shaliach tzibbur be ma’arich — taking his time, singing, davening with kavvanah — and should a member of the tzibbur be tempted to protest and press him to speed up, the Mishnah Berurah writes that this protest is prohibited even when the protester’s motive is l’shem shamayim: even if he seeks the earlier finish for the sake of additional Torah study afterward. The ikkar din is that Pesukei d’Zimra is to be said b’nachas u’b’kavvanah. Shabbos gives the added time; the shaliach tzibbur is entitled to use it.

VI. Baruch She’amar and Yishtabach: The Framing Blessing

A Berachah Masgeres

Baruch She’amar and Yishtabach together form a berachah masgeres (ברכה מסגרת) — a framing blessing that brackets Pesukei d’Zimra as a single halakhic unit.[26] The structural parallel is to a berachah recited upon food: as one may not interrupt with speech between the berachah and the eating that fulfills it, so one may not interrupt with speech between Baruch She’amar and Yishtabach. Sichas chullin — ordinary conversation — is prohibited outright; only devar mitzvah or a matter of tzorech gadol may be permitted.

The Practical Difference between Ashkenaz and Sfard

The two nuscha’os arrange this framework differently, and the practical consequence is often misunderstood. Nusach Ashkenaz opens Baruch She’amar early, shortly after the opening Hodu and the introductory pesukim; from that point through Yishtabach, the framing rule engages and no speech is permitted. Nusach Sfard and the chassidishe rites place Baruch She’amar later, following an extended body of korbanos and preliminary Shabbos additions. The effect is that the berachah masgeres engages later, and there is a longer pre-framework stretch during which speech is not strictly prohibited by the framing rule as such.

This distinction is not, however, a license. The pre-Baruch She’amar window in Nusach Sfard is not a designated schmooze-time; it is simply a stretch in which the strict framing rule has not yet engaged. One who recites Baruch She’amar and then converses with his neighbor through Pesukei d’Zimra is not merely relaxed; he is am ha’aretz in a matter of straightforward halakhah. A friend so occupied may — and should — be reminded that from the moment of Baruch She’amar onward, no speech is permitted.

VII. If One Arrives Late: The Order of Priorities

The reality of Shabbos-morning davening is that many arrive after the tzibbur has begun and must catch up. The Mishnah Berurah gives an explicit order of priorities.[27] The latecomer says, in this sequence: Baruch She’amar, the opening berachah; Ashrei (Tehillim 145), the halakhically central chapter of the entire section; Yishtabach, the closing berachah; and Nishmas Kol Chai, the Shabbos-specific centerpiece of the extended shirah. The extra kapitlach added on Shabbos are not me’akev and may be omitted if time is short, to be made up later if desired. But Nishmas is not to be missed. It stands as the central Shabbos piece of the entire morning shirah.

VIII. The Seventh Wing: Why These Additions

The Zohar in Tosafos

The question of the additions’ content — not merely how many, but why these particular kapitlach — is answered by a teaching of the Zohar HaKadosh brought in Tosafos on Sanhedrin.[28] The pasuk in Yeshayahu describes the Serafim: “שֵׁשׁ כְּנָפַיִם שֵׁשׁ כְּנָפַיִם לְאֶחָד” — six wings, six wings for each. Each of the six wings, in the Zoharic teaching, is the vehicle of the malachim’s song on one day of the week: Sunday one wing, Monday another, through the six days of creation.

The Question of Shabbos

When Shabbos arrives, the malachim have no wing remaining. They come before HaKadosh Baruch Hu and ask: Mi yashir hayom? — who shall sing today? And the answer given is: Knesses Yisrael. On the day of the covenantal sign — “אוֹת הִיא בֵּינִי וּבֵין בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל לְעֹלָם” — the Holy One prefers the song of His children to the song of His ministering hosts. On the seventh day, Klal Yisrael is itself the seventh wing.

The Practical Consequence

The added kapitlach in Shabbos Pesukei d’Zimra, on this teaching, are not merely a lengthening of the weekday form. They are, in their substance, the malachim’s song, entrusted on the seventh day to Klal Yisrael in their place. When the Jew on Shabbos morning says Mizmor Shir l’Yom haShabbos, or Yismechu Tzaddikim baHashem, or the other additions of the seventh day, he is not adding minutes to his prayer; he is stepping into the office that the Serafim have vacated. The nuscha’os differ in the specific kapitlach they include, but the theme is uniform: Bri’as haOlam and Mattan Torah, the two great acts that HaKadosh Baruch Hu has chosen to bind to the seventh day.

IX. L’David B’Shanoso es Ta’amo

The Superscription and the Story

Many nuscha’os include Tehillim 34 among the Shabbos-morning additions. The superscription of the perek is arresting: “לְדָוִד בְּשַׁנּוֹתוֹ אֶת טַעְמוֹ לִפְנֵי אֲבִימֶלֶךְ” — “Of David, when he altered his demeanor before Avimelech.” The narrative to which the superscription refers appears in Shmuel Aleph 21.[29]

David, having killed Golyas and having been promised Michal, the daughter of Shaul, in return, found himself under pursuit as Shaul’s jealousy overtook him. He fled and reached the court of Achish, king of Gat, capital of the Philistines. (“Avimelech” in the superscription is the generic title of the kings of the Philistines, as “Paro” was of the kings of Egypt.) At Achish’s court David was at once recognized as the warrior of whose exploits Israel sang in her streets — the man who had killed the Philistines’ greatest gibbor. He was in mortal danger.

David did what he had never done before and would never do again: he shinah es ta’amo, he altered his demeanor. He feigned madness, drooling upon his beard, marking the doors of the gate, playing the lunatic. Achish, unwilling to be occupied with such a figure, dismissed him with the famous line: “חֲסֵר מְשֻׁגָּעִים אָנִי כִּי־הֲבֵאתֶם אֶת־זֶה לְהִשְׁתַּגֵּעַ עָלָי” — “am I short of madmen, that you have brought this one to be a madman upon me?” David departed alive.

The Detail Preserved by Chazal

The meforshim, drawing on midrashic tradition, note a domestic detail that stood behind Achish’s exasperation.[30] Achish’s own household already numbered two meshugaim — his wife and his daughter — whose carrying-on filled the palace day and night. When David began his own performance in the courtyard, Achish had no patience for a third. The Holy One had arranged the historical circumstance that the ruse would land.

The Perek as the Song of the Delivered

The chapter of Tehillim that David composed after this deliverance opens: “אֲבָרְכָה אֶת ה' בְּכָל עֵת; תָּמִיד תְּהִלָּתוֹ בְּפִי”. It is a song of hoda’ah and bitachon, but of a specific character: it is the hoda’ah not of the conqueror who has prevailed by his strength, but of the fugitive who has been saved — the shirah of one who stood in a position of chisaron and was delivered by HaKadosh Baruch Hu alone. Every verse of the chapter reads in this key.

The Connection to Shabbos

A widely-received tradition among the mefarshei siddur and later ba’alei mussar reads David’s deliverance before Achish as having occurred on Shabbos, and locates the placement of Tehillim 34 among the Shabbos-morning additions in this reading.[31] The tradition is not explicit in the standard meforshim on Shmuel, and its precise textual foundation is not easily traced; but its coherence with the surrounding hashkafah of the day is striking. The Zoharic teaching of the seventh wing (chapter VIII) holds that on Shabbos HaKadosh Baruch Hu prefers not the polished song of the malachim at their strongest, but the song of Klal Yisrael from a position of vulnerability and emunah. David’s shirah after his deliverance before Achish is the exemplar of precisely this register: greatness expressed through smallness, praise arising from rescue rather than triumph. The perek belongs on the seventh day because the seventh day is the day of that particular shirah.

X. The Practical Order

The teachings of the earlier chapters yield a modest set of practical guides for the Shabbos-morning tefillah.

  • The individual should know his nusach — the placement of Baruch She’amar, the identity of the additional kapitlach, and the timing of the extended shirah.
  • Regarding Mizmor l’Todah, the individual follows the practice of his community: Ashkenazim, in accordance with Rashi, omit; certain Sephardic communities, in accordance with the Tur, recite.
  • Tefillin are not worn on Shabbos. The prohibition is not merely one of superfluity; it involves the very sanctity of the day, whose ois-status renders an additional sign not only unnecessary but a diminishment.
  • Whatever the beginning-time of the minyan, the individual guards sof zeman Kriyas Shema and sof zeman Tefillah. If the pace of the shaliach tzibbur threatens the zeman, the individual interrupts his own Pesukei d’Zimra and recites Kriyas Shema at the appointed time.
  • Pesukei d’Zimra is said b’nachas u’b’kavvanah. From Baruch She’amar through Yishtabach, no ordinary speech is permitted. On Shabbos, the recitation is aloud and with niggun: the seventh day is the day of song.
  • For the latecomer: Baruch She’amar, Ashrei, Yishtabach, Nishmas. The extras are not me’akev. Nishmas is not to be missed.

XI. Closing

The Shabbos-morning tefillah, in the aggregate, is the shape given to Jewish prayer on the day upon which the covenantal sign is worn by the day itself. Its omissions and its additions, its timing and its voice, are the tradition’s answer to a single underlying question: how does one who lives in a covenant address the King on the day when the King is Himself present? The korban todah retreats, for the yachid does not bring his private offering when the day’s own korbanos tzibbur stand in its place. The tefillin retreat, for the day itself is the sign. The pace lengthens and the voice rises, for the day gives time to be given. And the added kapitlach, sung by Klal Yisrael as the seventh wing, are not additions in the ordinary sense; they are the song of a creation that on the seventh day has been entrusted, in the deepest of its rooms, to those who stand in the covenant.

Each note runs short citation first (for quick lookup), then an expanded elaboration. Footnotes run sequentially with the body text.

  1. The two axes of Shabbos-morning departure — additions and omissions — discussed generally in Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chaim רפ”א and following. — The additions are principally within Pesukei d’Zimra; the omissions turn most visibly on Mizmor l’Todah. Each nusach shapes both axes differently, but the two categories — addition and omission — are the organizing framework of the entire Shabbos-morning divergence from the weekday form. ↩
  2. Rashi b’siddur — the omission of Mizmor l’Todah on Shabbos. — The siddur attributed to Rashi and preserved in manuscript records this practice explicitly. The ruling was taken up by minhag Tzarfat and, through French authority, became fixed in the standard Ashkenazic nusach. The siddur’s specific formulation of the reasoning varies between manuscripts; the standard reference for the reasoning in codified form is the Tur’s summary in Orach Chaim נ”א. ↩
  3. Categories of korban todah — Mishnah Berachos 9:5; Zevachim ch. 5. — The four categories of danger for which a korban todah is brought — sea, desert, illness, imprisonment — are enumerated by Chazal. The korban is a korban yachid, brought by the individual who has been saved, and is therefore not offered on Shabbos, when only korbanos tzibbur are brought. ↩
  4. Forty loaves in four categories — Mishnah Menachos 7:1. — The korban todah is accompanied by forty lachmei todah in four categories: ten challos matzos, ten rekikin, ten challos revuchah, and ten challos chametz. The Mizmor l’Todah (Tehillim 100) contains an introductory title-verse followed by exactly four content-verses; the meforshim read this as a remez to the four categories of loaves, sharpening the reading that the recitation is a verbal analog of the physical offering. ↩
  5. Tur, Orach Chaim siman נ”א. — The Tur’s treatment of the laws of Pesukei d’Zimra. He records both positions and rules against Rashi’s omission on the ground that reciting a chapter of Tehillim is not itself chillul Shabbos, nor is there any real concern that a Jew will infer from the recitation an obligation to bring the physical korban. Sephardic practice preserved the Tur’s ruling. ↩
  6. Rambam, Seder haTefillah, appended to Sefer Ahavah. — The Rambam’s outline of the daily Pesukei d’Zimra does not include Mizmor l’Todah. The Rambam’s framework therefore constitutes a third position alongside Rashi and the Tur: for the Rambam, the mizmor is not part of the fixed daily Pesukei d’Zimra at all, and its Shabbos-status is not an independent question. Later authorities working within the received Ashkenazic and Sephardic frameworks — in which the mizmor is a daily recitation — effectively choose between Rashi and the Tur. ↩
  7. Levush, Orach Chaim, laws of Pesukei d’Zimra and laws of Pesach. — The Levush (Rabbeinu Mordechai Yaffe, 1530–1612) observes that Mizmor l’Todah is omitted also on Erev Pesach and throughout Pesach. The reason is that the korban todah included ten loaves of chametz, and the offering itself would be problematic in a season when chametz is prohibited. The parallel omission strengthens Rashi’s reading that the mizmor-recitation is bound tightly to the physical structure of the korban. ↩
  8. The Vilna Gaon’s approach to Pesukei d’Zimra — Ma’aseh Rav and Siddur haGra. — The Vilna Gaon treated Pesukei d’Zimra as a closed and precisely-bounded unit. He was makpid upon the recitation of Mizmor l’Todah in its proper form on the days it is said, and equally makpid upon its omission on days — Shabbos and around Pesach — when it is not to be said. The Gaon’s siddur and the practice preserved by his talmidim in Ma’aseh Rav gave the Ashkenazic omission additional structural weight beyond the bare authority of Rashi. ↩
  9. Menachos ל”ו ע”ב. — The canonical sugya of Shabbos as ois: שַׁבָּתוֹת וְיָמִים טוֹבִים לָאו זְמַן תְּפִלִּין נִינְהוּ, שֶׁכֵּן אִיקְּרוּ אוֹת. The three mitzvos called ois in the Torah: milah (Bereishis 17:11), tefillin (Shemos 13:9), and Shabbos (Shemos 31:17). Related discussion in Berachos ch. 2, treating Kriyas Shema and tefillin. ↩
  10. Sma”g, Mitzvas Aseh of tefillin; Maharsha, Chidushei Aggados on Berachos י”ד. — The Sma”g (Rabbi Moshe of Coucy, mid-13th c.), in his exposition of the mitzvah of tefillin, gives the ois exclusion the frame of Devarim 19:15: עַל־פִּי שְׁנֵי עֵדִים אוֹ עַל־פִּי שְׁלֹשָׁה־עֵדִים יָקוּם דָּבָר. Two eidim suffice for a matter to stand; a third is not required. The framework is cited and elaborated in the Maharsha’s Chidushei Aggados. On weekdays, milah and tefillin constitute the two witnesses to the covenantal relationship; on Shabbos, the two witnesses are milah and Shabbos itself. ↩
  11. Zilzul b’kedushas Shabbos — the Sifrei HaKedoshim and later kabbalistic sources. — The reading that tefillin on Shabbos are not merely superfluous but constitute a diminishment of the sanctity of the day is developed in the kabbalistic literature and is invoked as the reason for treating tefillin on Shabbos as muktzeh. The practical halakhah is uniform; the categorization as zilzul supplies a svara for the stringency of the treatment. ↩
  12. The parable of the imperial portrait — circulated in the name of the Chasam Sofer, the Ksav Sofer, and Rav Shimon Sofer of Krakow. — The parable of the removed imperial portrait, cited to Emperor Franz Joseph of Austria-Hungary (r. 1848–1916), circulates in the tradition with variant attributions: to the Chasam Sofer, to his son the Ksav Sofer, and to Rav Shimon Sofer, chief rabbi of Krakow and grandson of the Chasam Sofer. The precise historical setting varies with the telling; the svara the parable transmits — that on Shabbos the King is Himself present, and that tefillin would be a portrait when the King is in the room — is what the tradition has preserved and passed forward, independent of the resolution of its exact provenance. ↩
  13. Ba’al HaMaor, Ramban, and Rashba on Menachos ל”ו. — The dispute concerning the character of the exclusion of tefillin from Shabbos. The Ba’al HaMaor holds that the exclusion is d’oraisa: the Torah itself, through the ois framework, removes Shabbos from the days of the mitzvah. The Ramban, in his hasagos on the Ba’al HaMaor, and the Rashba, argue for a d’rabbanan reading: the exclusion is a rabbinic derivation. Practical halakhah is uniform; the significance of the dispute lies in cases of doubt and in the character of the exclusion as such. The ois framework of the Sma”g/Maharsha (fn 10) functions as a hashkafic overlay that gives the practical halakhah coherence within either Rishonic camp. ↩
  14. Zohar, Parshas Pinchas; Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chaim ל”א; Ma’aseh Rav (Vilna Gaon). — The Zohar in Parshas Pinchas treats Yom Tov as similar to Shabbos with respect to ois-status. The Shulchan Aruch records the machloikes concerning tefillin on Chol HaMoed. The Vilna Gaon, following the Zohar, ruled not to wear tefillin on Chol HaMoed. Most Litvish yeshivos and the great majority of chassidishe communities follow this practice. Other Ashkenazic communities, following the Rambam and earlier Ashkenazi Rishonim, put on tefillin on Chol HaMoed without a berachah. (An earlier draft of the source study for this material inverted the Gaon’s position; the ruling as stated here is the standard.) ↩
  15. Ramo, Orach Chaim רפ”א:א. — The Ramo’s gloss records the minhag to daven Shacharis of Shabbos somewhat later than during the week. He does not specify how much later, and gives no reason in the siman itself; the reasoning is developed in the earlier Rishonic sources (fn 16–17) and in the later acharonim (fn 20–22). ↩
  16. Mordechai on Shabbos, perek Ha-Zorek, in the name of Rav Hai Gaon. — The Mordechai (Rabbeinu Mordechai ben Hillel, d. 1298), in his commentary on Shabbos, records a tradition heard by one of the Rishonim in Rome from Rav Hai Gaon (d. 1038), the last of the Geonim: on Shabbos morning one davens Shacharis later than on weekdays. The textual basis is Bamidbar 28:4 (בַּבֹּקֶר בַּבֹּקֶר, of the daily tamid) contrasted with Bamidbar 28:9 (וּבְיוֹם הַשַּׁבָּת, without the ba’boker qualifier). ↩
  17. Maharil, Hilchos Tefillah. — The Maharil (Rabbeinu Yaakov Molin, c. 1360–1427), the great codifier of Ashkenaz minhag, defends the Mordechai’s reading by claiming that even the morning tamid of Shabbos was brought later than the weekday tamid, since the kohanim slept later on Shabbos morning. The claim is not directly grounded in a chazal passage and functions as an interpretive move to close the textual objection that וּבְיוֹם הַשַּׁבָּת introduces mussaf, not tamid. ↩
  18. Shu”t Radvaz, defense of the Rav Hai Gaon tradition. — The Radvaz (Rabbi David ibn Zimra, 1479–1573, chief rabbi of Egypt) defends the Mordechai’s tradition on the ground that Rav Hai Gaon’s words carry the weight of divrei kabbalah even when the reasoning does not fully persuade. He then closes the teshuvah with a personal repudiation: chas v’shalom that he should sleep late and daven late on Shabbos morning. He calls the practice not minhag yafeh and not minhag hagun, and states that he himself davens vasikin every Shabbos. Standard reference is Shu”t Radvaz; the exact siman varies with printings. ↩
  19. Megillah כ”ג ע”א with Rashi ad loc. — The Gemara distinguishes Yom Tov, on which the tzibbur davens late (Rashi: because the household wakes early to cook the seudah), from Shabbos, on which the tzibbur should daven earlier. Rashi comments explicitly that on Shabbos one should daven vasikin. The sugya supports the Radvaz’s personal practice and works against the Rav Hai Gaon tradition of a late Shabbos-morning start. ↩
  20. Sheinah b’Shabbos ta’anug — acharonim, general. — Many acharonim prefer a simpler account of the later Shabbos-morning tefillah: the halakhic principle of sheinah b’Shabbos ta’anug supplies a category of oneg Shabbos that justifies the community’s later start, and the Rav Hai Gaon pasuk-argument is best understood as an asmachta. This is the reading most commonly invoked in the popular halakhic literature. ↩
  21. Alternative Rishonic reading — the interval used for Shnayim Mikra v’Echad Targum. — A different Rishonic reading holds that the later Shabbos davening is an accommodation not for sleep but for study, specifically for the weekly obligation of Shnayim Mikra v’Echad Targum. On this reading, the later davening is a gift only to those who use the added time for Torah; it is not a license for sleep. Attributions vary among the Rishonim; the reading is associated with the Shibbolei Ha-Leket and other early codifiers. ↩
  22. Beis Yosef on Orach Chaim רפ”א. — A third rationale: the delay is a communal accommodation, allowing latecomers to reach the tzibbur before it has advanced too far into the tefillah, so that tefillah b’tzibbur is more fully realized. The Beis Yosef discusses this along with the other traditions in his treatment of the siman. ↩
  23. Mishnah Berurah, Orach Chaim נ”ח and following. — The Mishnah Berurah rules that if the pace of the tzibbur threatens sof zeman Kriyas Shema, the individual must interrupt his own progress through Pesukei d’Zimra to say Kriyas Shema at the zeman. At a minimum the first verse is said with kavvanah; preferably the entire Kriyas Shema. The corresponding ruling applies for sof zeman Tefillah. ↩
  24. Mishnah Berurah, Orach Chaim נ”א and following, in the name of the Arizal. — The distinction between Pesukei d’Zimra during the week (recited b’lachash) and on Shabbos (recited aloud, with niggun) is brought by the Mishnah Berurah in the name of the Arizal. In the Lurianic framework the weekday lachash accompanies specific yichudim of concealment, and the Shabbos song is tied to the expanded da’as of the day. Practically the distinction is most vividly preserved in chassidishe shuls, where the entire Shabbos-morning Pesukei d’Zimra is sung; the halakhah as codified in the Mishnah Berurah is uniform across nuscha’os. ↩
  25. Mishnah Berurah, prohibition of pressuring the shaliach tzibbur. — The Mishnah Berurah rules that if the shaliach tzibbur is ma’arich in Pesukei d’Zimra, a member of the tzibbur may not protest even l’shem shamayim. The ikkar din is that Pesukei d’Zimra is to be said b’nachas u’b’kavvanah, and the Shabbos-morning latitude for the shaliach tzibbur to be ma’arich is halakhically established. The ruling appears in the general discussion of Pesukei d’Zimra in siman נ”א and following. ↩
  26. Berachah masgeres — Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chaim נ”א. — Baruch She’amar and Yishtabach together form the berachah masgeres that brackets Pesukei d’Zimra. The parallel structural rule is to a berachah before eating: no ordinary speech between the berachah and its fulfillment. Sichas chullin is forbidden between Baruch She’amar and Yishtabach; devar mitzvah or a matter of tzorech gadol may be permitted according to the standards laid out in the siman. ↩
  27. Mishnah Berurah — order of priorities for the latecomer on Shabbos morning. — The Mishnah Berurah gives the explicit sequence: Baruch She’amar; Ashrei (Tehillim 145); Yishtabach; Nishmas Kol Chai. The additional Shabbos kapitlach are not me’akev, and may be omitted if time is short and completed later if desired. Nishmas is not to be omitted; it is the halakhic center of the Shabbos-specific shirah. ↩
  28. Zohar cited in Tosafos, Sanhedrin ל”ז; Yeshayahu 6:2. — Tosafos on Sanhedrin ל”ז brings the Zoharic teaching on the six wings of the Serafim. Full pasuk: שְׂרָפִים עֹמְדִים מִמַּעַל לוֹ, שֵׁשׁ כְּנָפַיִם שֵׁשׁ כְּנָפַיִם לְאֶחָד; בִּשְׁתַּיִם יְכַסֶּה פָנָיו וּבִשְׁתַּיִם יְכַסֶּה רַגְלָיו וּבִשְׁתַּיִם יְעוֹפֵף. Each wing corresponds to one day of the week and to the malachim’s song for that day. On Shabbos, no wing remains; the office of shirah passes to Knesses Yisrael, the seventh wing. The added kapitlach in Shabbos Pesukei d’Zimra are the substance of the song thus entrusted. ↩
  29. Shmuel Aleph 21; Tehillim 34 with meforshim. — The narrative of David’s deliverance before Achish in Gat appears in Shmuel Aleph 21:11–16. Feigning madness, David drooled on his beard and marked the doors of the gate; Achish dismissed him with the famous rebuke that he had no shortage of madmen in his own household. The superscription of Tehillim 34 refers to this episode. “Avimelech” in the superscription is the generic title of the kings of the Philistines, corresponding to “Paro” of Egypt, as explained in Midrash Tehillim on the perek. ↩
  30. Midrash Shmuel and meforshim on Shmuel Aleph 21. — The domestic detail that Achish’s own household included two meshugaim — his wife and his daughter — is preserved in the midrashic and meforshic literature on the passage. The reading gives Achish’s exasperated dismissal its texture: he had no room for a third, and David’s ruse turned on that domestic pressure. The meforshim read this as one of the many small circumstances arranged by HaKadosh Baruch Hu for the deliverance to unfold. ↩
  31. The tradition of the deliverance on Shabbos — mefarshei siddur and ba’alei mussar. — The tradition that David’s deliverance before Achish occurred on Shabbos, and that this is the reason for the placement of Tehillim 34 among the Shabbos-morning additions, is widely received among the mefarshei siddur and later ba’alei mussar. It is not explicit in the standard meforshim on Shmuel, and its precise textual foundation is not easily traced; the reading is preserved and passed forward as a mesorah whose coherence with the surrounding hashkafah of the day — particularly the seventh-wing teaching of fn 28 — is what gives it its enduring place. Later chassidishe and mussar sources treat it as an established reading; the reader who seeks textual anchoring will find the tradition itself preserved in oral form more consistently than in printed form. ↩
