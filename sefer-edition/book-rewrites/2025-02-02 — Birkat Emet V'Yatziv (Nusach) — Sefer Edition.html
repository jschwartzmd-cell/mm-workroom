בִּרְכַּת אֱמֶת וְיַצִּיב — מָקוֹר, נֻסָּח, וּסְמִיכַת גְּאֻלָּה לִתְפִלָּה

Birkat Emet V'Yatziv — Origins, Nusach, and Semichat Geulah L'Tefillah
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Sunday Morning Series on Tefillah
Recorded 2 February 2025  ·  Sefer Edition · Revised 23 August 2026

L'ilui nishmas ha-tzaddik ha-gadol R' Levi Yitzchak, zt"l, sponsored by Rabbi Yehuda Pearlstein — and b'simcha, in honor of the birth of a grandchild in the Schwab family to Mayor Weissman.

I. The Question

The blessing that follows Krias Shema (קריאת שמע) in the morning — known by its opening words as Birkat Emet V'Yatziv (ברכת אמת ויציב) and by its chasimah (חתימה) as Birkat Ga'al Yisrael (ברכת גאל ישראל) — is already fixed in Jewish liturgy by the time of the Mishnah and Gemara. The sugyos in Berachos and Tamid take it as given, presuming its recitation in the days of the Tannaim (תנאים) and Amoraim (אמוראים) alike. Yet the questions it raises are not thereby settled. Why does the berachah not begin with the word Baruch (ברוך)? Who composed it? When was it fixed into the daily order? And why has it been placed precisely here — between Krias Shema and Shemoneh Esrei (שמונה עשרה) — and not elsewhere?

The Rishonim (ראשונים) offer four distinct mehalchim (מהלכים). To these will be added a fifth from the Ba'alei HaTosafos, and beneath all five a midrashic substrate that explains why the blessing must stand where it stands. The five shitos are not, in the end, competing accounts of one historical event. They are best read as strata — a single received lashon to which each generation added its own layer of meaning, its own reason for preservation, its own reading of a text older than any of them.

II. The First Mahalach — The Rokeach: A Response to a Plague

The Sefer HaRokeach (ספר הרוקח),[1] in his Hilchos Tefillah, preserves a mesorah (מסורה) of great antiquity. A terrible gezerah (גזרה) fell upon Eretz Yisrael (ארץ ישראל): a plague was consuming a hundred Jews each day. The bnei Eretz Yisrael (בני ארץ ישראל) dispatched an urgent letter to the great yeshivos (ישיבות) of Bavel (בבל): what were they to do?

The reply that came back was not, as one might have expected, the institution of one hundred berachos daily. It was, instead, the composition of a single tefillah to be recited each morning, containing exactly one hundred letters (in another girsa, one hundred words) from “ve-yatziv” through “l'olam va'ed.” Recited daily, it would lift the plague. Upon this mesorah the Rokeach adds only two words: kibalti kein — “so I have received.” The Anshei Knesset HaGedolah (אנשי כנסת הגדולה) later embedded this tefillah permanently in the daily siddur.

What this mahalach does not explain is the placement. Why here — adjacent to Krias Shema? The Rokeach speaks to the origin of the lashon; he does not yet speak to why it comes to rest at this seam of the tefillah.

The Rokeach — R' Elazar b'R' Yehudah of Worms

The Rokeach is R' Elazar b'R' Yehudah of Worms (רבי אלעזר בן יהודה מגרמייזא), among the most influential of the German Rishonim and cited constantly by the Beis Yosef — he is, for example, the first source for the minhag not to eat kitniyos (קטניות) on Pesach. The Sefer HaRokeach itself was born of a tragedy almost too painful to relate. In the winter of תתקנ"ו (1195/96), three nights before Chanukah, marauders forced their way into R' Elazar's home in Worms and murdered before his eyes his wife Dulcea (דולצא), his thirteen-year-old daughter Bellette (בעלא), and his six-year-old daughter Hannah (חנה). He and his son escaped, wounded, behind a locked inner door.

How does an oveid Hashem answer such a moment? R' Elazar composed a kinah upon his martyrs and made a resolve: he would become a greater oveid Hashem and yerei shamayim than before; he would set down in writing every halachah he possessed, and gather it into a sefer as zechuyos for teshuvah. The Sefer HaRokeach is that sefer. The daily halachos the tradition lives by — among them, the very count of the hundred letters of Emet V'Yatziv — are in part the quiet answer of one man to his own churban.

III. The Second Mahalach — Rashi: Eighteen Kiyumim

The Shibolei HaLeket (שיבולי הלקט), in the name of Rashi HaKadosh (רש"י הקדוש) — and reproduced likewise in the Machzor Vitry (מחזור ויטרי),[2] which preserves the minhagei beis midrasho shel Rashi — opens the berachah to a different reading altogether. Let the eye count the affirmations that stand at its head:

אֱמֶת, וְיַצִּיב, וְנָכוֹן, וְקַיָּם, וְיָשָׁר, וְנֶאֱמָן, וְאָהוּב, וְחָבִיב, וְנֶחְמָד, וְנָעִים, וְנוֹרָא, וְאַדִּיר, וּמְתֻקָּן, וּמְקֻבָּל, וְטוֹב, וְיָפֶה, הַדָּבָר הַזֶּה — לְעוֹלָם וָעֶד.
Nusach ha-berachah as reflected in Machzor Vitry

Eighteen kiyumim (קיומים). Chazal (חז"ל) fixed the berachah at this precise seam of the tefillah as preparation for Shemoneh Esrei: the mispallel is about to recite the eighteen berachos of Amidah (עמידה), and he prefaces them with eighteen affirmations of truth and acceptance. On this reading the placement becomes structurally lucid, and the phrase “ha-davar ha-zeh” at the close of the enumeration points forward — to the Shemoneh Esrei that is about to be davened.

Shimon HaPakuli at Yavneh

The Gemara in Berachos[3] records that Shimon HaPakuli (שמעון הפקולי) — a Tanna of remarkable memory whose epithet reflects his profession as a merchant in cotton (pakuli) — arranged the eighteen berachos of Amidah before Rabban Gamliel (רבן גמליאל) in the yeshivah at Yavneh. On the Rashi-Machzor Vitry reading, the nasi of Eretz Yisrael then wrote to Bavel: “This is the nusach of Shemoneh Esrei.” The cover language of that letter — “emet ve-yatziv ve-nachon ve-kayam ha-davar ha-zeh” — was preserved verbatim by the recipients and became, in time, the introduction to the Shemoneh Esrei itself.

Why No Opening “Baruch”?

The absence of Baruch at the head of the berachah follows from the same architecture. The Gemara insists on connecting “emet” directly to the closing “Hashem Elokeichem” of Krias Shema; the chain runs unbroken back into Ahavah Rabbah (אהבה רבה) and forward to the chasimah at “Ga'al Yisrael.” By the codified klal of the Shulchan Aruch (שולחן ערוך),[4] a berachah ha-semuchah la-chaverta (ברכה הסמוכה לחברתה) requires no opening Baruch. Emet V'Yatziv is the classic instance of that klal.

IV. The Third Mahalach — The Shibolei HaLeket: A Restatement of Shema

In his own peshat — drawn, as he tells his readers, mi-ha-Geonim she-b'Romi (from the Geonim of Rome), and citing also his brother Binyamin (“Yamin”),[5] whose transmission is preserved in the Beis Yosef — the Shibolei HaLeket reads “ha-davar ha-zeh” not forward, toward Amidah, but backward: to the three parshiyos of Krias Shema that have just been recited. The berachah is the acceptance and restatement, in the mispallel's own words rather than the words of the Torah, of the three yesodos just declared:

  • “Elokei olam malkeinu” answers to kabbalas ol malchus shamayim (קבלת עול מלכות שמים) of the first parshah.
  • “Ashrei ish she-yishma le-mitzvosecha” answers to kabbalas ol mitzvos (קבלת עול מצוות) of Ve-hayah im shamo'a.
  • The long passages of Yetzias Mitzrayim (יציאת מצרים) answer to parshas tzitzis (פרשת ציצית).

And the coda — “aleinu ve-al baneinu ve-al doroseinu” — declares that this kabbalah is not for the mispallel alone. It is a permanent covenant, binding him and those who will come after him. The Shema is not merely read; on this mahalach, it is received.

V. The Fourth Mahalach — The Seder HaYom: Ezra's Letter to the Diaspora

The Sefer Seder HaYom (ספר סדר היום),[6] composed by R' Moshe ben Machir (רבי משה בן מכיר) in Tzfas (צפת) and printed there in 1599, preserves a tradition whose theological reach exceeds all that has come before. R' Moshe ben Machir was a contemporary of the Arizal (האר"י) at the high tide of Lurianic Kabbalah; he is the first authority to record Modeh Ani (מודה אני) in writing, and his sefer is the earliest systematized guide to the arc of a Jew's day.

The Tradition

When Ezra HaSofer (עזרא הסופר), after the seventy years of galus Bavel, sought to gather Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל) back to Eretz Yisrael for the building of Bayis Sheni, he discovered that during those seven decades many yehudim had spread far beyond the Babylonian heartland — into the Germanic lands, into Sefarad (ספרד). To those far communities he sent letters: “Come up. We have a Beis HaMikdash. Kibbutz galuyos.”

Their reply is preserved in the Seder HaYom:

לָמָּה נִכְפּוֹל יְגוֹנֵנוּ?
“Why should we double our sorrow?”

They possessed a mesorah, so they wrote back, that only Bayis Shlishi would stand forever; Bayis Sheni would in time be destroyed and its people again scattered. They had no wish to endure that suffering twice. And they continued, in the very lashon the tradition now recites each morning: “You have Yerushalayim ha-gedolah; we have Yerushalayim ha-ketanah. You have a Beis HaMikdash; we have a mikdash me'at, our beis ha-kenesses. We are settled, we are observant, we believe — but we are remaining. Emet, ve-yatziv, ve-nachon, ve-kayam, ve-yashar... Elokei olam malkeinu, emet... aleinu ve-al baneinu ve-al doroseinu — we are Klal Yisrael like you; so too our children after us.”

The Anshei Knesset HaGedolah, on this account, embedded that letter into the daily tefillah. The berachah stands at the seam between Krias Shema and Shemoneh Esrei as a permanent reminder to those who did return: the brothers who did not are, no less, ma'aminim bnei ma'aminim; they too are Klal Yisrael; the covenant runs through them as it runs through us.

Toledo and Worms — The Communities That Stayed

Two historical sefarim preserve the memory of these diaspora communities. The Shalsheles HaKabbalah (שלשלת הקבלה) of R' Gedalya ibn Yachya of Italy (sixteenth century)[7] — a work whose material must, according to the Chida and others, be sifted with care[8] — and the more universally trusted Seder HaDoros (סדר הדורות) of R' Yechiel Heilprin,[9] the eighteenth-century Rosh Yeshivah of Minsk, both identify Toledo (טולידו) and Worms (וורמייזא) among the communities whose founding traced not to Bayis Sheni but to the earlier scattering that followed churban Bayis Rishon.

The stakes of this genealogy were not merely antiquarian. Through two millennia of Christian galus, the alilah most frequently pressed against European Jewry was the charge of complicity in the crucifixion — a charge that furnished the pretext for legislation, expulsion, and slaughter. The Jews of Toledo and Worms answered: “We arrived on these shores at the destruction of the First Temple; we have no historical connection to events of the Second.” The argument was, at moments, effective — but the historical record preserves this defense as mesorah more than as decree, and the Shalsheles records also its shadow: the very communities that did not answer Ezra's call would suffer, across the centuries, some of the harshest pera'os of European galus. The tradition draws its own heshbon; the sefer is content to record, not to adjudicate.

VI. The Fifth Mahalach — The Ba'alei HaTosafos: The Vindication of Moshe

The Rokeach traces the lashon to Bavel; the Rashi tradition to Yavneh; the Shibolei HaLeket to Rome; the Seder HaYom to the diaspora of the returnees. The Daas Zekenim mi-Ba'alei HaTosafos (דעת זקנים מבעלי התוספות),[10] at the opening of Parshas Pekudei, traces it further back still — to Moshe Rabbeinu (משה רבינו) himself.

The Mishkan (משכן) is called “Mishkan ha-eidus.” One reason lies in the Aron ha-eidus housed within it. The Daas Zekenim supplies a second, which turns on a moment of unease in the early days of its construction.

When Moshe presented to Klal Yisrael the accounting of the terumas ha-Mishkan, the books did not balance. A portion of the silver appeared to be missing. Whispers spread through the camp: had Moshe, of all men, kept back what belonged to the Mishkan? He turned to HaKadosh Baruch Hu in tefillah: “Help me understand.” The answer came: “You have forgotten the vavim la-amudim — the silver hooks of the pillars. The missing funds are there.”

Moshe returned at once to the people. Even a manhig — even the greatest of manhigim — must fulfill “vi-hiyisem neki'im me-Hashem u-mi-Yisrael” (והייתם נקיים מה' ומישראל),[11] the double obligation of transparency to HaKadosh Baruch Hu and to those in his charge. He stood before them and declared: “Emet, ve-yatziv, ve-nachon” — the lashon is his — to establish the accounting as accurate and to clear his name. So the Daas Zekenim. And Chazal, sensing the enduring weight of that lashon, took it into the daily siddur as a remez to the vavim la-amudim and to the moment in which the integrity of a manhig was vindicated before the people.

VII. The Midrashic Substrate — Va-Ya'aminu

Beneath the five mehalchim, and older than any of them, lies a midrash on which the entire architecture of semichas ge'ulah li-tefillah depends. The pasuk at Krias Yam Suf (קריעת ים סוף) records:[12]

וַיַּאֲמִינוּ בַּה' וּבְמֹשֶׁה עַבְדּוֹ.
And they believed in Hashem and in Moshe His servant.

The Mechilta:[13]

וּבִזְכוּת הָאֱמוּנָה שֶׁהֶאֱמִינוּ, זָכוּ לוֹמַר שִׁירָה וְשָׁרְתָה עֲלֵיהֶם שְׁכִינָה.
And in the merit of the emunah in which they believed, they merited to recite shirah, and the Shechinah rested upon them.

Three stages, in a fixed order: emunah begets shirah; shirah draws down hashra'as ha-Shechinah (השראת השכינה). This is the pattern that governs Krias Yam Suf, and this is the pattern that governs the daily tefillah. The mispallel declares Krias Shema — the primary kabbalah of emunah. He passes into Emet V'Yatziv, which speaks explicitly of Yetzias Mitzrayim and Krias Yam Suf and gathers together the shirah already recited in Pesukei d'Zimra. And only then, having recapitulated the sequence, does he approach Shemoneh Esrei — the avodah that requires hashra'as ha-Shechinah.

The particular vehicle matters. Any liturgical bridge might, in principle, have joined Krias Shema to Amidah; the tradition selected this one because its content re-enacts precisely the emunah-into-shirah-into-Shechinah arc that Chazal read out of Va-ya'aminu. The berachah that begins by affirming the emunah of the Shema and ends at “Ga'al Yisrael” is the daily miniature of the Yam Suf; it is the ground upon which the mispallel may stand before his Master. Moshe Rabbeinu — on the reading of the Daas Zekenim, and on the reading of the Mechilta both — had this in mind from the first.

VIII. The Nusach of Tzur Yisrael — Five Times Yisrael

Having followed the origins of the berachah's opening and its guf, the sefer turns to its close. The chasimah is preceded by the paragraph “Tzur Yisrael (צור ישראל) kumah be-ezras Yisrael...”, and in the Ashkenazic nusach the name Yisrael is invoked within it five times.

R' Meir Shatz in Worms

The Worms of Rashi's era, at the close of the eleventh century, possessed no printed siddurim; the nusach lay in the kisvei yad of a handful of talmidei chachamim. The leading shaliach tzibbur of Rashi's beis midrash was R' Meir Shatz (רבי מאיר ש"ץ) — the title Shatz (Shaliach Tzibbur), so central to his life's work, becoming in time his very surname. R' Meir was a paytan and mechabeir tefillos of the first rank; Rashi cites his nuschaos repeatedly. Into the Tzur Yisrael paragraph he inserted a pasuk from Yeshayahu (47:4) — “ve-ne'emar go'aleinu, Hashem Tzeva'os Shemo, Kedosh Yisrael” — not composing new language but placing an existing verse where it would complete a fivefold invocation of Yisrael, on the model of a parshah in Parshas Beha'aloscha[14] in which the name Yisrael appears five times in the context of the avodah of the Levi'im.

This is an Ashkenazic hosafah; the Sefaradim do not recite it, and the Sephardic close retains a briefer form.

The Rokeach's Count

An alternative mesorah from the Rokeach retains only four mentions of Yisrael and does not insert the Yeshayahu pasuk. On that count the paragraph reads out at exactly sixty letters and fourteen words, a remez to the fourteenth of Nisan — Yud-Daled Nisan — the date of the Yetzias Mitzrayim that Tzur Yisrael commemorates. The two mesoros are not, in the end, in conflict. The accepted Ashkenazic nusach follows R' Meir Shatz and structures the paragraph around five Yisraels; the Rokeach's fourfold count survives within it as an inner remez, the older skeleton on which R' Meir laid the completing verse.

IX. Synthesis — Strata of a Single Text

Five mehalchim stand on the page, and the reader is entitled to ask: which is the true history of the berachah?

The answer, if it is not to force a choice among Rishonim and Ba'alei HaTosafos and a mekubal of Tzfas, is that the five accounts do not compete for a single moment of authorship. They describe strata. A core lashon of surpassing dignity — “emet, ve-yatziv, ve-nachon” — enters the world with Moshe Rabbeinu at his vindication in Pekudei. It is taken up, in the era of Ezra, by the diaspora of the returnees, who reply to their brothers' summons with the words that their own leader had spoken to Klal Yisrael centuries earlier. It is embedded by the Anshei Knesset HaGedolah into the daily siddur. It is shaped, in a moment of gezerah, by the chachamim of Bavel into a hundred-letter tefillah whose numerical form is a refuah. And it is framed, in the days of Rabban Gamliel, by an eighteenfold enumeration that prepares the mispallel for Shemoneh Esrei.

Each stratum answers a different question. The Daas Zekenim tells the reader where the lashon came from. The Seder HaYom tells him why it was preserved. The Anshei Knesset HaGedolah tell him where it was embedded. The Rokeach tells him what numerical segulah was fixed within it. The Rashi tradition tells him why it stands precisely at the seam it stands at, and why the eighteen affirmations open the way to eighteen berachos. And beneath all five, the Mechilta tells the reader why any bridge between Krias Shema and Shemoneh Esrei is required at all: because emunah begets shirah, and shirah draws down the Shechinah without whose presence the mispallel may not enter into the avodah of Amidah.

Not a competition. A layered inheritance. And the daily tefillah is the seam at which every one of those strata is present at once.

The specific se'if in 66 that bears on the Emet V'Yatziv question is se'if 9. On this klal the Emet V'Yatziv's absence of an opening Baruch is fully accounted for: the berachah is a continuation of Ahavah Rabbah through the unbroken Hashem Elokeichem emet bridge, sealed at Ga'al Yisrael. ↩

R' Tzidkiyah b'R' Avraham HaRofeh, Sefer Shibolei HaLeket, Hilchos Krias Shema; with reference to his brother Binyamin (“Yamin”); cited by the Beis Yosef. — The minhag Romi reading: “ha-davar ha-zeh” points backward to Krias Shema; the berachah restates its three yesodos — malchus, mitzvos, Yetzias Mitzrayim — in the mispallel's own lashon, extending the kabbalah to aleinu ve-al baneinu ve-al doroseinu. The berachah as a daily reminder that the brothers who did not return remain ma'aminim bnei ma'aminim. R' Moshe ben Machir is the first known written witness to this tradition; whether it rests on an earlier oral chain now lost to us, or represents a chiddush of the Tzfas circle, cannot be recovered from the available record. Its embrace by the tradition is on the ground of the Seder HaYom's stature rather than external corroboration. ↩
  7. R' Gedalya ibn Yachya, Shalsheles HaKabbalah (Italy, sixteenth century). — The sixteenth-century Italian historical work documenting Jewish history from creation through the author's own day. Identifies Toledo and Worms among the communities tracing to churban Bayis Rishon, and preserves the mesorah of the pre-Bayis-Sheni defense against blood-libel accusations. Papal endorsement of the defense is attributed within the polemical tradition to more than one pope; the specific attribution to Gregory IX and to King Philip of France that circulates in later summaries does not match the papal-royal chronology (Gregory IX, r. 1227–1241, was contemporary with Louis IX of France, not with any of the Philips) and rests on no verifiable primary source known to us. The Chida's counsel is not to reject the work but to distinguish carefully within it. The Toledo tradition of an inscribed stone in the central square declaring the community innocent of the crucifixion appears in the Shalsheles; it is preserved by the tradition as mesorah rather than confirmed by extant physical or archival evidence. ↩
  9. R' Yechiel Heilprin, Seder HaDoros (Minsk, eighteenth century). — The standard Jewish historical compendium for chronological and biographical questions on Tannaim, Amoraim, Rishonim, and Acharonim. R' Yechiel Heilprin (c. 1660–1746) was Rosh Yeshivah in Minsk in the generation preceding the Gr'a (1720–1797); the two overlapped in years but not in generation. Seder HaDoros corroborates the Toledo/Worms Bayis Rishon tradition preserved in the Shalsheles HaKabbalah. ↩
  10. Daas Zekenim mi-Ba'alei HaTosafos, opening of Parshas Pekudei. — The fifth mahalach: Moshe Rabbeinu's presentation of the accounting of terumas ha-Mishkan; the appearance of missing silver; the suspicion in Klal Yisrael; Moshe's tefillah; the revelation of the vavim la-amudim; the public declaration “emet, ve-yatziv, ve-nachon” as an act of vindication before the people. The lashon of the daily tefillah traced back to that moment of leadership integrity. The reading yields a chakirah the Rishonim do not close: does the mispallel who recites the berachah daily compose praise, or does he quote the verified truth of Moshe? The sefer records the tension without resolving it. ↩
  11. Bamidbar 32:22 — vi-hiyisem neki'im me-Hashem u-mi-Yisrael. — The principle of leadership transparency, articulated by Bnei Gad ve-Reuven on the eve of kibbush ha-aretz. A manhig must be visibly clear of suspicion before HaKadosh Baruch Hu and before the people in his charge. The pasuk supplies the halakhic frame for Moshe's public accounting in Pekudei and, on the Daas Zekenim's reading, for the very lashon of Emet V'Yatziv. ↩
  12. Shemos 14:31 — va-ya'aminu ba-Hashem u-ve-Moshe avdo. — The pasuk at Krias Yam Suf: the declaration of emunah in HaKadosh Baruch Hu and in His servant Moshe. The pasuk from which the Mechilta derives the daily reenactment underwriting semichas ge'ulah li-tefillah: emunah begets shirah, and shirah draws down hashra'as ha-Shechinah. ↩
  13. Mechilta de-R' Yishmael, Parshas Beshalach, Parshas HaShirah; parallels in Shemos Rabbah. — Bi-zechus ha-emunah she-he'eminu, zachu lomar shirah ve-shorsah aleihem Shechinah: the merit of emunah at Krias Yam Suf drawing forth shirah and hashra'as ha-Shechinah. The structural template for the daily order: Krias Shema as emunah; Pesukei d'Zimra together with Emet V'Yatziv as the shirah/Yetzias Mitzrayim; Shemoneh Esrei as the hashra'as ha-Shechinah that follows. This is why the specific bridge of Emet V'Yatziv is required: no other berachah in the liturgy carries the emunah/shirah/Yam Suf content that permits the arc to be re-enacted. ↩
  14. Bamidbar 8 (Parshas Beha'aloscha) — fivefold mention of Yisrael in the avodah of the Levi'im. — The scriptural model for R' Meir Shatz's placement of ve-ne'emar go'aleinu, Hashem Tzeva'os Shemo, Kedosh Yisrael (Yeshayahu 47:4) into Tzur Yisrael: the paragraph structured around a fivefold invocation of Yisrael. R' Meir Shatz did not compose the lashon; the chiddush was the placement of an existing pasuk at the point that would complete the fivefold pattern. The insertion is Ashkenazic; the Sephardic nusach preserves a briefer form. The Rokeach's alternative count — four Yisraels, sixty letters, fourteen words — survives within the Ashkenazic nusach as an inner remez to Yud-Daled Nisan. ↩
