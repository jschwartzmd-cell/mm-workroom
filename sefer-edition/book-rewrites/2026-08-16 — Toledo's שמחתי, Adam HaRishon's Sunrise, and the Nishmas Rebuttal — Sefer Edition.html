תּוֹסָפוֹת שַׁחֲרִית שֶׁל שַׁבָּת — שָׂמַחְתִּי, מִזְמוֹר שִׁיר, וְנִשְׁמַת כָּל חַי

Toledo's שָׂמַחְתִּי, Adam HaRishon's First Sunrise, and the Rebuttal on נִשְׁמַת
Sefer Edition — Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati on Tefillah: Shabbos Shacharis, Part II
Delivered ג׳ אלול תשפ״ו · 16 August 2026 · Sefer Edition

L’zecher רבי יהודה הלוי, whose yahrzeit falls on ג׳ אלול, and the Ohr Sameach — רבי מאיר שמחה הכהן מדווינסק — whose yahrzeit is marked the same night. זכותם יגן עלינו.

The Pesukei d’Zimra of Shabbos morning receive a set of additions that the weekday order lacks. Behind each addition stands a source, and behind several of them stands a human story: a Toledo synagogue completed in the year 1180 whose inaugural Shabbos gave כלל ישראל the recitation of שָׂמַחְתִּי בְּאֹמְרִים לִי; the first sunrise אדם הראשון ever saw after the chet, which gave the world מִזְמוֹר שִׁיר לְיוֹם הַשַּׁבָּת; and a Rishonic thundering against a legend concerning the authorship of נִשְׁמַת כָּל חַי. This chapter treats each in turn, and the smaller additions that stand alongside them.

I. The Framework: בָּרוּךְ שֶׁאָמַר and יִשְׁתַּבַּח as a Berachah Masgeres

The order of Pesukei d’Zimra is constructed as a bracket. בָּרוּךְ שֶׁאָמַר opens; יִשְׁתַּבַּח closes. The two form a berachah masgeres (בְּרָכָה מַסְגֶּרֶת) — a framing blessing — enclosing everything between.[1] The opening declaration is a promise of praise: the tzibbur announces its intention to spend the following minutes praising the Master of the world with the words of שירי דוד עבדך. The closing blessing seals that praise.

This structural logic yields a Rishonic ruling of immediate liturgical consequence: whatever is added to Pesukei d’Zimra — and Shabbos morning brings substantial additions — belongs inside the bracket, between בָּרוּךְ שֶׁאָמַר and יִשְׁתַּבַּח. The additions are not appendages tacked onto the perimeter of the tefillah; they are the very praise which the opening blessing announced. Nusach Ashkenaz preserves this framework in its most direct form: בָּרוּךְ שֶׁאָמַר is said early, immediately following the introductory pesukim, and every added kapittel of Shabbos morning falls within the bracket.

II. Why Nusach Sefard Places the Additions Before בָּרוּךְ שֶׁאָמַר

Nusach Sefard, and the Chassidishe siddurim that follow its ordering, do something structurally different. The extended kapitlach of Shabbos morning — הוֹדוּ לַה׳ קִרְאוּ בִשְׁמוֹ and the sequence that follows — are recited before בָּרוּךְ שֶׁאָמַר, not within the bracket. On the surface this appears to contradict the very framework principle just laid out. In truth it embodies a different halakhic priority.

The Mesadrei HaSiddur, from the age of the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah through the Rishonim, were consistently sensitive to those who cannot easily be present for the full order of tefillah — the latecomer, the unlearned, the one who depends upon the shaliach tzibbur to be yotzei via shomeia k’oneh.[2] This is not an accommodation invented in later generations; it is a Rishonic sensibility woven throughout the architecture of the siddur.

בָּרוּךְ שֶׁאָמַר is precisely the sort of blessing at which this sensitivity operates. In earlier generations many mispallelim did not know how to daven independently and relied on the shaliach tzibbur’s recitation of the opening berachah, being yotzei through shomeia k’oneh. Were בָּרוּךְ שֶׁאָמַר said too early on Shabbos morning — before the tzibbur has fully gathered — those relying on the shaliach tzibbur would lose the berachah entirely with no path to recover it.

Hence the Nusach Sefard inversion. The extended kapitlach are recited first, providing perhaps fifteen minutes of runway during which the tzibbur assembles; only then is בָּרוּךְ שֶׁאָמַר said, catching everyone who has by then arrived. The framework yields to the practical need. Nusach Ashkenaz, by contrast, holds the framework strictly and accepts as its price that some will miss בָּרוּךְ שֶׁאָמַר. Neither rite is arbitrary; the divergence encodes a genuine dispute about which halakhic value carries the greater weight in the ordering of Pesukei d’Zimra.

III. Three Reasons for the Additions

The Seventh Wing of the Serafim

Tosafos on Sanhedrin[3] brings the tradition of the Zohar concerning the wings of the serafim. The pasuk in Yeshaya describes שֵׁשׁ כְּנָפַיִם שֵׁשׁ כְּנָפַיִם לְאֶחָד — six wings for each seraf. Each wing corresponds to one day of the week and serves as the vehicle for that day’s shirah. On Shabbos, no wing remains; the malachim have exhausted their wings across the week’s six days. On this day כלל ישראל becomes the seventh wing. The added kapitlach of Shabbos morning are, on this account, the shirah of the malachim now sung by Klal Yisrael in their stead.

The Turcha D’Tzibbura Relaxes

The Tur in the hilchos of Pesukei d’Zimra[4] offers a more practical reason. During the week the tzibbur is pressed for time; work and obligation weigh, and Chazal were reluctant to extend the length of the tefillah. On Shabbos the constraint releases. The added kapitlach reflect the opportunity of the day and the honor due it. Both reasons — the structural (the seventh wing) and the pragmatic (the released constraint) — may operate simultaneously, and both are valid.

Rabbeinu Shlomo mi’Garmisa: The Shabbos Set Was Once the Daily Norm

A third and startling account emerges from the siddur of Rabbeinu Shlomo of Worms (mi’Garmisa — רבנו שלמה מגרמייזא), an Ashkenazic Rishon of the late eleventh and early twelfth centuries, a contemporary of Rashi and a major mekubal.[5] In his own siddur he preserves a striking historical claim: the original takanah of Pesukei d’Zimra called for the full expanded corpus — the entire set that Shabbos morning now retains — to be recited every day. The gradual curtailment of the weekday version was a later concession to practical necessity: the working oylam could not sustain the length; some were leaving before אַשְׁרֵי וּבָא לְצִיּוֹן; attendance itself was at stake. The full expansion was preserved intact only on the one day that permitted it, and the weekday Pesukei d’Zimra was pared down to what remains today.

He adds a wistful conclusion: וְהָאוֹמְרָם כָּל יוֹם תָּבֹא עָלָיו בְּרָכָה — one who says the extended set daily will be blessed. Whether this ideal has ever been widely adopted in practice is another question; the historical claim itself carries a significant consequence for how the added kapitlach of Shabbos morning should be understood. They are not liturgical padding. They are the recovered original template of daily Pesukei d’Zimra, preserved intact on the day that permits their full deployment.

IV. שָׂמַחְתִּי בְּאֹמְרִים לִי and the Toledo Synagogue of 1180

A Chapter Without a Rishonic Genealogy

Among the added kapitlach of Shabbos morning stands שִׁיר הַמַּעֲלוֹת שָׂמַחְתִּי בְּאֹמְרִים לִי בֵּית ה׳ נֵלֵךְ — Tehillim 122[6] — a fixture of every Nusach Sefard siddur on Shabbos morning. Yet a striking feature of its liturgical history distinguishes it from every other addition: no Rishon of the standard corpus — not the Avudraham, not the Machzor Vitri, not Rabbeinu Shlomo mi’Garmisa — includes this particular perek in his list of Shabbos-morning additions. Each of the other added kapitlach can be traced to a Rishonic source. This one cannot — save for a single text.

The Sefer HaManhig Preserves the Origin

The one Rishonic source that records the origin of the שָׂמַחְתִּי minhag is the Sefer HaManhig of רבי אברהם בן נתן הירחי (early thirteenth century).[7] What he preserves is remarkable in specificity: a place, a date, a family, an occasion, and a single evening in which a universal minhag was born.

In the year 1180, in the city of Toledo in Castile, a Jew named רבנו יוסף בן שושן completed the construction of a grand synagogue.[8] The ibn Shushan family were among the nesi’im of the Toledo Jewish community — recognized by the Castilian crown as the community’s official leadership, possessed of extraordinary wealth. Rabbeinu Yosef himself was a gaon, a tzaddik, and a mekubal, and served in the court of King Alfonso VIII of Castile in a position of financial administration — a role analogous to the one Don Yitzchak Abarbanel would later occupy under Ferdinand and Isabella.

Driven by his devotion, Rabbeinu Yosef built not merely a shul but a substantial complex — a mikdash me’at of astonishing scale for the age. On the first Shabbos morning that the tzibbur gathered to daven in the newly inaugurated shul, Rabbeinu Yosef stood before them and, overcome by the joy of what he beheld, declared that in honor of the new Beis Hashem the tzibbur would add one specific perek to the Shabbos-morning Pesukei d’Zimra: שָׂמַחְתִּי בְּאֹמְרִים לִי בֵּית ה׳ נֵלֵךְ. The name of the shul, the joy of the moment, and the yearning of the pasuk formed a triple echo. The minhag was adopted by neighboring Toledan communities; through the influence of the widely read Sefer HaManhig it spread outward. And every Shabbos morning across the world today, millions of Yidden recite this perek because of that single Shabbos in Toledo in 1180.

The Gemara’s Backstory: Why David Rejoiced

The pasuk itself carries a Gemara backstory that deepens what the ibn Shushan chose. The Gemara in Makkos[9] teaches that דוד המלך heard murmurings behind his back: מָתַי יָמוּת זָקֵן זֶה וְיִבָּנֶה בֵּית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ — “When will this old man die, so that the Beis HaMikdash may finally be built?” The nation knew that David had been informed — יָדֶיךָ דָּמִים מָלֵאוּ — that he himself would not build the Mikdash, that Shlomo would do so after his passing.

The natural response to such murmuring would be insult and rage. David’s response was שָׂמַחְתִּי — he rejoiced. The rudeness of the words revealed the kisufin that lay beneath them: a collective yearning of כלל ישראל to be oleh l’regel, to serve in the Beis Hashem. Yes, the words themselves were a shtickel chutzpah; but beneath the harshness was national longing, and David heard the longing rather than the harshness.

When Rabbeinu Yosef ibn Shushan chose this precise pasuk for the inauguration of his shul, the choice reached toward that same layered joy. A mikdash me’at is not the fulfillment of the yearning but its extension: a Yid builds a Beis Hashem not because he has arrived but because he longs for the one that is not yet built. The pasuk carries the yearning forward, from David to Toledo to every Shabbos morning since.

V. The Toledo Synagogue’s Later History

The building itself still stands. Its later history closes an arc that begins in 1180 and ends only in the twenty-first century.[10]

In the summer of 1391 — a full century before the Alhambra Decree of 1492 — a wave of anti-Jewish violence and forced conversions swept Christian Spain. The pogroms were initiated in Seville by the preacher Ferrand Martínez and spread to Toledo and beyond; historians estimate tens of thousands of Sephardic Jews were forcibly converted or killed in the summer’s events. The Dominican friar Vincent Ferrer — whose itinerant preaching campaign peaked in the years 1411–1416 and whom the Catholic Church later canonized — carried the conversionary project directly into the synagogues of Spain in the decades that followed. Learned in Torah She’bichsav and portions of Torah She’be’al Peh, he would enter a shul with a cross in one hand and a Sefer Torah in the other and declare the building a church.

The ibn Shushan synagogue of Toledo was among the buildings he came to. Within a few years the Catholic Church took possession of it and renamed it Sinagoga de Santa María la Blanca — the name jarring on the tongue, להבדיל אלף אלפי הבדלות. The building served as a church for over six centuries. Only in 2013 did the Archbishop of Toledo formally return it to the local Jewish community; today it functions as a Jewish museum.

It is among the most ancient standing synagogue buildings in Europe. The claim of being the very oldest is contested — the Old-New Synagogue of Prague, dating to approximately 1270, is a serious competitor for the same title — but the Toledo building’s antiquity, standing form, and continuous physical existence across nearly nine centuries are matters of historical record. The architecture is mudéjar: designed by Muslim architects working for Jewish patrons in Christian Spain. Small windows, distinctive arches, a Magen David preserved in the mizrach wall, a central arch where the Aron HaKodesh once stood.

VI. רבי אברהם הירחי: The Wandering Rishon Who Preserved the Story

A word on the source itself. רבי אברהם בן נתן הירחי — the author of the Sefer HaManhig — is an unusual figure among the Rishonim.[11] The great majority of Rishonim were born, learned, taught, and died within a single region — Ashkenaz, or Sefarad, or Italy, or Provence. Their halakhic vantage was local; their rulings reflected the practice of their own community, drawn from what they had seen and heard within its borders.

רבי אברהם was different. He was born in the town of Lunel (לוֹנֵל) in Provence; his very appellation — HaYarchi (הירחי, “of the moon”) — is a bilingual play on the town’s name (French lune, Hebrew ירח). Having received his early formation in Provence, he then traveled by deliberate choice, systematically, from kehillah to kehillah — through France, Germany, Provence, and finally Spain — documenting the variations of local minhag as he went. He was, in modern language, a working ethnographer of Jewish practice, and his Sefer HaManhig stands among the most important early-Rishonic witnesses to the geographic diversity of Jewish tefillah and observance.

He ended his life in Toledo, where he died in approximately 1215. It is precisely his willingness to travel — his readiness to leave Provence for Castile and to record what he saw there — that preserves for us the story of the ibn Shushan synagogue and the origin of שָׂמַחְתִּי as a Shabbos-morning addition. Had he remained in Lunel the story would have died with the Toledo community; every Yid today would say שָׂמַחְתִּי Shabbos morning with no knowledge of why.

VII. הַלֵּל הַגָּדוֹל — Why the “Great” Hallel

The Shabbos-morning additions include Tehillim 136: הוֹדוּ לַה׳ כִּי טוֹב כִּי לְעוֹלָם חַסְדּוֹ — known throughout the tradition as Hallel HaGadol, the “Great Hallel.” The Gemara in Pesachim[12] preserves Rabbi Yochanan’s account of the epithet: it is called great because הקב״ה sits b’rum olamo — in the heights of His world — and personally distributes sustenance to every creature. The proof-text is the pasuk toward the close: נֹתֵן לֶחֶם לְכָל בָּשָׂר, כִּי לְעוֹלָם חַסְדּוֹ.

The gadlus of the Ribbono Shel Olam that this perek praises is not the abstract majesty of the distant sovereign. It is the specific, granular care that ensures every ant, every fish, every bird, every human being receives its sustenance. In infinite majesty the Creator attends to the smallest beriyah. That is what makes the perek — whose refrain celebrates precisely this quality — gadol.

The Gemara in Berachos[13] preserves a hava amina that measures the stature of the perek still more directly. It was passingly considered whether Hallel HaGadol should be recited daily in the place of Ashrei. Both share the alphabetic-acrostic structure that Chazal valued; both praise the Ribbono Shel Olam broadly. In the end the Gemara preserved Ashrei as the daily standard, but the very fact that Hallel HaGadol was considered for that role confirms its weight. It is reserved for Shabbos morning and for the Seder night at Pesach — both moments when the themes of bri’as ha’olam and universal chesed are foregrounded.

VIII. הָאַדֶּרֶת וְהָאֱמוּנָה — The Shirah of the Malachim

Certain kehillos — particularly the Chassidishe world — include in Shabbos Pesukei d’Zimra the alphabetical acrostic הָאַדֶּרֶת וְהָאֱמוּנָה לְחַי עוֹלָמִים, a text of divine attributes drawn from the ancient mystical literature of Pirkei Heichalos — the corpus that ascribes shirah to the malachei ha’shares.[14]

The Maharil (Rabbeinu Yaakov Molin, c. 1360–1427) ruled that this tefillah is so exalted in status that it ought not be recited b’tzibbur except on Yom Kippur — the one day of the year when Klal Yisrael’s parallel to the malachim is halakhically formalized (Shabbos Shabbason, no eating and no drinking, white bigadim). To recite the shirah of the malachim on ordinary days, on the Maharil’s reading, risks a certain chutzpah — the appropriation of a register that does not belong to man.

The Chassidishe oylam nonetheless recites הָאַדֶּרֶת every Shabbos morning. The justification runs directly through the seventh-wing principle set out above (Section III). If, on Shabbos, Klal Yisrael is the seventh wing of the serafim, then on that day the shirah that belongs on ordinary days to the malachim alone belongs also to Klal Yisrael. The Arizal is the halakhic ground for this practice, teaching that הָאַדֶּרֶת belongs specifically to Shabbos precisely because of the seventh-wing status; this is the counter to the Maharil’s concern. Some chassidim nonetheless preserve the Maharil’s scruple by reciting הָאַדֶּרֶת before the formal beginning of the tzibbur’s tefillah, so that it is technically outside the tzibbur’s formal davening.

A further consideration: the sanctity of Shabbos may in a certain sense exceed that of Yom Kippur itself. Yom Kippur is called Shabbos Shabbason — a Shabbos of Shabbosos — but the base kedushah, the source, is Shabbos. On that reading the very argument that reserves הָאַדֶּרֶת for Yom Kippur may quietly justify its recitation on Shabbos as well.

IX. מִזְמוֹר שִׁיר לְיוֹם הַשַּׁבָּת — The First Sunrise of Adam HaRishon

The Song of Shabbos

The most weighty of all the Shabbos-morning additions is מִזְמוֹר שִׁיר לְיוֹם הַשַּׁבָּת — Tehillim 92[15]. In the era of the Beis HaMikdash, this was the Shir Shel Yom which the Leviim would sing when the Tamid shel Shachar was offered on Shabbos. Every day of the week had its own Shir Shel Yom; on Shabbos this was theirs.

Its ultimate authorship — the identity of the composer whose words the Leviim received — is answered by Avos d’Rabbi Nassan[16]: אָדָם הָרִאשׁוֹן. And the story of the composition is among the most moving passages that the Torah She’be’al Peh preserves concerning the origin of any tefillah.

The First Nightfall

אדם הראשון was created on Friday. On Friday afternoon he committed the chet of the Etz HaDa’as. As Shabbos began the sun started to set — for the first time in his experience. This was the first day of a human being’s life on the earth, and therefore, in the most literal sense, the first night.

As the darkness fell, אדם was seized by terror. His interpretation of the setting sun was catastrophic. He believed his sin had brought upon the world an eternal darkness — that the world was ending, in the dark, on his account, in punishment for what he had done. He passed the night in that terror, waiting to see whether the morning would come.

The Ritva on Avodah Zarah[17] raises the obvious difficulty. אדם הראשון was, per the Gemara’s descriptions of him, a being of towering intellect and profound spiritual insight; the Gemara in Bava Basra teaches that even the polish of his heel outshone the sun’s wheel. Did he not understand that there is a natural cycle of ויהי ערב ויהי בוקר, of evening and morning? Even without prior experience, surely his intellect grasped the rhythm of creation.

The Ritva’s answer is that of course he understood; but החטא מבלבלו — the chet disorients. Post-chet, his clear mind clouded. He could not believe what he intellectually knew. The chiddush is theological as much as psychological: chet damages a person’s ability to believe what he knows. The same mechanism is described in Mesillas Yesharim in the language of aveiros mitatmim es ha’lev: the heart is dulled; the intellect’s conclusions no longer reach it.

The Sunrise

Shabbos morning, the sun rose. אדם saw the light and understood: the world continues. Even though I sinned. Even though I broke the first commandment ever given. A person can fail — and there is still a morning.

Overcome, he composed מִזְמוֹר שִׁיר לְיוֹם הַשַּׁבָּת, טוֹב לְהוֹדוֹת לַה׳ — a song of thanks for the day of Shabbos, for the day whose sunrise disclosed that the world was not over. And then: לְהַגִּיד בַּבֹּקֶר חַסְדֶּךָ וֶאֱמוּנָתְךָ בַּלֵּילוֹת — to declare His kindness in the morning and His faithfulness in the nights. The night was terrifying; the morning declared chesed.

Avos d’Rabbi Nassan adds a further detail. At the very moment אדם began to sing, בְּאוֹתָהּ שָׁעָה יָרְדוּ כִּתּוֹת שֶׁל מַלְאֲכֵי הַשָּׁרֵת — kittos of malachim descended from Shamayim with musical instruments in hand: עֲלֵי עָשׂוֹר וַעֲלֵי נָבֶל, עֲלֵי הִגָּיוֹן בְּכִנּוֹר. The instruments the perek itself names accompanied its first singing; heavenly music attended the first human sunrise.

Every Shabbos morning, when the tzibbur reaches מִזְמוֹר שִׁיר לְיוֹם הַשַּׁבָּת, the emunah that stands behind the words is that the sun always rises again — that a Yid who has failed can begin again in the morning. The chet did not end the world for אדם and it does not end the world now.

מִזְמוֹר שִׁיר לְיוֹם הַשַּׁבָּת. טוֹב לְהוֹדוֹת לַה׳, וּלְזַמֵּר לְשִׁמְךָ עֶלְיוֹן. לְהַגִּיד בַּבֹּקֶר חַסְדֶּךָ, וֶאֱמוּנָתְךָ בַּלֵּילוֹת.
A psalm, a song, for the day of Shabbos. It is good to give thanks to Hashem, and to sing to Your name, Most High — to declare Your kindness in the morning, and Your faithfulness in the nights.
Tehillim 92:1–3

The Shevisas Keilim Question and the Korbanos Parallel

A serious difficulty arises within the Avos d’Rabbi Nassan narrative and deserves being named rather than glossed. The malachim descend playing instruments — but Shabbos morning has arrived. The halakhic prohibition of shevisas keilim (the requirement that one’s utensils rest on Shabbos) applies to musical instruments among other keilim. Are the malachim, in this account, mechalel Shabbos? A parallel wrinkle: Avos d’Rabbi Nassan also records that אדם brought korbanos that morning — yet a korban yachid is not brought on Shabbos.

The Rishonim treating this sugya approach it in more than one direction. Some resolve the difficulty by dating the korbanos to Sunday rather than Shabbos itself; on this reading a portion of the aggadic material of the passage reflects the events of the following day. Others invoke the principle that malachim are not bnei chiyuv — not obligated in the halakhic system that binds Klal Yisrael — and hence their music on the first Shabbos morning does not stand under the prohibition of shevisas keilim as it applies to a Yid. The machlokes of the Rishonim on this point cannot be fully resolved here; it is enough that both directions be preserved, and that the difficulty be recognized as a machlokes rather than dismissed as a stray question.

X. ה׳ מָלָךְ גֵּאוּת לָבֵשׁ — הקב״ה’s Bigdei Shabbos

Immediately following מִזְמוֹר שִׁיר לְיוֹם הַשַּׁבָּת comes Tehillim 93[18]: ה׳ מָלָךְ, גֵּאוּת לָבֵשׁ — לָבֵשׁ ה׳ עֹז הִתְאַזָּר — Hashem has reigned; He has clothed Himself in majesty; Hashem has girded Himself with strength. The choice of this perek, specifically for Shabbos, follows from the verb לָבֵשׁ — “He clothed Himself.”

Just as a Yid puts on bigdei Shabbos — as the Friday-night piyut לק-ל אֲשֶׁר שָׁבַת[19] declares of הקב״ה: תִּפְאֶרֶת עָטָה לְיוֹם הַמְּנוּחָה — so, kaviyachol, the Ribbono Shel Olam Himself “wraps Himself” in the kedushah of Shabbos as one wraps himself in a garment of honor. The change is not one-sided. Something changes in the higher olamos on Shabbos, and the material act of the Yid changing into finer clothes is an emulation of a divine act.

The pasuk that commemorates this — ה׳ מָלָךְ גֵּאוּת לָבֵשׁ — is added on Shabbos morning to bring the parallel to speech. The kedushah of Shabbos reaches through all the olamos. What the Yid does with his body in preparation for the day corresponds to something that is happening, kaviyachol, in Shamayim.

XI. נִשְׁמַת כָּל חַי — The Yesod v’Shoresh of Shabbos Shacharis

The Peak of Shabbos-Morning Tefillah

נִשְׁמַת כָּל חַי is the peak of Shabbos-morning davening — the yesod v’shoresh ha’avodah of Shabbos Shacharis, according to the great mekubalim. It is a segulah of significant weight in its own right; some women recite it every day. Yet a legend attached to its authorship, of a kind that has circulated in some corners of Jewish popular literature for many centuries, requires being addressed directly — both because the legend itself is false and because the Rishonic rebuttal is a matter of the historical record.

The Legend of Shimon Kefa

The legend concerns a Jew known in the story as Shimon Kefa (Kefa — the Aramaic kefa, “rock,” rendered in Greek as Petros).[20] Its historical setting: the era of the Tanna’im, when the movement that later became Christianity had first emerged. In that early period the followers of Yoshke (ימח שמו וזכרו) were not yet a separate community — they were Yidden, neighbors and shul-goers, and the boundary between the two religions had not yet been drawn.

According to the legend, the Tanna’im officially appointed Shimon Kefa — a frum Yid — as an undercover agent charged with a specific mission: to infiltrate the emerging Christian movement, rise within its ranks, and establish laws that would create a decisive and permanent separation between Christianity and Yiddishkeit. He is credited within the legend with the institution of Sunday as the Christian day of rest — a single stroke that made the boundary of Shabbos between the two religions permanent and unmistakable. The legend then extends: that this same Shimon Kefa returned to Klal Yisrael, composed נִשְׁמַת, and had it inserted into the siddur of Israel.

If the legend were true — if נִשְׁמַת were the composition of a Yid who had (even for tactical purposes) crossed the boundary into Christianity — the presence of the tefillah in the siddur would carry a shadow. This is the question that the Rishonim in Northern France in the age of Rashi felt compelled to address, and did address, with striking force.

The Machzor Vitri’s Rebuttal

The Machzor Vitri[21] — authored by ר׳ שמחה בן שמואל מויטרי, a talmid of Rashi — rebuts the legend with unusual sharpness. The formulation is a paraphrase; the substance is that anyone who claims that נִשְׁמַת was composed by this Yid who became a Christian — even if only in the manner of an undercover mission — will be chayav, when the Beis HaMikdash is rebuilt, to bring a korban chatas for having uttered such a falsehood about a holy tefillah.

The severity of the language is itself the measure of how seriously the Machzor Vitri took the danger. He rules the legend a falsehood and its repetition a sin. His counter-attribution is the standard authorship of the fixed elements of the siddur: נִשְׁמַת was composed by the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah, and he brings multiple rayos from the internal structure and vocabulary of the tefillah in support.

What Remains of the Question

The persistence of the rebuttal implies the persistence of the rumor. The Machzor Vitri’s formulation is not an academic exercise; the fact that a talmid of Rashi in northern France felt the need to invoke a korban chatas to warn against the legend means that the legend was in fact circulating among Jews of his era, and that the potential damage of its uncritical repetition was real. Nothing in the historical or scholarly record supports the legend on its merits: the historical Simon Peter (c. 1–c. 65 CE) is a central figure in Christian tradition as founder of the Roman Church, and the “undercover Jewish agent” narrative appears only in Jewish popular literature and has no basis outside it.

The point is not to soften the Machzor Vitri’s severity. It is to hear him clearly: when a Yid reaches נִשְׁמַת כָּל חַי on Shabbos morning, he is saying words the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah composed. That is the fact of the matter per one of the closest talmidim of Rashi, and the legend to the contrary is one that the Rishonim themselves felt obligated to condemn in the strongest halakhic terms available.

XII. Closing

The added kapitlach of Shabbos morning are, in the account of Rabbeinu Shlomo mi’Garmisa, the recovered form of the original daily Pesukei d’Zimra — the ideal that the weekday tzibbur could not sustain, preserved intact on the one day that permits it. Each has its source and, in the case of the additions here traced, its human story: an inaugural Shabbos in Toledo in 1180 that seeded שָׂמַחְתִּי; the first sunrise of אדם הראשון that gave the world מִזְמוֹר שִׁיר לְיוֹם הַשַּׁבָּת; the Machzor Vitri’s thunder that preserved the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah authorship of נִשְׁמַת against a false legend that had crept into circulation.

The Berachah Masgeres encloses all of this. The extended kapitlach are not liturgical padding but the very praise the opening blessing promised. Bigdei Shabbos — the ones the Yid puts on and the ones הקב״ה wears kaviyachol — are worn precisely because Shabbos is the day on which Klal Yisrael becomes the seventh wing of the serafim, and the day whose sunrise, from the first one אדם ever saw, has always meant that the world does not end at nightfall.

The Toledo synagogue’s later history — the 1391 pogroms, Vincent Ferrer, and the 2013 return. — In 1391, a full century before the Alhambra Decree of 1492, a wave of anti-Jewish violence swept Christian Spain. The pogroms were initiated in Seville by the preacher Ferrand Martínez and spread to Toledo and beyond; tens of thousands of Sephardic Jews were forcibly converted or killed in the summer’s events. The Dominican friar Vincent Ferrer (c. 1350–1419, later canonized by the Catholic Church), whose itinerant preaching campaign peaked in the years 1411–1416, carried the conversionary project directly into the synagogues of Spain in the decades that followed. Learned in Torah She’bichsav and portions of Torah She’be’al Peh, he entered synagogues with a cross and a Sefer Torah and declared each shul a church. The ibn Shushan synagogue of Toledo was among the buildings so appropriated. Renamed Sinagoga de Santa María la Blanca, it served as a church for over six centuries. In 2013 the Archbishop of Toledo formally returned it to the local Jewish community; today it functions as a Jewish museum. It is among the most ancient standing synagogue buildings in Europe; the claim of being the very oldest is contested (the Old-New Synagogue of Prague, c. 1270, is a serious competitor for the same title). ↩

The Friday-night piyut לק-ל אֲשֶׁר שָׁבַת. — Text: לק-ל אֲשֶׁר שָׁבַת מִכָּל הַמַּעֲשִׂים, בַּיּוֹם הַשְּׁבִיעִי נִתְעַלָּה וְיָשַׁב עַל כִּסֵּא כְבוֹדוֹ; תִּפְאֶרֶת עָטָה לְיוֹם הַמְּנוּחָה — עֹנֶג קָרָא לְיוֹם הַשַּׁבָּת. The critical phrase — תִּפְאֶרֶת עָטָה לְיוֹם הַמְּנוּחָה — is the direct textual anchor for the chiddush that הקב״ה kaviyachol wraps Himself in tiferes for Shabbos, exactly as the Yid wraps himself in finer bigadim. The piyut appears in the extended Emes v’Emunah / Hashkiveinu section on Friday night in many nusachos. ↩
  20. The Shimon Kefa legend — נִשְׁמַת attributed to an undercover infiltrator. — A legend circulating in some Jewish popular sources and current already in Ashkenazic circles in the Rishonic era in Northern France (see fn 21). The legend holds that Shimon Kefa (“Kefa” = “rock” in Aramaic; “Petros” in Greek) was a frum Yid appointed by the Tanna’im to infiltrate the early Christian movement. His mission: to establish laws creating a definitive separation between Christianity and Yiddishkeit — among them the institution of Sunday as the Christian day of rest. The legend then claims he composed נִשְׁמַת and it was smuggled into the siddur of Israel. The historical Simon Peter (c. 1–c. 65 CE) is a central figure in Christian tradition as the founder of the Roman Church; the “undercover Jewish agent” narrative appears only in Jewish popular literature and has no basis in the historical or scholarly record outside it. The legend is decisively rebutted by the Machzor Vitri (fn 21). Whatever pastoral or narrative function the legend served in Jewish popular memory, it is not history. ↩
  21. Machzor Vitri — the rebuttal of the Shimon Kefa legend on נִשְׁמַת. — The Machzor Vitri is an important eleventh-to-twelfth-century Rishonic compilation authored by ר׳ שמחה בן שמואל מויטרי, a talmid of Rashi. (The occasional description of him as Rashi’s chasan is not the standard attribution and should not be relied upon.) The rebuttal is a paraphrase rather than a verbatim quotation: anyone who claims נִשְׁמַת was written by the Yid who became a Christian — even if only in the manner of an undercover mission — will be chayav, when the Beis HaMikdash is rebuilt, to bring a korban chatas for uttering such a falsehood about a holy tefillah. The specific formulation of “korban chatas shemeinah” is unusual and, as reported, deserves comparison against the actual Machzor Vitri text before final printing; a version of the rebuttal is also transmitted in the name of Rabbeinu Tam’s Sefer HaYashar, and the two Rishonic sources are sometimes conflated. The counter-attribution in either case is the same: נִשְׁמַת was composed by the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah, and multiple rayos from the internal structure and vocabulary of the tefillah are marshaled in support. Standard reference: Machzor Vitri, hilchos Shabbos / seder tefillas Shabbos; specific siman pinpointing would strengthen the citation. ↩
