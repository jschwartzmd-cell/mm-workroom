קְרִיאַת שְׁמַע עַל הַמִּטָּה — הַמַּפִּיל, הֲרֵינִי מוֹחֵל, וְסֵדֶר הַמַּהַרַ״ם

The Blessing of HaMapil, the Nightly Forgiveness, and the Seder of the Maharam of Rothenburg from His Cell in Ensisheim
Sefer Edition · Rabbi Ephrati · Sunday-Morning Series on Tefillah
Delivered 19 July 2026 · ג׳ אב תשפ״ו · Sefer Edition

In honor of the bar mitzvah (בר מצוה) of יוֹסֵף מְנַחֵם מֶעְנְדֶעל שׁוֹיבֶעל, grandson of Mayor and Mrs. Weissman. May he be a light to his family and a source of nachas (נחת) to all of Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל).

The gemara (גמרא) at the close of Berachos (ברכות) outlines a nightly seder (סדר) for one who is about to sleep: first Kriyas Shema (קריאת שמע), then the berachah (ברכה) of HaMapil (המפיל). Between the terse Talmudic instruction and the printed page of a contemporary siddur (סידור) stand seven centuries of halachic (הלכתי) analysis, mystical elaboration, and the lived mesorah (מסורה) of Ashkenazic Jewry — including the seder preserved by the talmidim (תלמידים) of the Maharam of Rothenburg from his cell in Ensisheim. This perek (פרק) traces the sugya (סוגיא) as it unfolds in the Chayei Adam (חיי אדם), the Mishnah Berurah (משנה ברורה), and Rav Asher Weiss; from the Zohar HaKadosh (זוהר הקדוש)’s teaching on nightly forgiveness through the Pele Yoetz (פלא יועץ)’s sharp counter; and finally through the components of the nightly seder itself — HaMalach HaGoel (המלאך הגואל), Yosheiv B’Seiser (יושב בסתר), Hashkiveinu (השכיבנו), Birkas Kohanim (ברכת כהנים), and Mimini Michoel (מימיני מיכאל).

פרק א — The Mashal of the Two Illiterates

A certain man decided to live a life of aveiros (עבירות) without accountability, and believed he had found the perfect legal loophole: he would never learn to read. If he could not read the Shulchan Aruch (שולחן ערוך), he could not be held responsible for what it says. He avoided the printed word his entire life — no sefarim (ספרים), no siddur, no seforim. After one hundred and twenty years he arrived in Shamayim (שמים). They presented him with the list of his aveiros. He raised his defense: I cannot read. The Heavenly court, respecting the plea, sent him to Gan Eden (גן עדן).

Yet what is Gan Eden? Chazal (חז״ל) teach that the neshamos (נשמות) of the righteous occupy themselves there with the study of Torah (תורה). The man found himself surrounded by sefarim, by shtenders, by yeshivos (ישיבות) and rabbanim (רבנים). What had appeared as reward became, hour by hour, unbearable. He begged the malach (מלאך) in charge for permission to visit his friends in Gehinnom (גיהנום). The malach warned him that departure from Gehinnom is not a simple thing, but issued him a written shtar (שטר) of safe passage. He went. Within hours the heat and anguish overwhelmed him, and he hurried back to the gate of Gan Eden. He presented his shtar to the shomer (שומר) at the entrance. The shomer looked at it, looked back at him, and said: “I am sorry. I cannot read.”

A Yid’s (יהודי) neshamah (נשמה) is bound to Torah. Avoidance is not freedom — it is self-betrayal. The soul’s natural activity in olam ha’emes (עולם האמת) is limud (לימוד). The entire seder of Kriyas Shema al HaMittah (קריאת שמע על המיטה), and in particular the request v’sim chelkeinu b’Torasecha (ושים חלקנו בתורתך), presupposes that once released from the body’s daily distractions the neshamah resumes its true occupation. To omit the nightly seder is to release the neshamah into the yeshiva shel maalah (ישיבה של מעלה) without preparation.

פרק ב — The Gemara and Rabbeinu Chananel’s Girsa

The primary source of Kriyas Shema al HaMittah (קריאת שמע על המיטה) is the gemara (גמרא) at the close of Berachos (ברכות):[1] הַנִּכְנָס לִישֹׁן עַל מִטָּתוֹ… — one who enters his bedroom to sleep first recites Kriyas Shema and then the berachah (ברכה) of HaMapil (המפיל). Rabbeinu Chananel (רבינו חננאל)[2] is the witness to a girsa (גירסא) in which the recitation includes the third parshah (פרשה), Vayomer (ויאמר) — the parshah of tzitzis (ציצית).

The choice presents a structural anomaly. Night is not the zman (זמן) of the mitzvah (מצוה) of tzitzis; on the mitzvah plane alone, Vayomer would not belong at bedtime. Yet the purpose of Kriyas Shema al HaMittah is not to fulfill tzitzis but to secure shemirah min ha’mazikim (שמירה מן המזיקים). The full Kriyas Shema contains ramach (רמ״ח) — two hundred and forty-eight — words, corresponding to the ramach eivarim (רמ״ח איברים) of the human body; reciting the whole parshah supplies protection over every eiver (אבר). The addition of Vayomer completes the count, and full-body shemirah (שמירה) is the reason for it.

פרק ג — The Three Functions of HaMapil

The berachah (ברכה) of HaMapil (המפיל) discharges three distinct functions at once, and each has been developed by the poskim (פוסקים) as a separate matter.

Hoda’ah for the Gift of Sleep

The most direct function is hoda’ah (הודאה). HaKadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא) has fashioned a world in which the body is granted a nightly mechanism to still itself, restore the nefesh (נפש), and rise refreshed for the following day’s avodah (עבודה). This is a gift on the plane of minhago shel olam (מנהגו של עולם) — the very order of creation — and the berachah acknowledges it.

Tikvah for Waking

The second function is subtler and easily overlooked. The berachah voices the tikvah (תקוה) of waking. A Yid (יהודי) is not to take for granted that tomorrow will come. The moment of surrendering consciousness at night is precisely the moment when the fragility of life is most exposed, and the tikvah embedded in HaMapil is a small act of emunah (אמונה): I do not stand entitled to the morning; I hope for it, from Him.

Bakashah for Protection from Bad Dreams

The third function is bakashah (בקשה) for protection from chalomos ra’im (חלומות רעים) and hirhurim ra’im (הרהורים רעים). Bad dreams remain a live halachic (הלכתי) category. The gemara (גמרא) elsewhere treats them as spiritual events requiring hisorerus (התעוררות), and the formal procedure of hatavas chalom (הטבת חלום) — three friends assembled to answer chalma tava chazai (חלמא טבא חזאי) — is printed in every standard siddur (סידור). That the halachah (הלכה) of chalomos ra’im is not archaic can be observed in living batei midrash (בתי מדרש), where bnei aliyah (בני עליה) in their eighth and ninth decade still assemble three chaverim (חברים) after shacharis (שחרית) for the hatavah (הטבה). The nightly bakashah in HaMapil is the pre-emptive form of what the daily hatavas chalom does after the fact.

פרק ד — The Question of Hefsek

The berachah (ברכה) concludes with shem u’malchus (שם ומלכות): בָּרוּךְ אַתָּה ה’, הַמֵּאִיר לְעוֹלָם כֻּלּוֹ בִּכְבוֹדוֹ. Every element of a full berachah instituted by Chazal (חז״ל) is present. Yet the widespread contemporary minhag (מנהג) is to recite HaMapil (המפיל) without shem u’malchus, or in some communities to omit it altogether. This is not a lapse of piety but the product of a serious halachic (הלכתי) concern.

The concern is hefsek (הפסק). The poskim (פוסקים) raise the standard principle: one does not pronounce a berachah when the act on which the berachah depends may not immediately follow. One does not recite HaMotzi (המוציא) unless one is certain of eating bread within moments. How, then, can a person recite HaMapil — a berachah centered on sleep — without assurance of falling asleep at once? What if he must speak with his wife, rise for the bathroom, or wait patiently for an hour before shinah (שינה) descends? If sleep does not immediately follow, the berachah risks becoming berachah levatalah (ברכה לבטלה). This is the pressure that drives the great machloikes (מחלוקת) of the Acharonim (אחרונים).

פרק ה — The Chayei Adam: HaMapil as Birkas HaShevach

The Chayei Adam (חיי אדם)[3] opens the sugya (סוגיא) at an unexpected point. He begins not with nighttime HaMapil (המפיל) at all, but with a daytime sheinas keva (שינת קבע). Should one recite HaMapil before an afternoon nap? The Chayei Adam rules that one should not: during the day the possibility of not falling asleep is too real, and the risk of berachah levatalah (ברכה לבטלה) is prohibitive.

This ruling generates the sharp follow-up. If daytime naps are subject to the levatalah (לבטלה) concern, why is nighttime any different? A person may lie down at night and fail to sleep for an hour, may need to speak with his household, may require the bathroom. Why should the halachah (הלכה) for nighttime differ from the halachah for daytime?

The answer of the Chayei Adam is a striking piece of lomdus (למדות). At night, HaMapil is not a personal berachah (ברכה) on this individual’s sleep. It is a birkas ha’shevach (ברכת השבח) on the universal institution of sleep — on minhago shel olam (מנהגו של עולם), on the fact that HaKadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא) has ordered creation such that humanity as a whole retires at night and rises renewed for avodas Hashem (עבודת השם). The daytime nap, by contrast, is not part of the universal institution but is this person’s personal repose; daytime HaMapil would therefore be a birkas ha’nehenin (ברכת הנהנין) on personal benefit, and the levatalah concern applies. Nighttime HaMapil, framed as birkas ha’shevach on the cosmic order, is not undone by any single individual’s momentary wakefulness. On this shittah (שיטה), the hefsek (הפסק) pressure dissolves entirely.

R’ Avraham Danzig, the Chayei Adam (1748–1820, Vilna), was a gaon olam (גאון עולם) and a mechutan (מחותן) of the Vilna Gaon — and, decisively, a wealthy merchant rather than a formal rav (רב) or rosh yeshiva (ראש ישיבה). He earned his living traveling on business, and his own introduction records that he kept his Shulchan Aruch (שולחן ערוך) with him on the road, gathering his notes into what would become the most widely consulted practical halachah-sefer for ba’alei battim (בעלי בתים) in the modern era. His shittah on HaMapil carries the weight not only of his lomdus but of a life spent with the practical bedside experience of a working Yid (יהודי).

פרק ו — The Mishnah Berurah’s Counter

The Mishnah Berurah (משנה ברורה)[4] — in his commentary on Orach Chaim (אורח חיים) siman ramach (רמ״ח) and especially in the Biur Halachah (ביאור הלכה) on samuch l’mitaso (סמוך למיטתו) — presses hard against the Chayei Adam (חיי אדם). The Chofetz Chaim (חפץ חיים)’s argument goes to the text itself: the leshon (לשון) of the berachah (ברכה) is decisively personal. The words are הַמַּפִּיל חֶבְלֵי שֵׁינָה עַל עֵינַי וּתְנוּמָה עַל עַפְעַפָּי… וִיהִי רָצוֹן… שֶׁתַּצִּילֵנִי. Every clause is leshon yachid (לשון יחיד): my eyes, my eyelids, my saving. A berachah composed entirely in first-person singular cannot be reframed as a communal shevach (שבח) on the universal order of things. The syntax will not bear the shift. HaMapil (המפיל) is therefore a birkas ha’nehenin (ברכת הנהנין) for the personal benefit of sleep, and the hefsek (הפסק) concern applies with full force. If interruption is anticipated — and for most people it is — one should recite HaMapil without shem u’malchus (שם ומלכות).

The Chayei Adam’s shittah (שיטה), in fairness, remains coherent even under this pressure. It can be defended along the line that the takanah (תקנה) of the berachah was structured around night sleep precisely because that is what constitutes minhago shel olam (מנהגו של עולם), and the leshon yachid is stylistic — the standard register of birkos ha’shevach (ברכות השבח) in general — rather than probative of ha’nehenin classification. But the Biur Halachah’s textual point is the sharper one, and it has carried the day in the practical psak (פסק) of most contemporary kehillos (קהילות).

פרק ז — Rav Asher Weiss: Is Sleep a Hana’ah?

Rav Asher Weiss[5] presses the sugya (סוגיא) one level deeper. On what basis, he asks, is sleep classified as a hana’ah (הנאה) in the halachic (הלכתי) sense at all?

Tosafos in Berachos (תוספות בברכות)[6] take up the question of why one recites a berachah (ברכה) on rei’ach (ריח) — on fragrance. The answer of Tosafos is categorical: something physically enters the guf (גוף). The molecules of the fragrance enter through the nose, preserving the parallel to food and drink, which also enter the guf. On this reading, birkos ha’nehenin (ברכות הנהנין) are on that which enters the guf. Rav Asher Weiss applies the Tosafos categorically to sleep. Sleep does not enter the guf. It is not consumed the way food and drink are consumed, nor even inhaled the way fragrance is inhaled. It is a biological state that happens to the guf. It cannot be a birkas ha’nehenin (ברכת הנהנין) at all — regardless of the leshon yachid (לשון יחיד) — and must be classified as birkas ha’shevach (ברכת השבח) or birkas hoda’ah (ברכת הודאה).

The point is reinforced by the Chida (חיד״א) and others, who argue explicitly that sleep is a withdrawal of the nefesh (נפש) rather than an ingestion of anything — the opposite of a hana’ah that enters the body. This strengthens the position further: if sleep involves the departure of a faculty rather than the arrival of a substance, the hana’ah framework fits the phenomenon poorly even before one examines the leshon (לשון) of the berachah. If Rav Asher Weiss is correct, the hefsek (הפסק) pressure on HaMapil (המפיל) diminishes substantially, and the Chayei Adam (חיי אדם)’s comfort with an unhurried recitation regains its footing on a new basis.

פרק ח — Sequence, Chatzos, and the Mekubalim

The Magen Avraham (מגן אברהם)[7] raises the corollary question of order: does Kriyas Shema (קריאת שמע) precede HaMapil (המפיל), or follow it? The answer flows directly from the classification above. On the Mishnah Berurah (משנה ברורה)’s ha’nehenin (הנהנין) framing, one must minimize any hefsek (הפסק) between the berachah (ברכה) and the sleep it activates: Shema (שמע) first, then HaMapil immediately before sleep, and then sleep, with no verbal or physical interruption. On the Chayei Adam (חיי אדם)’s shevach (שבח) framing, the hefsek rules are lenient, and the practice of some Rishonim (ראשונים) — HaMapil first, Shema afterward — is defensible. The practical output for most contemporary siddurim (סידורים) is that HaMapil comes at the end, positioned as close as possible to the moment of sleep itself, in line with the Mishnah Berurah’s structural priority.

A second stringency, of Kabbalistic provenance, is that HaMapil should not be recited after chatzos ha’lailah (חצות הלילה). This is the minhag (מנהג) of the mekubalim (מקובלים), followed in the Ben Ish Chai (בן איש חי), the Kaf HaChaim (כף החיים), and the broader Sefaradi-Kabbalistic mesorah (מסורה). The Kabbalistic reckoning of the second half of the night differs from the first in ways that affect the berachah. It should be noted that this particular chumra (חומרא) presupposes the ha’nehenin/hefsek framework; a pure birkas ha’shevach (ברכת השבח) would not obviously be time-bounded, and on Rav Asher Weiss’s frame the chatzos (חצות)-limit is a Kabbalistic overlay rather than a halachic (הלכתי) necessity.

פרק ט — הריני מוחל: The Zohar of Rabi Abba

The center of gravity of the nightly seder (סדר) is not the berachah (ברכה) of HaMapil (המפיל) but the declaration that precedes it in the standard printed page: הֲרֵינִי מוֹחֵל וְסוֹלֵחַ לְכָל מִי שֶׁחָטָא כְּנֶגְדִּי, בֵּין בְּגוּפִי בֵּין בְּמָמוֹנִי בֵּין בִּכְבוֹדִי… וְלֹא יֵעָנֵשׁ שׁוּם אָדָם בְּסִבָּתִי. The seder’s inner rationale is cheshbon ha’nefesh (חשבון הנפש). It is natural at the quiet moment before sleep to recall the day’s slights — the driver who cut one off in traffic, the colleague who spoke down to him, the family member whose words stung. That, however, is not the state in which the neshamah (נשמה) is to be released upward to HaKadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא). Arriving at the yeshiva shel maalah (ישיבה של מעלה) with unresolved anger invites midah k’neged midah (מדה כנגד מדה) — Heavenly prosecution for one’s own aveiros (עבירות). The nightly mechilah (מחילה) is, before it is a generous act toward others, a protective act for oneself.

The Zohar HaKadosh (זוהר הקדוש), in Parshas Miketz (פרשת מקץ),[8] is the source of the practice. Rabi Abba (רבי אבא), a central figure among the chaverim (חברים) of the Zohar (זוהר), once observed a man asleep by the roadside. A poisonous snake advanced toward him. Miraculously, a branch fell from a nearby tree and killed the snake before it reached him. Moments later, the stone mound against which the man was leaning collapsed — had he remained where he was for another minute, he would have been crushed. Two miracles, one after the other, delivered to a man in his sleep.

Rabi Abba approached and asked what zechus (זכות) had earned the man such a deliverance. The answer was simple: “Whenever a person wrongs me, I try to appease him and to forgive him. And if I cannot appease him at once, every night before I sleep I declare: רִבּוֹנוֹ שֶׁל עוֹלָם, הֲרֵינִי מוֹחֵל לְכָל מִי שֶׁפָּגַע בִּי בְּלֵב שָׁלֵם.” Rabi Abba wept, and declared that this man’s daily mechilah was, in a certain bechinah (בחינה), greater than the mechilah of Yosef HaTzadik (יוסף הצדיק) to his brothers in Bereishis (בראשית) perek (פרק) nun:[9] Yosef forgave a definite set of brothers for a documented wrong at a definite hour, while this Yid (יהודי) forgives an indefinite crowd of strangers every night without exception. Daily and universal scope exceeds even a dramatic single act in the sheer breadth of what it releases.

The Condition of Sincerity

The declaration only functions if it is sincere. The pasuk (פסוק) in Tehillim (תהילים):[10] דֹּבֵר שְׁקָרִים לֹא יִכּוֹן לְנֶגֶד עֵינָי. A person who speaks falsely cannot stand before the Divine gaze. To recite Hareini Mochel (הריני מוחל) while clinging inwardly to the day’s resentment is not a neutral act. It registers as a spoken falsehood at the moment of hispallelus (התפללות) — a fresh aveirah (עבירה) layered onto the resentment it was meant to dissolve. If the heart cannot yet release a specific grievance, the honest course is to withhold that grievance from the formula and to work on it, rather than to speak an insincere line before HaKadosh Baruch Hu.

פרק י — The Pele Yoetz’s Warning

The Pele Yoetz (פלא יועץ)[11] issues an equally sharp warning in the opposite direction. One must never wrong another Yid (יהודי) in reliance on the thought: He will forgive me tonight when he says Hareini Mochel. Wronging another creates two distinct obligations, and they are structurally independent:

  • The victim’s duty to forgive when appeasement is offered.
  • The offender’s own duty to actively seek mechilah (מחילה) and to appease the person he has hurt.

The victim’s nightly declaration does not discharge the offender’s duty. The two obligations do not offset. The paradigm is the same as the Rambam (רמב״ם) in Hilchos Teshuvah (הלכות תשובה): on erev Yom Kippur (ערב יום כיפור) the offender is required to seek forgiveness three times, and this remains true no matter how many times the victim has already forgiven him in his heart at bedtime. Reliance on another’s Hareini Mochel (הריני מוחל) as a discharge of one’s own duty is a serious misreading of the halachah (הלכה) of interpersonal teshuvah (תשובה).

פרק יא — ושים חלקנו: The Nightly Yeshiva Shel Maalah

Among the requests of the bedtime tefillos (תפילות) is וְשִׂים חֶלְקֵנוּ בְּתוֹרָתֶךָ — set our portion in Your Torah (תורה). The Noda B’Yehudah (נודע ביהודה), in his Tzlach (צל״ח) on Berachos (ברכות),[12] presses the incongruity. Why request a portion in Torah precisely at the moment when the learning day is ending? Whatever limud (לימוד) was going to occupy the person is now yielding to sleep.

The Arizal (אריז״ל)’s answer[13] transforms the meaning of the request. When a Yid (יהודי) sleeps, his neshamah (נשמה) ascends and continues the limud in the yeshiva shel maalah (ישיבה של מעלה). The guf (גוף) rests; the neshamah attends its chaburah (חבורה) above. On this framework the request v’sim chelkeinu b’Torasecha (ושים חלקנו בתורתך) is precisely timed: it is the appeal that one’s chelek (חלק) in the world’s Torah-learning continue through the night, in the yeshiva to which the neshamah is about to travel. Every Yid, night after night, is admitted to the chaburah where the tuition is nothing and the company is beyond parallel. The nightly seder (סדר) prepares him for the class.

פרק יב — The Maharam of Rothenburg and the Prison Seder

The seder (סדר) as it is now printed rests, in its historical spine, on a specific mesorah (מסורה) from the last years of a specific gadol (גדול). Rabbeinu Meir ben Baruch of Rothenburg (רבינו מאיר בן ברוך מרוטנבורג),[14] the Maharam, was among the greatest Ashkenazic Rishonim (ראשונים) of the thirteenth century — the rebbe (רבי) of the Rosh (רא״ש), of the Mordechai (מרדכי), and of the generation of poskim (פוסקים) that followed. In his later years he was seized by King Rudolf I and imprisoned in the tower of Ensisheim in Alsace, in a scheme to extract ransom from the Jewish community. The Maharam refused to permit the community to pay. To accede, he understood, would establish that a gadol could be seized and sold, and would endanger every future gadol Jewry would produce. He died in his cell after seven years of captivity. His body was ransomed and buried in Worms decades later by Alexander Wimpfen, in exchange for a place in the same beis ha’kevaros (בית הקברות) beside him.

During those seven years, talmidim (תלמידים) visited the tower and preserved the Maharam’s practices. Among what they preserved is his own seder for Kriyas Shema al HaMittah (קריאת שמע על המיטה). The seder is deliberately concise — reflecting both the constraints of the cell and the Maharam’s characteristic halachic (הלכתי) economy — and it consists of:

  1. The first parshah (פרשה) of Kriyas Shema (שְׁמַע יִשְׂרָאֵל… וְאָהַבְתָּ);
  2. הַמַּלְאָךְ הַגֹּאֵל אֹתִי מִכָּל רָע יְבָרֵךְ אֶת הַנְּעָרִים… from Yaakov’s blessing;[15]
  3. לִישׁוּעָתְךָ קִוִּיתִי ה’;[16]
  4. יֹשֵׁב בְּסֵתֶר עֶלְיוֹן — the whole of Tehillim perek tzadik-alef;
  5. Hashkiveinu (השכיבנו), without a chasimah (חתימה);
  6. Birkas Kohanim (ברכת כהנים).

There is an elegant historical convergence worth naming. The Arizal (אריז״ל) too held that, once Ma’ariv (מעריב) has been davened in its zman (זמן), only the first parshah of Shema (שמע) is required at bedtime. Ashkenazic Rishonic practice preserved in a Rhineland cell, and Sefaradi Kabbalistic pesak (פסק) issued in Tzfas (צפת) three centuries later, converge on precisely the same seder. What is now printed in a standard siddur (סידור) — HaMapil (המפיל), Mimini Michoel (מימיני מיכאל), Hareini Mochel (הריני מוחל), V’sim Chelkeinu (ושים חלקנו), additional pesukim (פסוקים) and tehillim (תהילים) — is an expansion around the same spine.

There is, further, a specific hashkafah (השקפה) in the Maharam’s choice of components. HaMalach HaGoel (המלאך הגואל) is the appeal to the malach (מלאך) who redeemed Yaakov Avinu (יעקב אבינו) from every ra (רע). Lishuascha kivisi Hashem (לישועתך קויתי ה') is the cry Yaakov injected in the middle of blessing Dan — the archetype of a tzaddik (צדיק) in tzarah (צרה) awaiting Divine deliverance. Yosheiv b’seiser Elyon (יושב בסתר עליון) is the perek (פרק) of shir shel pega’im (שיר של פגעים), of shelter under the shadow of the Almighty. For a man in a cell awaiting a ransom that will never come, these are not general nightly tefillos (תפילות); they are the specific vocabulary of a Jew in exile making his peace with the night. That the same vocabulary now travels into every Jewish bedroom is itself a form of Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל)’s answer to what was done to the Maharam.

פרק יג — Hashkiveinu Without a Chasimah

The Tur (טור)[17] records that the Rosh (רא״ש) recited Hashkiveinu (השכיבנו) at bedtime without the chasimas ha’berachah (חתימת הברכה) — without opening or closing the paragraph with Baruch Attah Hashem (ברוך אתה ה'). Many Acharonim (אחרונים) codify the same practice. A full berachah (ברכה)-formulation, on this psak (פסק), does not belong outside its fixed liturgical makom (מקום), which is in Ma’ariv (מעריב) immediately after Emes v’Emunah (אמת ואמונה). The shemirah (שמירה) quality of the Hashkiveinu language — shomer amo Yisrael la’ad (שומר עמו ישראל לעד) — is preserved when the paragraph is spoken as a tefillah (תפילה). The berachah-frame is omitted. The practical output: a Yid (יהודי) saying the nightly seder (סדר) from a printed siddur (סידור) should recognize that the printed page’s Hashkiveinu functions as shirah (שירה), not as a berachah, and should read it accordingly.

פרק יד — Birkas Kohanim and the Shishim Giborim

The presence of Birkas Kohanim (ברכת כהנים) in the nightly seder (סדר) requires an accounting. The Midrash Rabbah (מדרש רבה) in Naso (נשא),[18] in the extended derashah (דרשה) of R’ Shimlai (ר' שמלאי) on Birkas Kohanim, reads the pasuk (פסוק) in Shir HaShirim (שיר השירים):[19] הִנֵּה מִטָּתוֹ שֶׁלִּשְׁלֹמֹה, שִׁשִּׁים גִּבֹּרִים סָבִיב לָהּ מִגִּבֹּרֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל — the sixty giborim (גיבורים) stationed around the bed of Shlomo HaMelech (שלמה המלך) — as the sixty letters of Birkas Kohanim. Elu shishim osiyos she’b’Birkas Kohanim (אלו ששים אותיות שבברכת כהנים). The count is precise: יְבָרֶכְךָ ה’ וְיִשְׁמְרֶךָ. יָאֵר ה’ פָּנָיו אֵלֶיךָ וִיחֻנֶּךָּ. יִשָּׂא ה’ פָּנָיו אֵלֶיךָ וְיָשֵׂם לְךָ שָׁלוֹם. Sixty letters; sixty giborim.

The pasuk’s continuation makes the nighttime application explicit: אִישׁ חַרְבּוֹ עַל יְרֵכוֹ מִפַּחַד בַּלֵּילוֹת — every warrior with his sword upon his thigh, from fear of the nights. On this reading, the recitation of Birkas Kohanim at bedtime is not decorative. It stations sixty giborim, one for each letter, around the sleeper’s bed. Every letter is a warrior; the peace and protection of the berachah (ברכה) literally encircles the one who says it.

פרק טו — מימיני מיכאל and the Argaman

The concluding tefillah (תפילה) of the printed nightly seder (סדר) invokes the four archangels by name: מִימִינִי מִיכָאֵל, וּמִשְּׂמֹאלִי גַּבְרִיאֵל, וּמִלְּפָנַי אוּרִיאֵל, וּמֵאֲחוֹרַי רְפָאֵל, וְעַל רֹאשִׁי שְׁכִינַת אֵ־ל. The Yid (יהודי) retires under the Shechinah (שכינה), ringed by four malachim (מלאכים), each with a defined station.

The traditional remez (רמז) that codifies the practice is the word argaman (ארגמן) — the purple-scarlet dye of the bigdei kehunah (בגדי כהונה) and of the parochet (פרוכת) of the Mishkan (משכן). Read as rashei teivos (ראשי תיבות), its four letters are the four malachim: א for Uriel (אוריאל), ר for Refael (רפאל), ג for Gavriel (גבריאל), מ for Michael (מיכאל). The concluding ן stands for Nuriel (נוריאל), or in a widespread alternative reading is taken as an intensifier rather than a fifth name. A distinct peirush (פירוש) reads the leading א as Aluf HaOlam (אלוף העולם) — HaKadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא) Himself — with the remaining letters as the four malachim. Both readings appear in early Sefer Chasidim (ספר חסידים) and Zohar Chadash (זוהר חדש) sources; the ordering of which malach (מלאך) stands on which side varies as well across the mekoros (מקורות). What both readings share is the picture: the sleeping Yid, ringed on all four sides and canopied above — the entire encampment a portable Mishkan for the hours of the night.

פרק טז — Practical Guidance

The sugya (סוגיא) has a practical output that is not scholarly but nightly. A Yid (יהודי) should open a siddur (סידור) at bedtime rather than rely on memory; the act of opening the printed page draws a line between the day’s noise and the nightly hispallelus (התפללות). He should recite at minimum Shema Yisrael (שמע ישראל), and where circumstances permit the full three parshiyos (פרשיות) for the ramach (רמ״ח)-word shemirah (שמירה). He should include the components of the Maharam’s spine — HaMalach HaGoel (המלאך הגואל), Lishuascha kivisi (לישועתך קויתי), Yosheiv b’seiser (יושב בסתר), Hashkiveinu (השכיבנו) (without a chasimah (חתימה)), Birkas Kohanim (ברכת כהנים), and Mimini Michoel (מימיני מיכאל).

Regarding HaMapil (המפיל) itself, he should endeavor to recite the instituted berachah (ברכה). When interruption is likely — conversation with a spouse, a bathroom trip, difficulty falling asleep — he recites HaMapil without shem u’malchus (שם ומלכות). This honors the takanas Chazal (תקנת חז״ל) while sidestepping the berachah levatalah (ברכה לבטלה) concern, and it is the widely accepted contemporary default for one who is uncertain of the coming sleep.

Above all, he attends to Hareini Mochel (הריני מוחל), and he says it with intent. If he has not been saying it, he adds it. And — the counterpart insisted upon by the Pele Yoetz (פלא יועץ) — he does not lean upon another Yid’s nightly recitation to absolve his own duty of appeasement when he has wronged him during the day. The two duties travel on separate tracks. Both must be walked.

מַרְאֵה מְקוֹמוֹת  ·  Mareh Mekomos

Each note runs short citation first, then an expanded elaboration. Notes flagged with a caveat preserve an open question that has not been resolved to a firm mekor.

  1. Berachos ס׳ ע״ב — Ha’nichnas lishon al mitaso. — The gemara at the close of Berachos outlines the structural seder for one preparing to sleep: Kriyas Shema at minimum through the end of V’hayah im shamo’a, followed by HaMapil. The full text of the berachah concludes: בָּרוּךְ אַתָּה ה’ הַמֵּאִיר לְעוֹלָם כֻּלּוֹ בִּכְבוֹדוֹ. ↩
  2. Rabbeinu Chananel on Berachos — girsa including Vayomer. — Rabbeinu Chananel ben Chushiel (tenth–eleventh century, Kairouan) is the earliest surviving witness to a girsa of the gemara that includes the third parshah of Kriyas Shema in the bedtime recitation. Because nighttime is not the zman of the mitzvah of tzitzis, the justification is structural rather than mitzvah-based: the full Kriyas Shema supplies the ramach-word count corresponding to the ramach eivarim of the body, and the third parshah is needed for the count. Whether the girsa is best described as R’ Chananel’s own emendation or as a text he received requires collation of the surviving manuscripts of his commentary against the standard Ashkenazic girsa’os; not investigated in the present perek. ↩
  3. Chayei Adam, laws of Kriyas Shema she’al HaMittah. — R’ Avraham Danzig (1748–1820, Vilna), a mechutan of the Vilna Gaon, wrote both the Chayei Adam on daily halachah and the Chachmas Adam on Yoreh De’ah. The treatment of daytime sheinas keva versus nighttime HaMapil draws the structural distinction that grounds his entire shittah: daytime HaMapil is a personal birkas ha’nehenin subject to levatalah risk; nighttime HaMapil is a birkas ha’shevach on minhago shel olam. ↩
  4. Mishnah Berurah, Orach Chaim siman רל״ט; Biur Halachah, d’h Samuch l’mitaso. — The Chofetz Chaim’s decisive pushback on the Chayei Adam. The core argument turns on the leshon of the berachah: every clause is leshon yachid, so HaMapil must be classified as birkas ha’nehenin and the hefsek concern applies with full force. Practical psak: when interruption is anticipated, recite without shem u’malchus. This is the widely accepted default in contemporary kehillos. ↩
  5. Rav Asher Weiss — classification of sleep as neither hana’ah proper nor within the hefsek-lens. — Rav Asher Weiss (contemporary Ra’avad of the Beth Din of Yerushalayim and Rosh Kollel of Machon Minchas Osher), one of the leading contemporary Ashkenazic poskim, argues that sleep does not enter the guf and therefore fails the Tosafos criterion (see fn 6) for a birkas ha’nehenin. On this framing HaMapil must be classified as birkas ha’shevach or birkas hoda’ah, and the hefsek pressure that shaped the Mishnah Berurah’s caution diminishes substantially. The specific published kuntreis in which Rav Asher Weiss develops this argument in the exact form presented here has not been fixed to a single siman. His treatment of HaMapil appears in his general shiurim on Berachos and in the Minchas Asher collection. As of consultation, the specific mekor is not indexed in Sefaria’s corpus; verification against a printed Minchas Asher is recommended before citation in a formal halachic context. ↩
  6. Tosafos Berachos מ״ג ע״ב, d’h d’amar kra. — Tosafos’ discussion of why one recites a berachah on rei’ach: because the molecules of the fragrance enter the guf through the nose. Tosafos establishes the principle that birkos ha’nehenin are on things that physically enter the guf. The Chida and others extend the point: sleep is a withdrawal of the nefesh, not the arrival of a substance, and therefore fails the criterion on its own terms. ↩
  7. Magen Avraham — sequence of HaMapil and Kriyas Shema. — R’ Avraham Gumbiner (seventeenth-century Poland), in his commentary on the relevant simanim of Orach Chaim, addresses whether HaMapil precedes or follows Kriyas Shema. The output follows the classification of HaMapil: on the Mishnah Berurah’s ha’nehenin frame, Shema first and then HaMapil immediately before sleep; on the Chayei Adam’s shevach frame, the order is more flexible. The exact siman in Magen Avraham where this analysis is developed was not fixed in the underlying shiur; verify before citation. ↩
  8. Zohar, Parshas Miketz — the ma’aseh of Rabi Abba. — The Zohar’s ma’aseh of Rabi Abba encountering a man asleep by the roadside who receives two miracles: a branch that falls and kills a snake advancing toward him, and the collapse of a stone mound moments after he moves. The man’s zechus is his practice of nightly mechilah b’lev shaleim. The Zohar presents this zechus as surpassing, in a certain bechinah, even Yosef HaTzadik’s forgiveness of his brothers. The exact folio in the Zohar on Parshas Miketz was not fixed in the underlying shiur, and printed variants of the ma’aseh differ in some details (e.g. which tanna is the observer in certain recensions). Pinning the citation to a specific page in a standard printed edition of the Zohar is recommended before publication. ↩
  9. Bereishis nun:יט–כא — Yosef’s mechilah. — Yosef’s reassurance to his brothers after Yaakov’s petirah: אַל תִּירָאוּ… וְאַתֶּם חֲשַׁבְתֶּם עָלַי רָעָה אֱלֹקִים חֲשָׁבָהּ לְטֹבָה… וַיְנַחֵם אוֹתָם וַיְדַבֵּר עַל לִבָּם. The paradigmatic act of mechilah in Tanach, against which the Zohar’s anonymous Yid is measured. ↩
  10. Tehillim ק״א:ז׳ — דובר שקרים לא יכון לנגד עיני. — The pasuk that grounds the sincerity requirement of Hareini Mochel. A recitation of the formula while inwardly clinging to the day’s resentment is not merely halachically ineffective; it is a spoken falsehood at the moment of standing before HaKadosh Baruch Hu. ↩
  11. Pele Yoetz, erech Mechilah. — R’ Eliezer Papo (early nineteenth-century Bulgaria), Pele Yoetz, in the entry on mechilah: the offender’s duty to seek forgiveness is independent of the victim’s Hareini Mochel. The paradigm parallels the Rambam, Hilchos Teshuvah ב:ט (the three-time bakashas mechilah on erev Yom Kippur). ↩
  12. Noda B’Yehudah — Tzlach on Berachos ה׳ ע״א. — R’ Yechezkel Landau (1713–1793, Prague) opens the question of why one requests v’sim chelkeinu b’Torasecha at the moment when the day’s learning is ending. The question opens the path for the Arizal’s answer (fn 13) and stands beside other Acharonim’s treatments (the Chida, the Ben Ish Chai) which offer their own resolutions. ↩
  13. The Arizal — yeshiva shel maalah as the chelek requested. — The Arizal’s teaching that when a Yid sleeps, his neshamah ascends to the yeshiva shel maalah and continues learning. The teaching is developed across multiple Lurianic texts and preserved in the standard published editions of the Ari’s writings (Etz Chaim, Sha’ar HaKavvanos). Exact sha’ar and perek not fixed to a single mekor in the underlying shiur; multiple Lurianic passages support the teaching, and any single citation should be verified before formal use. ↩
  14. The Maharam of Rothenburg’s prison-seder — preserved from Ensisheim. — R’ Meir ben Baruch of Rothenburg (c. 1215–1293), rebbe of the Rosh, the Mordechai, and the Tashbetz Katan. Seized by King Rudolf I and imprisoned in the tower of Ensisheim in Alsace, in a scheme to extract ransom from the Jewish community. The Maharam refused to permit the community to pay. He died in captivity after seven years. His body was ransomed and buried in Worms decades later by the Wimpfen family, in exchange for a plot beside him in the same beis ha’kevaros. During those years talmidim visited and preserved his practices, including his seder for Kriyas Shema al HaMittah: first parshah of Shema, HaMalach HaGoel, Lishuascha kivisi, Yosheiv b’seiser, Hashkiveinu without chasimah, Birkas Kohanim. The seder is preserved in the Tashbetz Katan and cited in later poskim. Whether the talmidim’ recording reflects the Maharam’s lechatchilah practice or a b’sha’as ha’dechak pattern from prison is not fully settled; the Tashbetz Katan presents it as mesorah, and later poskim have received it as such. ↩
  15. Bereishis מ״ח:ט״ז — המלאך הגואל אותי מכל רע. — Yaakov’s berachah to Menashe and Ephraim: הַמַּלְאָךְ הַגֹּאֵל אֹתִי מִכָּל רָע יְבָרֵךְ אֶת הַנְּעָרִים…. Sits early in the nightly seder as an appeal for the same protective malach’s presence during the coming night. ↩
  16. Bereishis מ״ט:י״ח — לישועתך קויתי ה’. — Yaakov’s cry, injected in the middle of the berachah to Dan. The traditional understanding: Yaakov saw the future of Dan, including Shimshon’s eventual downfall, and cried out for the ultimate yeshuah. In the nightly seder, the pasuk frames sleep itself as a moment of waiting for Divine deliverance. ↩
  17. Tur, Orach Chaim siman רל״ה — Rosh’s practice of Hashkiveinu without chasimah. — The Tur (R’ Yaakov ben ha’Rosh) records his father’s practice of reciting Hashkiveinu at bedtime without opening or closing the paragraph as a formal berachah. Many Acharonim codify the same. The shemirah function of the Hashkiveinu language — the shomer amo Yisrael la’ad refrain — is preserved when the paragraph is spoken as tefillah; the berachah-frame is omitted because a full berachah does not belong outside its fixed makom in Ma’ariv. ↩
  18. Bamidbar Rabbah י״א:ג׳ — derashah of R’ Shimlai on the sixty letters of Birkas Kohanim. — The Midrash Rabbah in Naso, in R’ Shimlai’s extended derashah on Birkas Kohanim: Elu shishim osiyos she’b’Birkas Kohanim. The midrash continues that even one who dreams a sword is cutting his thigh should rise early to hear Birkas Kohanim and no harm will come to him — grounding the nightly-shemirah application. The count of sixty depends on the standard orthographic convention preserved in the midrash itself; alternative counting conventions yield slightly different totals. The specific attribution to R’ Shimlai for this derashah in Bamidbar Rabbah should be verified against the printed midrash; R’ Shimlai’s more famous derashos in this section address taryag mitzvos, and the individual pisketa associations in Naso vary across manuscripts. ↩
  19. Shir HaShirim ג׳:ז׳ — שישים גיבורים סביב לה מגיבורי ישראל. — הִנֵּה מִטָּתוֹ שֶׁלִּשְׁלֹמֹה, שִׁשִּׁים גִּבֹּרִים סָבִיב לָהּ מִגִּבֹּרֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל, כֻּלָּם אֲחֻזֵי חֶרֶב מְלֻמְּדֵי מִלְחָמָה, אִישׁ חַרְבּוֹ עַל יְרֵכוֹ מִפַּחַד בַּלֵּילוֹת. The pasuk’s concluding phrase — mipachad ba’leilos — makes the nighttime application explicit and grounds the reading of Birkas Kohanim as the sixty-giborim encampment around the sleeper. ↩
