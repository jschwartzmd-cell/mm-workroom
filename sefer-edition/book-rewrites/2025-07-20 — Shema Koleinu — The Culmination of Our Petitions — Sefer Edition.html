שְׁמַע קוֹלֵנוּ

Shema Koleinu — The Culmination of Our Petitions: The Quality of Hearing, the Limits of Addition, and the Plea Not to Be Sent Away Empty
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Sunday Morning Series on Tefillah
Recorded כ״ד תמוז תשפ״ה  ·  20 July 2025  ·  Sefer Edition

Sponsored לעילוי נשמת מורינו ורבינו. The discourse was first delivered on the yahrzeit (יארצייט) of Rabbi Yaakov Yosef,[1] first and last Chief Rabbi of New York.

A treatise on Shema Koleinu (שמע קולנו), the berachah (ברכה) that closes the twelve bakashos (בקשות) of Shemoneh Esrei (שמונה עשרה), and on the different kinds of shema (שמע) for which it asks: the warm hearing and the cold, the latter set out in the Gemara (גמרא) of Taanis (תענית) on Shmuel HaKatan (שמואל הקטן) and the rain that came too soon. It treats the question of personal additions to the berachah: the Taz (ט״ז), the sharp protest of the Aruch HaShulchan (ערוך השלחן) against the printers, and the counsel of the Yaavetz (יעב״ץ) to add in one’s own words. It closes with “reikam al teshiveinu” (ריקם אל תשיבנו) and Rav Schwab’s memory of his mother and a loaf of black bread in the Germany of the First World War.

I. The Place of Shema Koleinu

Shema Koleinu (שמע קולנו) is a berachah (ברכה) of critical importance: it is the culmination of the middle section of Shemoneh Esrei (שמונה עשרה). The first three berachos (ברכות) are shevach (שבח), praise; the middle twelve are bakashah (בקשה), petition; the last three are hoda’ah (הודאה), thanksgiving. The twelve themselves divide into the personal six and the communal six, and Shema Koleinu closes the entire arc.

It is often overlooked that the amen (אמן) that follows Shema Koleinu requires special kavanah (כוונה). Just as the amen after Kedushas Hashem (קדושת השם) seals the opening unit, so the amen after Shema Koleinu seals the twelve: it lifts up the amens (אמנים) and the bakashos (בקשות) of the entire central section.

Why It Follows Tzemach David

The Gemara (גמרא) in Megillah (מגילה)[2] derives the order of the berachos verse by verse. Once Yerushalayim (ירושלים) is rebuilt, David (דוד) comes: “Afterward the children of Israel shall return, and seek the Lord their God and David their king.” And once David has come, tefillah (תפילה) has come: “והביאותים אל הר קדשי ושמחתים בבית תפילתי”, the Beis HaMikdash (בית המקדש) as the house of prayer. The completeness of Yerushalayim under the kingship of the house of David opens directly into the berachah in which Hashem is asked to listen to our prayers.

II. The Original Moment

The Midrash (מדרש) teaches[3] that when Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל) cried out in the servitude of Mitzrayim (שעבוד מצרים) — “ויאנחו ויזעקו ותעל שועתם אל האלקים” — and HaKadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא) heard them, the malachei hashares (מלאכי השרת) proclaimed: “ברוך אתה ה׳ שומע תפילה”. Each day the worshipper asks that Hashem hear him as He heard Israel then.

III. “Av HaRachaman” or Not?

A question of nusach (נוסח) presents itself. In Nusach Sefard (נוסח ספרד) the berachah opens “אב הרחמן שמע קולנו”; most Nusach Ashkenaz (נוסח אשכנז) siddurim (סידורים) omit “אב הרחמן” and open with “שמע קולנו”.

The Tur (טור)[4] enters into uncharacteristic detail on gematria (גימטריא) and the counting of letters in Shemoneh Esrei. The opening letters of the berachos sum to 1,800, corresponding to the 1,800 malachim (מלאכים) appointed to receive the tefillos (תפילות) of Israel; the Tur cites the Midrash Dorshei Reshumos (מדרש דורשי רשומות) for this, and the Rav connects it with the Zohar HaKadosh (זוהר הקדוש). From the count the conclusion is drawn that the berachah must open with “שמע” and not with “אב הרחמן”: the shin (ש׳), worth 300, is needed, not the aleph (א׳), worth 1, to reach the sum.

The Arizal (אריז״ל)[5] nevertheless has “אב הרחמן שמע קולנו” in his nusach, for he was not makpid (מקפיד) on the roshei teivos (ראשי תיבות). Certain nuschaos (נוסחאות) from various chassidic (חסידות) circles compromise by placing “אב הרחמן” in the middle of the berachah, preserving the opening shin (ש׳).

IV. The Two Kinds of Hearing — A Gemara in Taanis

The whole of Maseches Taanis (מסכת תענית) turns on the fasts that were decreed when rain was needed. In it the Gemara (גמרא) (Taanis 25b)[6] tells two accounts of Shmuel HaKatan (שמואל הקטן) — the same tanna (תנא) who composed Birkas HaMinim (ברכת המינים) — that complicate the plain reading of “שמע”.

The First Year

There was a drought, and Shmuel HaKatan decreed a taanis tzibbur (תענית ציבור). On the very morning the fast was to begin — before the people had prayed, before the fast had started — the rain began to pour. The community rejoiced and came to him: “Even before we fasted, even before we prayed, Hashem has sent us so much rain. He loves us. It is to the praise of the congregation.”

Shmuel HaKatan replied: “No. משל למה הדבר דומה — to a servant who keeps coming to his master’s door, asking and asking. The master cannot bear the sight of him, and says to a friend: Give him whatever he wants; I do not wish him knocking at my door any longer.”

There are times when Hashem gives and gives — “only do not trouble Me.” It is a frightening thought.

The Second Year

Another drought; another fast. They prayed the entire day. Just after shkiah (שקיעת החמה), as the congregation was leaving the beis knesses (בית כנסת) to break the fast, the rain began to fall. They came to Shmuel HaKatan: “This year you cannot say anything negative. We prayed, He listened, and we have rain.”

He answered: “Not necessarily. משל למה הדבר דומה — to a servant who comes to his master begging. The master, displeased with him, says to a friend: Let him beg until he is exhausted; then I will give him what he wants.”

The mashal (משל) teaches that Hashem sometimes wishes the petitioner to plead until he is spent, and only then to grant. Both accounts — the rain before the fast and the rain at sundown — are forms of hearing, but neither is the shema (שמע) that is sought.

What Would Have Been the Praise of the Congregation

The Gemara itself raises the obvious question: if neither was the praise of the congregation, what is? It answers: when the shaliach tzibbur (שליח ציבור) says “משיב הרוח” and the wind blows, and says “מוריד הגשם” and the rain falls — that is, rain that answers the prayer as it is being said. The distinction thus lies not in the rain but in the manner in which it is given.

V. The Lesson — A Pleasant Tefillah

This is what is requested in Shema Koleinu: not the cold “שמע” of the master who cannot bear the servant, nor the wearying “שמע” of the master who waits for exhaustion, but the warm “שמע” — that the tefillah (תפילה) be pleasant in the ears of HaKadosh Baruch Hu, and not a burden.

When a person carries chataim (חטאים) and aveiros (עברות), his words have difficulty rising. The Baal Shem Tov (בעל שם טוב)[7] is said to have entered a beis medrash (בית מדרש) and declared: “This shul is full of Torah and prayer.” The chazanim (חזנים), the rav (רב) and the roshei hakahal (ראשי קהל) swelled with pride. The Baal Shem Tov clarified: “That is not what I meant. Prayers and Torah are meant to ascend. If they are weighted with aveiros and lack yiras shamayim (יראת שמים), they remain in the room. Perhaps one day someone will break through the ceiling and lift them all up. Your beis medrash is full of Torah and prayer because the Torah and prayer never left.”

The desired state is the opposite: a shul empty of tefillah because the tefillah has risen. “שמע קולנו” is the prayer for that very ascent.

VI. Personal Requests in Shema Koleinu

Shema Koleinu is the place in Shemoneh Esrei for the broadest range of personal bakashos (בקשות). The Gemara in Avodah Zarah (עבודה זרה) 8a[8] permits a person to add specific needs in the berachah whose theme they fit — refuah (רפואה) in Refaeinu (רפאנו), parnasah (פרנסה) in Birkas HaShanim (ברכת השנים) — a principle called me’ein haberachah (מעין הברכה). Shema Koleinu is general: here anything may be asked.

The Arizal on Viduy

The first addition that many siddurim (סידורים) place at Shema Koleinu is a viduy (וידוי). The Arizal (אריז״ל) taught that one should add a viduy over one’s sins; some say at every tefillah. His instruction is to specify the cheit (חטא) — not a generic confession but a particular one. The Arizal draws this from the Zohar HaKadosh (זוהר הקדוש).[9]

The Prayer for Parnasah

The next standard insertion is a prayer for parnasah (פרנסה). The Shulchan Aruch (שולחן ערוך)[10] permits it, and many acharonim (אחרונים) say that it may be added daily.

Two Terms: Be’arai and Bekviyus

The discussion that follows turns on a distinction. To add be’arai (בעראי) is to add occasionally, when the need arises — that day, that week, that month. To add bekviyus (בקביעות) is to make the addition a fixed part of the daily Shemoneh Esrei.

VII. The Taz and the Aruch HaShulchan

The Taz’s Caution

The Taz (ט״ז)[11] is presented as protesting: “You are going to add something bekviyus — to make it a permanent fixture of your daily Shemoneh Esrei? You are adding to what the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah (אנשי כנסת הגדולה) instituted? Who are you?” The Gemara in Avodah Zarah permits adding be’arai — when the need arises that day, that week, that month — and not bekviyus.

The Mishnah Berurah (משנה ברורה)[12] records the Taz’s position, and most acharonim permit daily recitation. One who recites it daily does not follow the Taz, but Hashem certainly listens.

The Aruch HaShulchan — Sharper Still

The Aruch HaShulchan (ערוך השלחן)[13] goes much further. The takanah (תקנה) of the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah is kodesh kodashim (קודש קודשים); every word and every letter is holy; the permission to add applies only be’arai, never bekviyus. To add daily is chutzpah (חוצפה).

The Aruch HaShulchan’s polemic runs: “All the printers — they are guilty. They sought to insert these additions into Shemoneh Esrei every day. It is their fault; without them no one would say it. We have just spoken of this in Pirkei Avos (פרקי אבות) — the printers.” He continues: “Who are you to insert these additions? It is all the fault of the printers. Many amei ha’aretz (עמי הארץ) do so regularly.”

And again: “I mourn over the corruption of our siddurim (סידורים). One of the gedolei hador (גדולי הדור) made a great protest about this. The world has been handed over to fools who decide what to put in the siddur; the printers do what they wish; Klal Yisrael is corrupted.”

These are strong words. The Rav observes that the Aruch HaShulchan is said to be the opposite of the Shulchan Aruch: in many places he rules differently from most poskim (פוסקים). He was a great mechadesh (מחדש), and he is relied upon in many places, though most of Klal Yisrael, especially those who follow the Mishnah Berurah, accepted the Mishnah Berurah more. Yet for clarity in any sugya (סוגיא), the Aruch HaShulchan is the volume to open: “fantastic” is the only word.

No one holds this way lema’aseh (למעשה). It is to be understood that the permission of the Gemara, on this view, is a license to add a request, not a license to install one into the fixed text. Whoever says the viduy daily should continue to do so, and should take no offense at the strength of the Aruch HaShulchan’s words, for many Jews do exactly this. The matter is related le’yedi’ah (לידיעה), for knowledge, not for practice.

VIII. The Yaavetz — In Your Own Words

The teaching of the Yaavetz (יעב״ץ), drawn from a derashah (דרשה) in Prague, must not be forgotten[14] — pages upon pages of it; people in those generations had patience.

The Gemara permits adding. Printed prayers for addition have been issued. Forget the printed prayers — add your own words.

This is a chiddush (חידוש). Shema Koleinu is general. If a person needs a shidduch (שידוך) for his child — three hundred and fifty years ago the Yaavetz already speaks of the shidduch crisis of his generation: “we suffer greatly; we have great difficulty finding shidduchim (שידוכים) for our children or grandchildren” — let him add the prayer in his own words, his own language, his own kavanah.

If he needs parnasah, let him add his own words. The source of many of the printed prayers is not even known: one appears in Maseches Soferim (מסכת סופרים); another only in the Geonic (גאונים) period; who composed them is not known. Let him use his own language. “ממעמקים קראתיך ה׳”: prayer from the depths of the heart is more powerful than what is printed. Yet the printed prayers are powerful too, for the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah wove every kavanah into them.

The two positions are closer than they first appear. Both resist the normalization of someone else’s request as one’s own; they differ only in what is to stand in its place — for the Aruch HaShulchan, nothing fixed; for the Yaavetz, one’s own words.

Stop and Speak From the Heart

At Shema Koleinu one stops and says: “רבונו של עולם — I need parnasah; I need this business deal to come through today.” The Brisker Rav (הגרי״ז)[15] is reported to have ruled that one with a major business meeting that day should do exactly this — one line. The Yaavetz teaches that Hashem listens because it comes from the heart. If a person needs a shidduch, a refuah (רפואה), anything, he should say it briefly, not at length, just before the close of the berachah. The Chida (חיד״א) says the same.

One Caution — Do Not Miss Kedushah

The Mishnah Berurah: if one’s Shemoneh Esrei will run so long that the shaliach tzibbur will already be in the chazaras hashatz (חזרת הש״ץ) and one will miss Kedushah (קדושה), he should not add at length here; let him wait until after Shemoneh Esrei. Kedushah is too important to lose, and the balance must always be kept.

IX. The Zohar on Constant Prayer — Rav Yeiva Sava

The Zohar HaKadosh (זוהר הקדוש)[16] records a tanna (תנא) named Rav Yeiva Sava (רב ייבא סבא). He never sat down to a meal — breakfast, lunch or dinner — without first pausing to pray: “Just as You have given me food for this meal, give me food for the next meal.” He was a Jew who lived with Hashem in every moment, taking nothing for granted.

The worshipper does not stop before every meal. But three times a day, in Shema Koleinu, he should add a prayer for parnasah (פרנסה), even if, baruch Hashem (ברוך השם), he is doing well — in his own words, as Rabbi Yonah Landsofer (רבי יונה לנדסופר) taught.

X. The Whole Point of Studying Tefillah

It is for this reason that every Sunday morning since Simchas Torah (שמחת תורה) after October 7 has been given to the study of tefillah (תפילה). Something was needed on Sunday morning; the community agreed that tefillah was what it needed to learn; and each berachah of Shemoneh Esrei has been taken in turn, in order to develop an appreciation of what is said every day.

Tefillah is a meeting with HaKadosh Baruch Hu. The essence of Shema Koleinu is exactly this: tefillah as chibbur (חיבור), connection. Naftali (נפתלי) is chibbur, says Rashi (רש״י):[17] we connect. Man is the only species capable of intellectual communication with his Creator. Birds chirp; Perek Shirah (פרק שירה) is full of songs; the trees rustle — “ירננו כל עצי היער”. Man speaks. He does not always hear an answer; sometimes there is silence. But he speaks, and is certain that Hashem listens.

XI. “Reikam Al Teshiveinu” (ריקם אל תשיבנו)

The Rav shares a personal kavanah on an expression in this berachah, and sets it with an observation from the day before. A member of the congregation had told him that when he says “טוב ה׳ לכל ורחמיו על כל מעשיו” he has more kavanah than in any other pasuk (פסוק) in all of tefillah. Everyone has his own pasuk; this one is the Rav’s own. There are no more beautiful words in tefillah than “כי א-ל שומע תפילות ותחנונים אתה, מלפניך מלכנו ריקם אל תשיבנו”.

The Simple Peshat (פשט)

“מלפניך מלכנו ריקם אל תשיבנו”: Ribbono shel Olam (רבונו של עולם), I have asked for so much — from da’as (דעת) to refuah, from parnasah to geulah (גאולה). Do not let me leave empty-handed. “Make a deal — give me a third, half, something.” Whoever can shed a tear at these words, his tefillah is being accepted.

A Deeper Reading

Several meforshim (מפרשים) read “ריקם אל תשיבנו” differently. Even if Hashem, for the petitioner’s own benefit, does not grant the request, at least the feeling of having spent four minutes connected to Him should remain. Do not send me away empty in that sense. A man takes three steps back, and within minutes he has lost the connection. The prayer is that he not feel empty: whether Hashem gives what was asked or not, let him leave still feeling connected.

XII. Rav Schwab’s Reading — A Loaf of Black Bread

The Rav relates from Rav Shimshon Schwab (רב שמשון שוואב) in Sefer Iyun Tefillah (עיון תפילה)[18] a reading of unusual depth. Rav Schwab writes that when he says “ריקם אל תשיבנו”, this is what he has in mind.

He was six years old in Germany during the First World War. Food was severely short. Weekly government rations arrived — Kommissar Brot, commissary bread. A family of nine received nine loaves a week. The bread was black, made with potato peels and whatever else could be ground into it: terrible bread, which on an ordinary day one would not touch, and in those days it was everything.

Each child had his own loaf, paced for the week — a piece on Sunday, a piece on Monday. One day his mother cut him a piece for his meal. He finished it; he was hungry; he asked for another. There was none to give; it had to last the week. She told him sharply: “Enough. Save it. We need it for tomorrow.” She raised her voice at the six-year-old, and he was hurt.

But when she turned her face away, he saw a single tear escape her eye, fall onto her cheek, and drip onto the loaf of bread she was holding. And the six-year-old understood, in that moment, that everything was for his benefit. She raised her voice at him because she so wished to give him more bread; she so wished to give him more that she could not give him.

The Pasuk in Tehillim 37:25

David HaMelech (דוד המלך) says:[19] “נער הייתי גם זקנתי, ולא ראיתי צדיק נעזב וזרעו מבקש לחם” — “I was young, I have aged, and I have never seen a tzaddik abandoned, his children begging for bread.” Surely there are tzaddikim (צדיקים) whose children beg for bread. How can David HaMelech say this?

The reading of Rav Schwab: I have never seen a tzaddik who felt abandoned — never seen a tzaddik whose children carried that abandonment within them. The tzaddik remains connected to HaKadosh Baruch Hu regardless of the bread on the table. This is a reading that moves the verse from a statement of what happens to a statement of what is felt; it is Rav Schwab’s reading, and the verse is commonly understood in a more literal sense.

Rav Schwab writes: “I am one hundred percent certain that my mother — the tzadekes (צדקת) — even at the moment the tear fell on the black bread, because she could not give her child another piece, did not feel abandoned by Hashem. She had to do what she had to do. This is the situation now. Hashem arranged it.”

This Is “Reikam Al Teshiveinu”

Even when the piece of bread does not arrive, Ribbono shel Olam, the worshipper wishes to remain connected; he wishes to feel that he has not been abandoned. That is the deeper request, and a very high madreigah (מדרגה) if it can be held daily in tefillah.

XIII. The Power of Tears

There is nothing wrong with crying. It is forgotten how to cry on an ordinary weekday.

The Rav tells of his father, of blessed memory (ע״ה). Rabbi Avraham Schorr,[20] the Flatbush rav, with whom the Rav’s father davened in his later years on the East Side, said publicly after the Rav’s father had passed away: “There was no other person in the beis knesses who cried during a regular weekday Shemoneh Esrei. Your father cried.”

As a child, the Rav was embarrassed. No one else’s father cried on a regular Tuesday. “You are a weakling if you cry.” But his father would shed a tear at Krias Shema (קריאת שמע); he truly meant it. Perhaps he was more emotional than others — that makes no difference. One is allowed to cry. When the words come out — “השיבנו בחיים”, “בא נשלום”[21] — one should have something to cry for. The tear can take a person to far places, and he can get what he needs.

What Rav Schwab describes in the tear of his mother was lived in the Rav’s own generation in the tears of his father: the worshipper who stays connected whatever the circumstance.

The theme of inheritance through tefillah found a second expression at the close of the shiur. When the Rav took the siddur of one of the participants in order to check whether these insertions appear at Minchah, he learned that it had been the participant’s father’s siddur, which the son had inherited and now davened from. The Rav stopped, visibly moved, and told him: “It is a very big zechus (זכות) to daven in the siddur of your ancestors, of your father. It is a very, very choshuveh (חשובה) thing.”

XIV. The Prayer for Rain in Shema Koleinu

One further insertion belongs in Shema Koleinu: a prayer for rain when there is a drought. It is not said in the ordinary course. Some make the mistake of saying it without a taanis (תענית), and the poskim (פוסקים) are divided. A couple of years before the shiur, the batei din (בתי דין) in Eretz Yisrael (ארץ ישראל) issued a letter that all should add Aneinu (עננו) during a drought; the Rav remarked that this is probably not relevant for those living in New York.

The Bach’s Warning

The Bach (ב״ח)[22] rules that to request rain in any berachah other than Birkas HaShanim (ברכת השנים) is forbidden b’tzibbur (בציבור). An individual (b’yachid, ביחיד) may insert it at Shema Koleinu; but for the congregation to add it together, even at Shema Koleinu, is a serious matter.

The Bach records a tradition that two gedolim (גדולים) instituted exactly this in their communities — when they needed rain and did not declare a formal taanis, they had the entire congregation say the prayer for rain at Shema Koleinu. Both gedolim died within the year. It is a grave thing to add to Shemoneh Esrei beyond what Chazal (חז״ל) instituted, particularly to insert sheilas geshamim (שאלת גשמים) outside its proper place.

Halachah lema’aseh (הלכה למעשה): when there is a communal need for rain, one consults a rav; one does not improvise. The letter of the batei din is the proper form — a communal addition under rabbinic direction.

Timing

These insertions are typically said at Shacharis (שחרית), not Minchah (מנחה) or Maariv (מעריב). Some siddurim include them at all three; some do not. The Aruch HaShulchan warns against saying them at Minchah and Maariv; the Shaarei Tziyon (שערי ציון) and the Mishnah Berurah are more permissive.[23]

XV. Closing

Shema Koleinu is the place in which to bring one’s week, one’s year, one’s life. It is not to be passed through mechanically, the words turning over without engagement. Let the worshipper add a viduy in the spirit of the Arizal — specifying the cheit. Let him add a prayer for parnasah in the spirit of the Zohar HaKadosh — like Rav Yeiva Sava (רב ייבא סבא) at every meal. Let him add for his shidduchim and his children in the spirit of the Yaavetz — in his own words. And, at the very close, let him mean “ריקם אל תשיבנו”: do not let me leave empty; and if You will not give me bread today, do not let me feel abandoned. Stay connected to me. We will keep coming.

The words were first spoken on the yahrzeit of a man who stood in a wilderness of Torah observance and kept going to the door of a city that would not easily listen; the final line, “we will keep coming,” is a fitting one on that day.

Rabbi Yaakov Yosef of New York (רבי יעקב יוסף הלוי זצ״ל, 1840–1902; a talmid of the Salant school; rav of Vilna and Slutsk before emigrating to America in 1888) — the first and only Chief Rabbi of New York, appointed by the Association of American Hebrew Orthodox Congregations with the support of fifteen Eastern European immigrant congregations. His tenure was marked by the difficulty of imposing centralized halachic authority on a fragmented immigrant community, and he faced significant opposition from competing factions; New York in his day was a midbar (מדבר) in terms of Torah observance, and his tzaros (צרות) were great. The Rav recalled the opposition as an open rebellion, in which, as he put it, stones were thrown at the people there. He suffered a stroke in 1895 and was largely incapacitated for the remaining years of his life; his petirah (פטירה) in 1902 (כ״ד תמוז) drew tens of thousands. He is buried in Cypress Hills, Queens (the weekly write-up gives Union Field Cemetery); on the yahrzeit great numbers visit the grave, even in the rain. The Satmar Rebbe’s tribute, that every good machshavah (מחשבה) a Jew has in New York is bizchuso (בזכותו) of Rabbi Yaakov Yosef, circulates in oral mesorah (מסורה).
    Caveat: the dates, offices, appointing body, burial site and the details of the opposition are given from the weekly write-up and the Shiur Review of the live telling (which also records the surviving shul on Ocean Parkway, the cheder “Mevakshei Hashem” on Ditmas, and his eventual move to the Lower East Side); none is independently verified here, and no published source for the Satmar Rebbe’s remark is specified.
