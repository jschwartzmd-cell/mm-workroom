תַּחֲנוּן — הַוִּדּוּי, נְפִילַת אַפַּיִם, וּמִנְהַג הַהַשְׁמָטָה

Tachanun — The Confession, the Fall on the Face, and the Custom of Omitting It
Studies in Tefillah (תפילה) — The Order of the Morning Prayer
11 Cheshvan 5786  ·  2 November 2025  ·  Sefer Edition
לעילוי נשמת ר׳ דוד בן משה שלום ע״ה — Danny Kriegsman, whose yahrzeit (יארצייט) fell on Shemini Atzeres (שמיני עצרת).[1]
Sponsored by Reb Meyer and Mrs. Weissman.

This discourse takes up tachanun (תחנון), the supplication that follows the weekday Shemoneh Esrei (שמונה עשרה), in three aspects: the confession that precedes it, the fall on the face that is its heart, and the custom, in some circles, of leaving it out. It begins from a question about the closing words of the Shemoneh Esrei, and goes on to ask where the vidui (וידוי), Ashamnu (אשמנו) and the Thirteen Attributes of Mercy (שלש עשרה מידות הרחמים), that now stands before the fall on the face came from, why it is said by those who have not committed the sins it lists, and how the Ve'atah Tzaddik (ואתה צדיק על כל הבא עלינו) of the same prayer is to be understood.

It then turns to the fall on the face itself: its sources in the Torah, the absence of an explicit passage in the Gemara (גמרא) that places it after the prayer, and the narrative of the oven of Achnai, Rabbi Eliezer (רבי אליעזר בן הורקנוס) and Imma Shalom (אימא שלום), from which the Rishonim (ראשונים) derive that it follows the Shemoneh Esrei immediately and that it is a reshus (רשות) and not a chiyuv (חיוב). A digression on obeying a wife against the will of Heaven, and a discussion of the days on which tachanun is omitted, lead to the Chassidic custom of omitting it.

Finally it takes up the Chassidic custom in detail, with the criticism of the Minchas Elazar (מנחת אלעזר), the stand of the Divrei Chaim (דברי חיים), and the story of the Radoshitzer Rebbe (ראדושיצער רבי) and the five thousand tachanunim (תחנונים), and closes with the guidance of Rav Moshe Feinstein (רב משה פיינשטיין) for one whose minyan (מנין) omits it, and the practice of the Chazon Ish (חזון איש), who said it alone after a baal tefillah (בעל תפילה) had skipped it in his own house, and then thanked him.

I. A Preliminary Question: To Whom Is ve'imru amen (ואמרו אמן) Said?

The Shemoneh Esrei (שמונה עשרה) ends with the words עושה שלום במרומיו, הוא יעשה שלום עלינו ועל כל ישראל ואמרו אמן, “He who makes peace in His heights, may He make peace upon us and upon all Israel, and say, Amen.”[2] In the Kaddish (קדיש) no question arises as to whom the words are addressed: the chazan (חזן) says them and the congregation answers. But here, at the end of a private prayer, there is no one to answer. To whom, then, is ve'imru amen said?

Some say that the words are addressed to the malachim (מלאכים). Having finished the long prayer, the worshipper turns to them: that all his prayers be accepted, and that there be peace upon Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל), say Amen. Their answer cannot be heard, but one is to imagine that they respond with a resounding Amen.

II. The Vidui (וידוי) Before Nefilas Apayim (נפילת אפים)

An Addition of the Arizal (האריז״ל)

Tachanun (תחנון), as it is said, begins with Ashamnu (אשמנו) and the Thirteen Attributes of Mercy (שלש עשרה מידות הרחמים), and only afterwards comes the fall on the face. The confession before the fall is not part of the original tachanun. In the time of the Rishonim (ראשונים) most communities did not say it. It is an addition from the time of the Arizal (1534–1572), and one more example of the degree to which the siddur (סידור) was reshaped by his introduction into daily prayer of practices rooted in Kabbalah (קבלה) and not in halachah (הלכה).

At present all of Klal Yisrael that follows Nusach Sefarad (נוסח ספרד) says Ashamnu before the fall on the face. In Nusach Ashkenaz (נוסח אשכנז) the practice varies: some say it on Mondays and Thursdays, some not at all, some only at certain times. All of it derives from the Arizal, who found the practice in the Zohar (זוהר).[3] It is not a matter of halachah in the Tur (טור) or the Shulchan Aruch (שלחן ערוך); the Tur is reported to note that some communities had the custom, but it was far from universal.[4] The Arizal introduced it, and from him it spread throughout Klal Yisrael, so that for some four centuries and more it has been kept with great fidelity.

The Zohar and the Accusers

The Zohar reveals what is neither seen nor heard. In Heaven, the moment a person finishes his prayer, the mekatrigim (מקטרגים) — the Sitra Achra (סטרא אחרא), the Samech-Mem, the Satan (שטן), whatever name is given to those forces of impurity — pounce on his prayers and seek to block them from reaching the Holy One. Their plea is: Why should the prayer of this man be accepted? He is a sinner; he has done this and that. And they begin to recite, against him, all the Ashamnu and bagadnu (בגדנו) that he has done.

The counsel of the Zohar is to be clever and to take their words from them: to state one’s sins before the accuser has had the chance to open his mouth. When the worshipper has already said that he has sinned and is sorry, the accuser is left standing in the corner with nothing to say; it has all been said already. Before he can block the prayer, a breach is made, and the worshipper says to the Holy One that there is no need to listen to him, for he himself has confessed. So important did the Arizal consider this, that he established it at once after the prayer, even though the Rishonim and the Geonim (גאונים) had not said it.

Why Only After Shacharis (שחרית)?

It may be asked why the confession is not placed likewise after Mincha (מנחה) and Maariv (מעריב). Only a tentative answer is given: it appears, from the account of the Zohar, that it is immediately after the morning prayer that the accuser acts to block, and the confession makes the breach before him. What takes place on high is not known, and a fuller answer was deferred.

III. Ashamnu bagadnu: A Confession Made on Behalf of All

“But I Did Not Do These Things”

A question is asked of the confession. If a person has never stolen, or if he repented and returned what he took, why does he say gazalnu (גזלנו)? Why does he stand before the Holy One and confess to what he did not do?

The answer is that in saying Ashamnu a person confesses not only for himself. He says: I am sorry on behalf of my friends, my colleagues, my peers, on behalf of all Klal Yisrael. It is not for a Jew to say, “Live and let live; my life is my own concern.” The people is connected in every member, and it is incumbent on each to do what he can to prevent any transgression. Klal Yisrael is one organism, one body; though its members may be distant from one another, in their essence they are one. Therefore each says Ashamnu even for sins he did not commit. And, to a degree, even he who has stolen nothing may have done something that was a trace of theft, and for that too he confesses.

The Account Brought by the Chida (חיד״א): The Sage and the Rambam (רמב״ם)

The Chida brings a well-known account.[5] In the time of the Rambam there was a great sage and tzaddik (צדיק) who came to him with a question. The Chida says that he had the account from one who had it from one, in an unbroken chain reaching back to the Rambam himself, which is a rare thing; the sage is not named.

The man told the Rambam that he did not say tachanun on Yom Kippur (יום כיפור), neither Ashamnu bagadnu nor Al Cheit (על חטא), because, looking at the list, he had not done these things. Why should he stand five times before the Holy One on that day and lie, saying he had done what he had not? The Rambam answered that he had better say it: every one of the sins in the Al Cheit and in the Ashamnu he transgresses every day; and, at his level, probably more.

How can a Jew transgress avodah zarah (עבודה זרה)? When was the last time any of us served an idol? The answer is that whoever becomes angry is as if he worships idols.[6] It is not literally so, but Chazal (חז״ל) regard it as a trace of avodah zarah. Whoever shouts at his wife in anger transgresses it; whoever lets his ga'avah (גאוה) rear its head transgresses it. So it is that a person transgresses all these things and does not know it. The Rambam added that he must now say Al Cheit also for the sin of not wanting to say Al Cheit; and that too is a transgression.

Bagadnu

The word bagadnu means that we were disloyal: that we were not faithful to the Holy One in the relationship with Him. It is the language of infidelity. Every transgression is, as it were, a form of unfaithfulness, and that is the meaning of the word.

IV. Ve'atah Tzaddik (ואתה צדיק על כל הבא עלינו): Accepting His Judgment

The Author of the Yesod VeShoresh HaAvodah (יסוד ושורש העבודה)

An interesting midrash (מדרש) is brought in the Yesod VeShoresh HaAvodah, a work of Rabbi Alexander Ziskind of Horodna, one of the great men of the generations immediately after the Baal Shem Tov (בעל שם טוב).[7] He was a great tzaddik and mekubal (מקובל) who came very close to joining the Chassidic movement, which in his time was a revolution in Klal Yisrael. He went to the Maggid of Mezritch (מגיד ממעזריטש), the chief disciple of the Baal Shem Tov and his successor, and said that he wished to be his disciple. The Maggid told him that he did not belong there: the Litvaks (ליטוואקעס) needed him. He was to remain with the misnagdim (מתנגדים), who would study his book.

Many of those who came to the Maggid stayed, and that is how Chassidus (חסידות) flourished. This author was perhaps the only man of his era so steeped in Kabbalah and in what may be called Chassidus in the general sense, not as a movement; and the world of the misnagdim is grateful that it held on to him. His book is easy to learn, and it gives understanding and appreciation of every prayer that is said and every mitzvah (מצוה) that is done; each part of it is best learned in its season, before Rosh Hashanah (ראש השנה), before Sukkos (סוכות), before Pesach.

The midrash

The book brings the midrash:[8] the Holy One says to the ministering angels, Look at My children, look at My beloved Klal Yisrael. I give them troubles, I give them sufferings, personal and communal, and the people suffers greatly. Yet instead of complaining, instead of saying that it is not fair, My children come to Me every day and say ואתה צדיק על כל הבא עלינו: You are righteous in all that has come upon us; what You do to us is right; we deserve it. You are the righteous One and we are the wicked, we have sinned. When they say this with passion, it arouses great compassion and mercy, and the Holy One shows it to the angels: Look at My children; they never blame Me, they always blame themselves.

For this reason there is a great segulah (סגולה) in saying Ve'atah Tzaddik with kavanah (כוונה) and complete anavah (ענוה), and in meaning it. It lends force to all the prayers that have just been said, that they be accepted.

V. Nefilas Apayim: Sources and Posture

The Sources in the Torah

Whence the practice of falling on the face? It is found with Moshe Rabbeinu (משה רבינו), in Parashas Eikev (פרשת עקב), ואתנפל, “and I fell down before the Lord.”[9] It is found in the story of Korach (קרח), where Moshe Rabbeinu and Aharon (אהרן) ויפלו על פניהם ויאמרו: א-ל אלהי הרוחות לכל בשר, “fell on their faces and said, O God, God of the spirits of all flesh.”[10] It appears that when forgiveness was sought in the matter of Korach, it was granted through the falling. Falling on the floor expresses subjugation: we are worthless; we nullify ourselves before the Holy One, immediately after the Shemoneh Esrei.

The Posture

In earlier times they literally fell on the floor, in every synagogue. At present the practice has been softened, and some remain standing. The Shulchan Aruch directs that it be done sitting and not standing, and so, it is said, is the practice according to Kabbalah.[11]

Why After the Prayer?

But whence the placement of the fall on the face immediately after the prayer? There is no passage in the Gemara (גמרא), and nothing explicit in Chazal, that says that it is to be done after the Shemoneh Esrei. The source is a story in the Gemara, one of the most important in understanding the authority of the Torah Shebe'al Peh (תורה שבעל פה), the story of the oven of Achnai.

VI. The Oven of Achnai (תנור של עכנאי), Rabbi Eliezer (רבי אליעזר בן הורקנוס), and Imma Shalom (אימא שלום)

The Setting

After the churban (חורבן), Rabbi Yochanan ben Zakkai (רבי יוחנן בן זכאי) led Klal Yisrael and was its authority in halachah. It was a time of crisis: the spiritual center of the people, the Beis HaMikdash, was gone; the Sadducees (צדוקים) and the Pharisees (פרושים) had been at war, and the Sadducees had undermined all of the Torah Shebe'al Peh. The Pharisees, a far smaller body, the Sages of Chazal with whom the people has stayed, had moved the Sanhedrin (סנהדרין) to Yavneh (יבנה) shortly before the churban, and it became the center of authority in halachah.[12] Rabbi Yochanan ben Zakkai was afraid that without the Beis HaMikdash, with Klal Yisrael scattered, the people would be lost. It was necessary to show that the power of Chazal was intact, and that a ruling of halachah remained binding on every Jew.

The Dispute

In that period Rabbi Eliezer, one of the greatest of the tanna'im (תנאים), disputed with all the Sages (חכמים) the case of a certain oven.[13] It had been cut into rings, with sand placed between them, and the question was whether it could receive tumah (טומאה). Rabbi Eliezer held that it was tahor (טהור): once it had been broken down, the putting of it together again did not make it a keli (כלי), and so it could not become tamei (טמא). The Sages held that it was tamei.

The two sides argued, and Rabbi Eliezer would not yield. The Sages said that they had counted the votes, that they were the rabim (רבים); he might hold as he wished, but he must submit to them. He answered that the truth was with him and that he would prove it through nissim (נסים). The carob tree outside the beis midrash (בית המדרש) uprooted itself; the stream of water flowed backward; the walls of the beis midrash began to lean, as though to fall on the Sages. He said: See, the halachah is as I say. The Sages were not moved. At the height of the dispute he called out: Masters, hear the bas kol (בת קול)! A voice from Heaven declared that the halachah was as he said. Rabbi Yehoshua (רבי יהושע) rose and proclaimed לא בשמים היא, “It is not in heaven.”[14]

That declaration established the authority of Chazal to this day: Heaven is not heeded in halachah, for the Torah was given to us. The Gemara relates that when the Holy One heard it He smiled and said Nitzchuni Banai (נצחוני בני), “My children have defeated Me.” The Holy One held the halachah as Rabbi Eliezer did, but the decision was no longer His to make. The Torah was given to us, and we decide by our power of understanding it and by the methods we were taught.

The Ban

Rabbi Eliezer was therefore placed in niduy (נידוי), one of the harshest penalties in all the stories of the tanna'im. He remained at home, barred from the beis midrash, unable even to pray with a minyan (מנין), a lonely, isolated, rejected and alienated man for many years, until the day of his death, when, as the account is told, the Sages came to him and said that they were sorry for what they had done, but that they had had to do it.[15] Rabbi Akiva (רבי עקיבא), his own student, stood with the Sages and was the one who brought him the news. The greatest man of the generation was under the ban.

Imma Shalom

The wife of Rabbi Eliezer was named Imma Shalom, “Mother of Peace,” an apt name for a wife. She was the sister of Rabban Gamliel (רבן גמליאל), who led the ban. He had become nasi (נשיא) after Rabbi Yochanan ben Zakkai, and came of a family of royal lineage. She was caught between a rock and a hard place: her brother, the leader of the generation, had placed her husband, its greatest man, under the ban.

She understood that her husband was deeply hurt, and that his power of prayer was exceptional. Should he pray — God forbid — that her brother be punished or die for what he had done to him, though the ban was justified (the Gemara teaches that even the Holy One agreed that Rabbi Eliezer was right, and yet we stand by Chazal; it is our Torah), his prayer would be answered. It was not the Shemoneh Esrei alone that she feared. She was most concerned for the fall on the face that followed it: for in the Shemoneh Esrei his feelings might or might not rise, but when he fell to the floor and felt the full pain of being cast out of his beis midrash for the sake of a true ruling, his anguish would be so great that the Holy One would surely hear him. She wanted peace.

Every day, therefore, while Rabbi Eliezer prayed at home, she stood by him; and the moment he finished the Shemoneh Esrei she distracted him, so that he forgot tachanun and the fall on the face. So she did every day for a long time, and she was saving her brother’s life every morning.[16]

The Day of the Shofar (שופר)

One day there was a mistake. There were no calendars on the wall in those days; she thought it was Rosh Chodesh (ראש חודש), when tachanun is not said, but she had erred by a day in the length of the month. For the first time in a long while she did not interrupt, and Rabbi Eliezer fell on his face and said tachanun. Suddenly she ran into the room: Woe! You have killed my brother! As the words left her mouth a shofar sounded from the house of Rabban Gamliel. The nasi had died. Rabbi Eliezer had done it in that one moment.

The Gemara gives another version: a poor man knocked at the door, and she ran to give him a loaf of bread, and in those moments Rabbi Eliezer had his chance. Whichever account is correct, on that day, in that moment, he fell on his face, and Rabban Gamliel died.

VII. The Power of Nefilas Apayim

The power of the fall on the face is thus plain. Rabbi Eliezer stood on the highest rank. But when an ordinary Jew says it, according to the Arizal and the Rishonim it is even more potent than the Shemoneh Esrei. The physical act of falling, of subjugating oneself completely, saying Ashamnu, bagadnu, gazalnu, and showing the Holy One that for all one’s ego when one stood before Him in the Shemoneh Esrei, one is nothing: the anguish that a Jew then feels arouses overwhelming compassion and mercy. If a man is pursued by another, if someone hates him, this is the time to ask that his pursuers be removed.

That is not to be done against those one merely dislikes. The model is not the world but Imma Shalom, who wanted peace. Yet this is the most powerful moment of the entire prayer, where a Jew may ask the Holy One to be rid of his enemies, and it is to be said as much as possible.

VIII. The Halachos (הלכות) of Nefilas Apayim

Immediately After the Shemoneh Esrei

It is not stated in the Gemara that Rabbi Eliezer fell on his face immediately after the Shemoneh Esrei; the Rishonim understand it so. The Ritva (ריטב״א) asks: Had Imma Shalom nothing better to do, all day, than to stand beside her husband while he prayed, and the moment he finished to begin to distract him? Perhaps he might have said it later in the day; did she not leave his side the whole day, to see that he did not say tachanun? She was not that kind of wife, the Ritva answers. The proof is that it was right after the Shemoneh Esrei, so that she needed to be with him only for those few minutes. She knew when Rabbi Eliezer finished the prayer each day, and she came into the room at that moment and made him forget. From this the Gemara teaches that tachanun, the fall on the face, is bound to the Shemoneh Esrei and is to be said immediately after it.[17]

The Beis Yosef (בית יוסף) brings a proof from this Gemara that nefilas apayim is said after the Shemoneh Esrei. The Acharonim (אחרונים) ask what, exactly, the proof is. On the view of the Ritva it can be understood in part, though it might equally have been after Mincha; but the implication of the Gemara is that her interruption was after Shacharis.

A reshus (רשות), Not a chiyuv (חיוב)

The Rishonim derive another point from this Gemara: that the fall on the face is only a reshus, not a chiyuv; it may be done and it may be left undone. Were it a chiyuv, Rabbi Eliezer would not have yielded to his wife; he would have said, Forgive me, Rebbetzin (רבנית), I am saying tachanun. If one’s wife tells him not to say Kerias Shema (קריאת שמע) or the Shemoneh Esrei, he does not listen.

The Chofetz Chaim (חפץ חיים) is reported to write that there are times when one does not listen to one’s wife.[18] One should listen to her in nearly everything, and be a great advocate of shalom bayis (שלום בית); but when what is asked runs against the will of the Holy One, he must stand firm, doing so with care and without causing trouble at home. Where a wife asks her husband to act against what the Holy One wants, that is the time to say: we follow the Holy One, and peace upon Israel.

IX. The Chassidic Custom of Omitting Tachanun

Who decides whether tachanun is said on a given day? In many Chassidic houses of prayer, when the baal tefillah (בעל תפילה) has a yahrzeit, or even a doubtful yahrzeit, it is he who determines whether the congregation omits it. This is a sensitive matter, and it is not for the lecturer to mock or to criticize Chassidim (חסידים).

The Origin of the Custom

The custom goes back to the disciples of the Baal Shem Tov.[19] Their reasoning was a grave one. Tachanun is an extraordinarily potent and powerful prayer; if one is not fully directed in it, as Rabbi Eliezer was directed, whose intense kavanah cost Rabban Gamliel his life, it is dangerous. One is falling before the Holy One, with all the power of the prayer, and if one fails in his kavanah the result is terrible. The Zohar is reported to say in so many words that whoever is not properly directed in the fall on the face will not merit length of days: the Holy One will cut him down before his time. From this the custom spread to omit it whenever there is even a trace of joy.

A second ground is given for the same custom: the disciples of the Baal Shem Tov held that the day of the death of a tzaddik is a hilula (הילולא), like that of Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai (רבי שמעון בר יוחאי), which, as reported, the Zohar calls a hilula, and a hilula connotes joy; so tachanun is not said. By the same reasoning tachanun is omitted on Chanukah (חנוכה) and Purim (פורים). It is of interest that in the great academies of Bavel (בבל), Pumbedisa (פומבדיתא) and the others, it is said that tachanun was said even on Chanukah and Purim.

The Criticism of the Minchas Elazar (מנחת אלעזר)

The holy Minchas Elazar wrote in his books, with fire and brimstone, against the Chassidim who choose any yahrzeit of any tzaddik as grounds to omit tachanun.[20] His argument is that there is no day in the Jewish calendar without the yahrzeit of some holy man; carried to its conclusion, tachanun would never be said at all. Many Chassidim did indeed keep handbooks of the yahrzeit of tzaddikim (צדיקים) for this purpose. It is recalled that at the Chassidic yeshiva in Kerestir, after the prayer, the handbook was opened at once, and it was noted that this day was the yahrzeit of such and such a Rebbe; and tachanun was almost never said.

The Divrei Chaim (דברי חיים) and His Stick

The Divrei Chaim, the holy Rebbe of Sanz (צאנז), was the father of the last generation of Chassidus.[21] For the Galician, Polish and Hungarian Chassidim, the Rebbe of Sanz was everything; even the Satmar Rebbe, one of the leading figures of Chassidic life on American shores, followed Sanz in all things. The Rebbe of Sanz omitted tachanun on one yahrzeit only, 21 Adar, that of the Noam Elimelech (נועם אלימלך). That was the single exception. On any other occasion, if a Chassid (חסיד) decided on the strength of his Rebbe’s yahrzeit that tachanun was not to be said, the Rebbe of Sanz took out a stick and said: Everyone, say tachanun, now. So the great Chassidic Rebbes, Chassidic as they were, derided the custom, and so did the Minchas Elazar.

The Radoshitzer Rebbe (ראדושיצער רבי) and the Five Thousand tachanunim (תחנונים)

Even the Chassidic Rebbes who cared about halachah were troubled by the abandonment of tachanun. A story is told of the Radoshitzer Rebbe.[22] A man came to him who had lost his fortune, a yored minechasav (יורד מנכסיו), and said: Rebbe, I have lost everything; my family is hungry; I have debts; what shall I do? I need salvation.

The Rebbe asked him whether he said tachanun every day. The man answered that it was a foreign word. How long, the Rebbe asked, had he been missing tachanun at Shacharis? Since childhood, he said; he had grown up in a Chassidic yeshiva and never said it, perhaps a few times a year. And how many times had he missed it in his life? The man sat down and calculated: thousands.

The Rebbe told him that he must now make up every tachanun he had missed: to sit and say tachanun five thousand times, over and over, and only then to ask for salvation. The man took it seriously. He sat for five weeks, saying tachanunim seventy or eighty times a day, until he had completed the five thousand. The day he finished the last of them, he closed a successful business deal; it all ended well, and he recovered his fortune.

X. The Days on Which Tachanun Is Omitted

Since tachanun is in its origin a reshus and not a chiyuv, whenever there is a recognized reason it is omitted. By the standard list: on Lag BaOmer (ל״ג בעומר), the yahrzeit of Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai, called a hilula in the Zohar; at a bris (ברית מילה), though there is a dispute concerning Mincha on the day of a bris; when a chasan (חתן) is present, as at Shalosh Seudos (שלש סעודות); by some, even at a bar mitzvah (בר מצוה) or a siyum (סיום מסכתא); on Chanukah and Purim; on Rosh Chodesh, on yamim tovim (ימים טובים), and on Chol HaMoed (חול המועד); and through Tishrei (תשרי) after Sukkos.[23]

The question of Mincha on the day of a bris is discussed by an authority the lecturer called the Bar Heitam (בר היטם), in siman (סימן) 131: in Brisk (בריסק) they did say tachanun at Mincha even if the bris had taken place that day, while elsewhere they did not — a dispute, it is said, between Brisk and Vilna (וילנא).[24]

A Note on Yahrzeit Practice

According to the Shulchan Aruch, the original practice on a parent’s yahrzeit was not to drink brandy and eat cake, but to fast.[25] Who fasts at present on the yahrzeit of his father? The Litvaks do, and others who follow the Shulchan Aruch keep it; for the most part the practice has been softened. The holy Rav Zalman Meltzer is reported to have fasted on a yahrzeit and to have held a seudah (סעודה).

XI. Rav Moshe Feinstein (רב משה פיינשטיין): Do Not Make a Fight

Even the great rabbis who held strongly that tachanun should be said addressed the practical problem: what is a person to do who finds himself in a minyan that omits it? Rav Moshe Feinstein rules, according to the report, that he is not to make a fight and not to start screaming.[26] This is what so often happens in the synagogue: tachanun, or no tachanun, and an argument breaks out. He teaches that this is to miss the point. Even if in his view the others are acting contrary to halachah, after the Kaddish one goes to the side and says tachanun quietly on his own. One is not trying to embarrass anyone, and one is not making a public stand. (In the order of the prayer, tachanun stands before Ashrei (אשרי) and U'va LeTzion (ובא לציון), for a reason the lecturer proposed to take up later. But it is to be said.)

XII. The Chazon Ish (חזון איש) and the baal tefillah

The Chazon Ish never prayed in a synagogue; he had his own minyan in his house in Bnei Brak, a private shtiebel (שטיבל).[27] He did not know of selective omission of tachanun. The Shulchan Aruch says that it is said; it is omitted on Rosh Chodesh, on yamim tovim, on Chol HaMoed, and, as the discourse has it, through Tishrei after Sukkos, since most of the month it was not said — and no more. In his minyan, where he was the rabbi and decided everything, all went by the book.

One day a shaliach tzibur (שליח ציבור) came, perhaps a Chassid, who decided that it was his Rebbe’s yahrzeit and that he would not say tachanun. Without asking the Chazon Ish, in his own house, he went straight into Kaddish after the Shemoneh Esrei, giving no one a chance to begin.

One would have expected the Chazon Ish to protest. He said nothing. A student who was present testified that he remained silent. After the prayer, quietly, after Ashrei and U'va LeTzion, the Chazon Ish went to the side, in his own house and in his own minyan, and said tachanun by himself. Then he turned to the baal tefillah and said יישר כח: not a word of criticism.

The baal tefillah had certainly erred in acting against halachah in the Chazon Ish’s own minyan. But the lesson is for all. He did not publicly correct or rebuke. When one is tempted to make a scene over tachanun, whether to say it or not to say it, one should think twice. If one wishes to say tachanun as Moshe Rabbeinu said it, as Rabbi Eliezer said it, as the Chazon Ish said it, one should say it quietly, on his own.

Editorial Summary

The discourse closes on the guidance of the last two stories, and does not itself draw a conclusion. In outline, it presents tachanun as a prayer of great power: preceded by a confession that the Arizal introduced to forestall the accusers; resting, in its fall on the face, on the story of Rabbi Eliezer and Imma Shalom; a reshus that is omitted for recognized reasons; and the subject of a Chassidic custom of omission that earlier authorities criticized. Where the custom of one’s minyan differs, the course taught is to avoid dispute, and to say it quietly alone.
