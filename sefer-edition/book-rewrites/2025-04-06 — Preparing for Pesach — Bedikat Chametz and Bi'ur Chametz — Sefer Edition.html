הַהֲכָנָה לְפֶסַח — בְּדִיקַת חָמֵץ וּבִיעוּר חָמֵץ

Preparing for Pesach — The Uniqueness of Chametz, the Nature of Tashbisu, and the Avodah of Rooting Out the Yetzer HaRa
Shiur of Rabbi Ephrati — Sunday Morning Series on Tefillah
Recorded 6 April 2025 (ח' ניסן תשפ"ה)  ·  Sefer Edition

A pause from the running track of hilchos tefillah six days before Pesach: the uniqueness of the issur of chametz, the Rambam’s reading of bittul, the tekanah of bedikah, the Minchas Chinuch’s chakirah, and the chametz that lies concealed in the corners of a man’s own heart.

I. Hachanah — The Preparation Before the Mitzvah

Why Prepare

To enter a Yom Tov (יום טוב) authentically, a person cannot simply be carried into it by the passage of the calendar. Bedikas chametz (בדיקת חמץ), bi’ur chametz (ביעור חמץ), the Seder (סדר) with all its mitzvos — each of these requires hachanah (הכנה). It is a teaching of the chassidim (חסידים) that the preparation before a mitzvah is itself essential to the mitzvah: appreciating, valuing, and treasuring the mitzvah begin with understanding what one is about to do — both spiritually and concretely. For that reason, the ordinary weekly study of hilchos tefillah (הלכות תפילה) yields, in the days before Pesach, to the halachos and the avodas Hashem (עבודת ה') of bedikas chametz and bi’ur chametz.

A Milsa D’Bedichusa Told Le’Sheim Shamayim

It is fitting, before entering into the substance, to preserve one milsa d’bedichusa (מילתא דבדיחותא) told le’sheim Shamayim (לשם שמים) — a saying of Rabbi Itzele Charif (רבי איצלה חריף) of Slonim,[1] a gaon olam (גאון עולם) more widely remembered for his sharp bon mot than for his Torah. On Erev Pesach his rebbetzin (רבנית) inquired whether he had finished with all the chametz. He replied that he had burned everything — but there was one piece of chametz he had not been able to get rid of for thirty-five years. She, equally sharp, answered: “That is what you are worried about? My father sold that chametz to a goy gamur (גוי גמור) thirty-five years ago.” (It is a lashon of Chazal (חז"ל) that when we say goy gamur, we always mean a Jew — a non-Jew is never called a goy gamur.)

II. The Uniqueness of the Issur of Chametz

Three Layers of Prohibition

Chametz (חמץ) on Pesach is unlike any other issur (איסור) in the Torah. Three distinct prohibitions layer upon a single substance:

  1. Issur achilah (איסור אכילה) — eating chametz on Pesach, carrying with it the chiyuv kares (חיוב כרת).
  2. Issur hana’ah (איסור הנאה) — even non-eating uses, such as feeding it to one’s animal.
  3. Bal yeira’eh u’val yimatzeh (בל יראה ובל ימצא) — a Jew may not own chametz on Pesach, even if he never intends to eat it. The Torah commands the elimination of possession, not merely of consumption.

This third element has no parallel elsewhere in the Torah. Basar v’chalav (בשר וחלב) is forbidden to eat, but the Torah does not forbid the ownership of basar v’chalav. Cheilev (חלב) — forbidden fats — dam (דם), and other issurei achilah may sit in one’s house indefinitely without transgression. Only chametz on Pesach does the Torah demand be removed from one’s domain altogether.

The Rationale of the Tosafos

The Tosafos (תוספות) at the opening of Maseches Pesachim[2] address the structural question directly. The reason is that chametz is what one eats every day of the year. Basar v’chalav is forbidden always, and a Jew is conditioned from childhood never to eat it; chametz is the inverse case: a daily food that becomes forbidden for a single week. The Torah anticipates the natural mistake — the person wakes tired after the Seder, walks into the kitchen, reaches. It removes the temptation by removing the chametz entirely. Bal yeira’eh is a seyag (סייג) against accidental achilas chametz with its chiyuv kares.

III. Bittul Alone — The Mideoraisa Fulfillment

The Position of the Rambam

The Rambam (רמב"ם),[3] following the peshat of the Gemara at the opening of Pesachim,[4] rules that mideoraisa, the mitzvah of “tashbisu” (תשביתו) is fulfilled through bittul (ביטול) alone. Were one to strip away the layer of tekanos that Chazal added on top of the Torah’s command, Erev Pesach would look very different from the day the mispallel knows: no bedikah; no sereifah (שריפה), for mideoraisa there is no obligation to burn the chametz. One would sit at the proper moment, focus one’s mind, and declare:

כָּל חֲמִירָא וַחֲמִיעָא דְּאִיכָּא בִּרְשׁוּתִי, דְּלָא חֲזִיתֵיהּ וּדְלָא בִעַרְתֵּיהּ, לִבָּטֵל וְלֶהֱוֵי הֶפְקֵר כְּעַפְרָא דְאַרְעָא.
All chametz in my possession is nullified, rendered ownerless, like the dust of the earth.

How Bittul Works

Once one has authentically made this bittul, the chametz is no longer his. It may sit in his cabinets throughout Pesach, and he has not transgressed bal yeira’eh u’val yimatzeh, for there is no chametz of his to be seen or found — just as the dust upon his floor lies in his house but is not his, and any passerby may take it. On this mideoraisa plane, Erev Pesach would consist entirely of a family seated together, each reciting the bittul with genuine kavvanah (כוונה) — and the entire obligation would be discharged.

IV. The Tekanas Chachomim — Bedikah and Bi'ur

The Mishnah

The first Mishnah in Pesachim[5] records the rabbinic layer: “Or l’arba’ah asar bodkin es hachametz l’or ha’ner” (אור לארבעה עשר בודקין את החמץ לאור הנר) — on the night of the fourteenth of Nissan (ניסן) the chametz is checked by candlelight; and on the morning of the fourteenth it is burned. Active removal — a ma’aseh (מעשה). What compelled Chazal to add to the Torah’s requirement?

The Reason of the Tosafos

The Tosafos explain that bittul is purely a matzav nafshi (מצב נפשי) — a mental act of release. The words themselves are not the bittul; the inner kavvanah is. Not everyone can authentically reach that inner state, and many people mouth the words of the nusach (נוסח) without any genuine act of release. For that reason the Mishnah Berurah[6] rules that one who does not understand the Aramaic of “kol chamira” must translate it into a language he understands — Yiddish, English, or Hebrew — before reciting; the bittul requires understanding. Where authentic bittul has not been achieved, the chametz remains the person’s and bal yeira’eh u’val yimatzeh is being violated. Chazal’s tekanah: do not rely on the inner state alone. Make it physical. Search, find, remove. Ma’aseh operates precisely where machshavah may have failed.

The Reason of the Ran

The Ran (ר"ן)[7] offers a different rationale. He concedes that bittul works mideoraisa for bal yeira’eh. The concern to which he addresses the tekanah is downstream: the mispallel wakes on Pesach morning, sees a beautiful bottle of schnapps or a geshmake (געשמאקע) roll of chametz, and his hand instinctively reaches. His earlier bittul protects him from bal yeira’eh, but it does not protect him from the issur achilah that operates independently of ownership. Chazal intervened: remove the chametz physically from the home, and the temptation goes with it.

V. The Chakirah of the Minchas Chinuch

The Sefer and Its Author

The Sefer Minchas Chinuch (ספר מנחת חינוך)[8] — composed by Rabbi Yosef Babad (רבי יוסף באבאד), Rav of Tarnopol in eastern Galicia (1801–1874; remarkably, his birth and death fell on the same day of the year) — is one of the great sefarim of analytical lomdus (למדנות). On the mitzvah of tashbisu he raises a foundational chakirah (חקירה) whose implications shape the entire understanding of the day.

Two Categories of Mitzvah

The general framework of mitzvos divides into two categories:

  1. Mitzvos kum va’aseh (מצות קום ועשה) — fulfilled by active performance: tefillin (תפילין), shofar (שופר), sukkah (סוכה), matzah (מצה).
  2. Mitzvos sheiv v’al ta’aseh (מצות שב ואל תעשה) — fulfilled by inaction. The mitzvas asei (מצות עשה) of “u’vayom hashvi’i tishbos” (וביום השביעי תשבות): every moment one refrains from melachah (מלאכה) is a moment of active fulfillment of the mitzvah of resting. Hands folded, seated still on Shabbos (שבת) — the mitzvah is being performed.

Which Is Tashbisu?

The Minchas Chinuch’s chakirah: did the Torah command an active destruction — get up, find chametz, burn it, perform bittul, do something? Or did the Torah command not having chametz, a sheiv v’al ta’aseh in which the absence itself is the fulfillment?

VI. Three Nafka Minos

The Minchas Chinuch brings three concrete cases in which the answer to this chakirah is decisive.

The First — The Jew Who Has No Chametz

Consider a Jew who is gluten-free; chametz has not entered his house in seven months. Erev Pesach arrives. Must he buy chametz in order to burn it?

  • If tashbisu is kum va’aseh, he must perform an act; he should acquire chametz in order to discharge the mitzvah.
  • If tashbisu is sheiv v’al ta’aseh, he is already yotzei; he has no chametz; that is precisely what the Torah desired.

The Minchas Chinuch raises the first option in earnest, however strange it may sound: perhaps the Torah desired that one buy chametz for the sake of burning it.

The Second — Eating Chametz as Bi'ur

One finds a piece of chametz on Pesach. He must remove it. May he eat it? It would, after all, no longer be in his possession. Suppose the discovery occurs on Shabbos afternoon — when burning is forbidden anyway — while the issur achilah is fully operative. If he eats, has he been mekayem tashbisu through the eating?

The objection: this appears to be mitzvah ha’ba’ah b’aveirah (מצוה הבאה בעבירה) — many poskim hold that a mitzvah performed by means of an aveirah is invalid. Eating chametz is the aveirah; one cannot dress it up as a mitzvah. The Minchas Chinuch answers that the question depends on our chakirah:

  • If tashbisu is kum va’aseh, the act of removal is the mitzvah, and here the act of removal is the eating itself. This is textbook mitzvah ha’ba’ah b’aveirah; invalid.
  • If tashbisu is sheiv v’al ta’aseh, only the state of not having chametz is the mitzvah. The eating was a hechsher (הכשר), a means to that state. Not mitzvah ha’ba’ah b’aveirah; the mitzvah may be valid (although the eater is nevertheless chayav kares for the achilas chametz).

The Third — Stealing Another’s Bi'ur

A Jew walks to his bi’ur (ביעור) with a bag of chametz. Another grabs the bag and throws it into the fire before its owner can. He has stolen the mitzvah.

The general gemara’s rule, established in the sugya of kisui ha’dam (כיסוי הדם) — where one who covers another’s blood owes the shochet ten zuzim[9] — is that one who steals another’s mitzvah pays the fixed kenas. Whether that halachah applies here depends on our chakirah:

  • If tashbisu is kum va’aseh, the interloper stole the mitzvah; he owes ten zuzim.
  • If tashbisu is sheiv v’al ta’aseh, he did not steal the mitzvah; he helped. One says to him yasher ko’ach (יישר כח) and owes him nothing.

VII. Chametz as the Yetzer HaRa

The Chasam Sofer’s Question on Matzah

The Chasam Sofer (חתם סופר) in his commentary on the Haggadah[10] raises a counter-intuitive observation. Chazal teach that matzah may only be made from a grain that can become chametz. Rice flour, corn flour, ground potato — none may be used for matzah, however diligently baked. Only the five minei dagan (מיני דגן), which can ferment into chametz, qualify.

The observation cuts sharply: the most dangerous ingredient on Pesach is matzah itself. Matzah is flour and water — the exact formula for chametz. It is baked within eighteen minutes; scrupulous ba’alei batim on Erev Pesach are makpid on kefulos (קפולות),[11] the folds in matzah where the dough may have doubled over and failed to bake through, breaking off the folds and discarding them before the zman bi’ur. Why did the Torah command that we take precisely the most dangerous ingredient and make our mitzvah from it? Why not use rice — which the Gemara teaches cannot become chametz, undergoing only sirchon (סרחון), decay, rather than fermentation — and have a worry-free Pesach?

The Radbaz — Chametz Is the Yetzer HaRa

The Radbaz (רדב"ז)[12] writes — not as chassidus and not as Kabbalah, but as the simple peshat of the chumrah in the issur — that chametz represents the yetzer ha’ra (יצר הרע). The Torah&rsquo>s extreme stringency on chametz — even a mashehu (משהו) forbidden, even owning it forbidden, chiyuv kares for eating — is grounded in the fact that the chametz we hunt is simultaneously the yetzer ha’ra we hunt.

The Chasam Sofer’s Answer

The avodah of achilas matzah is precisely the journey from potential chametz to matzah: taking the very flour that could ferment into the yetzer ha’ra and arresting it within eighteen minutes, transforming it into kedushah (קדושה). The tzaddikim gemurim (צדיקים גמורים) who have long since transmuted their yetzer ha’ra into avodas Hashem are not the primary audience for this mitzvah; the mitzvah is for the rest — those still struggling, those whose flour can still ferment. Rice flour lacks the potential; it cannot teach the avodah.

The same reading applies to tashbisu itself. The mitzvah is not the mere absence of chametz but the active finding and burning. Pesach is the season for going into the corners of one’s own heart, seeking out the yetzer ha’ra, and defeating it — searching, breaking, burning.

This reading may sit behind the Minchas Chinuch’s chakirah itself. If tashbisu were merely the absence of chametz, the gluten-free Jew would be yotzei automatically. But if tashbisu is the avodah of confrontation — finding the yetzer ha’ra in order to burn it — then the gluten-free Jew is missing the avodah of Pesach. He needs to confront chametz, not merely to lack it.

VIII. The Search Within

The other face of the same teaching addresses the many who walk into Pesach convinced that they are already fine. “I am fine relative to others. I learn the daf; I daven three times a day; I give tzedakah; I put on tefillin. I am fine.”

Yet the pasuk[13] teaches: there is no such person as one who is wholly tzaddik. The Torah’s instruction at this parshah of the year: go check for chametz. Go into the hidden recesses of one’s heart, find the chametz being carried there, and burn it. To sit on Erev Pesach declaring “I have no chametz” is not the avodah; the avodah is the ma’aseh of bedikah — looking honestly, by candlelight, into the corners.

IX. Yachatz — The Deliberate Breaking

This avodah explains a striking feature of the Seder. Throughout the Torah, our mitzvos generally require their objects to be whole. A shofar with a hole is invalid; an esrog (אתרוג) with a blemish is invalid; the box of tefillin must be perfectly square. Wholeness is the rule.

And then, in the very middle of the Seder, we perform yachatz (יחץ): we deliberately break the middle matzah in half.

The teaching: Pesach is the night on which one acknowledges that he is not whole. However righteous he believes himself to be, there is a piece missing. One comes to the Kadosh Baruch Hu (הקדוש ברוך הוא) through yachatz not whole but vulnerable — “we are only half; help us to become whole.” The avodah of Pesach begins with that admission.

X. A Historical Note on Mechiras Chametz

The Question of Chametz Gamur

An adjacent question arises around chametz gamur (חמץ גמור) — the practice of selling actual chametz (not merely chametz-touched utensils) to a non-Jew over Pesach. Practice varies widely, and many ba’alei nefesh (בעלי נפש) follow the older minhag of disposing of chametz gamur entirely rather than relying on mechirah.

The Historical Development

The historical reality of mechiras chametz (מכירת חמץ)[14] is instructive. In earlier generations no such practice existed. One had chametz, one disposed of it, and that was that. Mechiras chametz in its formal contemporary shape developed in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries in Europe, in response to a specific economic situation.

The most common respectable parnasah (פרנסה) for the European Jew in that era was running an inn or a tavern — the kretshmer (קרעטשמער) Jews, holding the local nobleman’s license, operating distilleries with barrels of whisky and grain-based spirits. To require these Yidden to destroy their entire stock for Pesach would have meant economic ruin. The poskim of those generations developed the formal mechanism of mechiras chametz to permit these Yidden to continue in their trade.

The Contemporary Machlokes

Some poskim hold that mechiras chametz for actual chametz gamur was developed only for cases resembling the schnapps of the tavern-keeper — significant economic loss, serious enjoyment over the year — and not for a few dollars’ worth of leftover cookies. On this position, better to throw the marginal cookies away than to include them in the sale. Other poskim hold that mechiras chametz applies fully to all chametz gamur without such distinction. The machlokes is live.

The broader point is one of historical awareness in halachah: what appears today as a self-evident, universal minhag is in fact a three-hundred-year-old response to a specific economic reality. Halachah did not descend uniform from Sinai on every particular; the mechanisms that structure our Erev Pesach were forged in the workshops of the poskim of Poland, Galicia, and Bohemia in the face of the world in which their communities lived.

XI. Practical Closing

  • Bittul is real. Recite “kol chamira v’chami’a” with genuine kavvanah; understand the words. If Aramaic is opaque, translate.
  • Bedikah is a ma’aseh, not a formality. Search honestly, by candlelight, into the corners of the home.
  • Bi’ur is the physical arm of the mitzvah. The chametz is removed by hand and by fire.
  • The inner search is the point. Beneath the halachos, the avodah of the day is to find the chametz hidden in the corners of one’s own heart, the yetzer ha’ra one has been quietly carrying, and to burn it.
  • Yachatz teaches the posture. Come before the Ribbono Shel Olam (רבונו של עולם) not whole but broken; only from that place can wholeness be granted.

May we merit to fulfill bedikas chametz and bi’ur chametz with full heart, and to see the coming of Mashiach Tzidkeinu bimheirah b’yameinu (משיח צדקנו במהרה בימינו), Amen.

Shu’t HaRadbaz — chametz as the yetzer ha’ra; the peshat behind the stringency of the issur. — Rabbi David ben Zimra (the Radbaz, 1479–1573, Egypt and Tzfas), in his Shu’t, develops the reading that chametz’s extreme chumrah in the Torah — the only issur achilah with issur hana’ah and bal yeira’eh, forbidden even b’mashehu, chiyuv kares for achilah — is grounded in chametz as a representation of the yetzer ha’ra. The Radbaz presents this not as chassidus or Kabbalah but as the peshat of the chumrah: the Torah’s war against chametz is, simultaneously, the annual avodah of confronting and defeating the yetzer ha’ra. ↩
  13. Koheles 7:20 — כי אדם אין צדיק בארץ אשר יעשה טוב ולא יחטא. — Shlomo HaMelech’s pasuk: Ki adam ein tzaddik ba’aretz asher ya’aseh tov v’lo yecheta — “there is no tzaddik on the earth who does good and does not sin.” The pasuk anchors the classical teaching that even the most upright person carries chametz within — residual chelbenah (חלבנה) in the ketores of a life — and that Pesach is the season for honest self-examination rather than complacent self-assessment. The same theme runs through the Nefesh HaChaim and the mussar literature more broadly. ↩
  14. Historical development of mechiras chametz in seventeenth- and eighteenth-century European poskim. — The formal mechanism for selling chametz gamur to a non-Jew over Pesach emerged in response to the economic structure of the kretshmer (innkeeper/tavern-keeper) Jewish economy in Poland, Galicia, and Bohemia, where Jewish lessees of noble distilleries and taverns held large stocks of grain spirits whose destruction would have meant financial ruin. The mechanism was developed by poskim across this period (the Beis Meir, the Chok Yaakov, the Pnei Yehoshua, and others) and codified in the Shulchan Aruch HaRav and later poskim. A live machlokes remains among contemporary poskim whether the heter applies fully to all chametz gamur or only to cases of significant economic loss; many ba’alei nefesh follow the older minhag of disposing of chametz gamur entirely rather than including it in the sale. ↩
