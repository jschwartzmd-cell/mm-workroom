זוֹכֶה וְרוֹאֶה בְּשִׂמְחָתָהּ — אֲבֵלוּת שֶׁיֵּשׁ בָּהּ טַעַם שִׂמְחָה

Zoche V'Roeh B'Simchasa: Mourning That Already Tastes of Joy
Tisha B'Av — The Inner Architecture of Aveilus and Nechamah
Tisha B'Av, 13 August 2024  ·  Reworked 30 April 2026  ·  Sefer Edition · Revised 10 August 2026

A meditation for the afternoon of Tisha B'Av — when only divrei emunah u'vitachon are permitted — on the question of why Chazal phrase the reward of mourning Yerushalayim in the present tense, and what that grammar reveals about the inner life of aveilus: that faithful mourning is not despair but fidelity, and fidelity already contains, in hidden form, the first taste of nechamah.

I. The Halachic Frame of the Day

The Gemara in Ta'anis[1] teaches that one who performs melachah on Tisha B'Av "will not see in [Yerushalayim's] joy." There are kulos for one under genuine pressing need, but the plain halachah is unambiguous: the day belongs to aveilus. The same Gemara's positive formulation states the foundational principle:

כָּל הַמִּתְאַבֵּל עַל יְרוּשָׁלַיִם — זוֹכֶה וְרוֹאֶה בְּשִׂמְחָתָהּ.
"Whoever mourns Yerushalayim — merits and sees in her joy."

The pesukim of nechamah in Yeshayahu, read in the Sheva D'nechemta — the seven haftaros of consolation[2] that follow Tisha B'Av through the Yamim Nora'im — fulfill that promise with language overflowing with emunah, hope, and the assured restoration of Yerushalayim's grandeur. Their effect on the neshamah is immediate and deep: even in the darkest personal season, the nechamos of Yeshayahu lift and steady.

A Counsel from the Pre-War Yeshiva World

A mashgiach of Yeshivas Chachmei Lublin in the generation of Rav Meir Shapiro[3] would offer a consistent counsel to bachurim who came to him in the grip of despondency: open Yeshayahu and read the nechamos — the haftaros we recite in the weeks after Tisha B'Av. The nevuos of consolation are saturated with emunah and hope; they lift the neshamah directly, and the rest of life follows. This was a spiritual-halachic prescription, not a clinical one — and the prescription has helped many in every generation since.

II. The Linguistic Puzzle — Present Tense, Not Future

The grammatical anomaly in the Gemara's phrasing presses itself on any careful reader. Chazal say zoche v'roeh — "merits and sees" — in the present tense. They should have said yizkeh v'yireh — will merit and will see. Yerushalayim is not yet rebuilt. The Beis HaMikdash is not standing. We sit on the floor; we say kinos; we cry. In what sense are we, at this very moment, merit-ing and seeing the simchah?

This grammatical kashya is raised by all the mefarshim. Its classic resolution comes from the Chasam Sofer — Rav Moshe Sofer, Chief Rabbi of Bratislava in the early nineteenth century[4] — and it draws together a pasuk in Bereishis, the closing narrative of Maseches Makkos, and the inner mechanics of nechamah as understood by Chazal.

III. Vayema'en L'Hinachem — The Mechanism of Forgetting

The Chasam Sofer begins with a pasuk in Bereishis. The shevatim sell Yosef HaTzaddik, dip his kesones pasim in blood, and bring it to Yaakov Avinu. All his children gather to console him. The pasuk[5] records:

וַיָּקֻמוּ כָל בָּנָיו וְכָל בְּנֹתָיו לְנַחֲמוֹ, וַיְמָאֵן לְהִתְנַחֵם.
"And all his sons and daughters arose to console him, and he refused to be consoled."

The question forces itself: Yiddishkeit has a structured halachic process for grief — shivah, shloshim, yud-beis chodesh. After a year, the formal aveilus concludes. Endless bereavement is not the Jewish way. So what does it mean that Yaakov "refused to be consoled"? Was he rejecting the entire halachic framework of grief?

The Midrash's Answer

The Midrash, cited by Rashi on the pasuk, resolves the difficulty with a fundamental principle: "Ein gozrin gezeirah shel shichchah ela al ha'meis" — the divine decree of forgetting is issued only for one who has died. HaKadosh Baruch Hu built into human nature a mechanism that allows the wound of bereavement to gradually ease — but only when the person has in fact died. For someone still living, no such mechanism engages. There is no halachic pathway to nechamah for the living.

Yaakov Avinu refused consolation because, in the depths of his neshamah, he sensed that Yosef was still alive. Accepting nechamah would have been a betrayal — a declaration, in effect, that Yosef was dead. As the end of Bereishis reveals, his refusal was vindicated: father and son were reunited in the dramatic scenes that close the sefer.

A Mashal for Shivah. An analogy sometimes offered at shivah calls: when a parent is niftar, it is as if a ditch has been dug into the middle of your dining room. Every time you walk through, you fall in. Gradually, over shivah and shloshim and the year's end, you learn to walk around it. The ditch never disappears — the loss remains — but the mechanism of shichchah teaches you to navigate around it. That is nechamah. But it works only when the person is genuinely gone. For the living, no such navigation is possible — and no comfort should be sought.

IV. Application to Tziyon — Why Yerushalayim Cannot Be Forgotten

The Chasam Sofer applies the principle directly to Yerushalayim — and in doing so, answers the grammatical puzzle of zoche v'roeh. The argument runs as follows.

The gzeiras shikchah — the divine mechanism of forgetting — is hard-wired into human nature. When someone dies, time eventually allows the wound to scar over; we learn, as the mashal has it, to walk around the ditch. This is not disloyalty. It is the Creator's mercy, built into the structure of the psyche, because the dead are gone and the living must continue.

But Yerushalayim is not dead to Klal Yisrael. If she were, the gzeiras shikchah would have engaged within a generation or two of the Churban. The wound would have closed. The prayers would have faded. Yet here we are, nearly two thousand years later, sitting on the floor and reciting kinos. That act of mourning is itself decisive testimony: Yerushalayim lives in the heart of Klal Yisrael. Our covenant with her was never terminated. The Shechinah, the kedushah, the relationship — these were not annulled by the Churban; they were suspended, waiting.

And this is the precise logic of the present tense. A Jew who mourns Yerushalayim on Tisha B'Av is, at that very moment, in living relationship with her future simchah. The future joy is not somewhere distant in the calendar; it is the necessary consequence of a bond that was never severed. That bond is present. That certainty is present. And because the certainty is present, the simchah — in the form of inner sight, of aligned emunah — is also present. Zoche v'roeh: meriting and seeing, right now, on the floor, before Mashiach has come.

The Napoleon Legend

A legend circulates — attested in substance, if not in the famous Napoleonic framing, as far back as the writings of the Rema[6] — that a European ruler once encountered Jews sitting on the floor weeping in their synagogue and asked the reason. When told they were mourning a home destroyed seventeen hundred years earlier in Yerushalayim, the ruler is said to have replied: "A nation that still cries for something destroyed so long ago is guaranteed to recover it." Whether or not this exchange occurred as told, its logic is precisely the Chasam Sofer's yesod: tears for Yerushalayim are not the grief of the bereaved — they are the fidelity of one who refuses to declare a living covenant dead.

V. Rabbi Akiva on Har HaTzofim

The Chasam Sofer's yesod illuminates a famous puzzle in Maseches Makkos.[7] Roughly a century after the Churban, four Tannaim — Rabban Gamliel, Rabbi Elazar ben Azaryah, Rabbi Yehoshua, and Rabbi Akiva — ascended Har HaTzofim. They tore kriah. Continuing to Har HaBayis, they saw a fox emerge from where the Kodesh HaKodashim had stood — the most sacred chamber in creation, into which only the Kohen Gadol entered, once a year, on Yom Kippur.

The three Tannaim wept. Rabbi Akiva laughed. The exchange:

אָמְרוּ לוֹ: עֲקִיבָא, מִפְּנֵי מָה אַתָּה מְצַחֵק? אָמַר לָהֶם: מִפְּנֵי מָה אַתֶּם בּוֹכִים?
"They said: Akiva, why are you laughing? He said to them: Why are you weeping?"

Rabbi Akiva then explained: the nevuah of destruction and the nevuah of consolation are tlui zeh ba'zeh — bound together. If the prophecy of churban was fulfilled in stone and ash, the prophecy of nechamah is equally certain — "Old men and old women will yet sit in the streets of Yerushalayim, and the streets will be filled with children playing" (Zechariah 8:4–5).[8] The Tannaim answered: "Akiva, nichamtanu — nichamtanu" — you have consoled us.

Re-reading the Dialogue

On the surface, Rabbi Akiva's counter-question — "Why are you crying?" — appears to be a rhetorical deflection, a Jewish habit of answering a question with a question. But the Chasam Sofer's yesod reveals it as something more precise. Rabbi Akiva was not deflecting their grief; he was identifying its source. His words carry a hidden meaning: the reason I am laughing is precisely because you are crying.

The logic: Rabbi Akiva had ascended Har HaBayis and seen the devastation — a fox in the Kodesh HaKodashim. He also saw his colleagues tear their garments and weep. Those tears told him something about the state of Yerushalayim that the physical ruins could not: she is alive. Not dead. Not a chapter closed. If the mechanism of shichchah had engaged, there would be no tears — only the indifference with which one looks at a historical site. But they were not indifferent; they were devastated. Their devastation was the proof. Yerushalayim lives in your hearts, he was telling them, and therefore the nevuah of her future simchah — which requires a living Yerushalayim, a living covenantal relationship — is certain. Your weeping is my laughter's source.

When the three Tannaim heard this, they understood. Their tears, far from being a sign of hopelessness, were the very grounds of hope. "Akiva, nichamtanu" — and in that moment, they saw themselves differently: not as mourners of a dead city, but as faithful witnesses of a living one.

VI. The Inner Arc of Tisha B'Av

This yesod illuminates the structure of the day itself:

  • Morning — deep aveilus; kinos, sitting on the floor, no tefillin at Shacharis.
  • After chatzos — many communities ease certain customs;[10] one may sit on a chair; some communities have the custom of sweeping the house; a few paint the walls. These are not contradictions to the aveilus — they are the aveilus beginning to reveal its hidden interior of nechamah. The mourning has been real, full, and uncompromised; precisely because it was real, what it contains becomes visible after midday.
  • The day is called a moed — a yom tov.[9] The day of deepest mourning is bound, in its very nature, to assured joy. Faithful aveilus contains, hidden within, the seed of geulah.

VII. Jewish Memory — Never Forgotten, Never Finished

Daniel Goldhagen — a Harvard-trained scholar and son of a Holocaust survivor — wrote the seminal study Hitler's Willing Executioners (1996),[11] which documents in extensive detail how ordinary German civilians — not only the SS leadership — actively believed that exterminating Klal Yisrael was right and necessary. He compiled personal records, letters, and documented choices of rank-and-file soldiers and citizens who exerted their own initiative in perpetrating what was done to Jewish communities across Europe.

At a conference in Germany, a respected German professor posed a question — politely, but with a sharp edge: "With all due respect, why do you Jews not move on from the Holocaust? Eight decades on, books, documentaries, new initiatives. Are there not other concerns? Why this persistence?"

Goldhagen's answer, as retold in various accounts of the exchange,[11b] was quiet and complete:

"Thousands of years ago, a home burned in Yerushalayim. To this day, observant Jews mention that home roughly sixty times daily — through Shacharis, Minchah, Maariv, Birchas HaMazon, the entire siddur. If we have not moved on from a destruction that occurred two millennia ago, do you really expect us to forget what happened seventy years ago?"

This is not pathology. It is mesorah. Never again requires never forgotten. The Jew who has made peace with the absence of Yerushalayim — who has accepted nechamah on something still alive — is no longer misabel, and the entire structure of present-tense nechamah becomes unreachable for him. The persistence of Jewish memory, across all its categories and across all its centuries, is one continuous act of covenantal fidelity.

VIII. Rav Yisrael Meir Lau — The Testimony of Survival

Rav Yisrael Meir Lau, former Chief Rabbi of Eretz Yisrael,[12] is among the most powerful darshanim of the generation. At Neilah in Beit Knesset HaGadol on King George Street in Yerushalayim — an audience that included many who had come only for Neilah, who had not fasted, who wore paper yarmulkes borrowed at the door — he delivered a drashah that became a landmark in his long career of touching Jewish hearts.

Lulek

Rav Lau was born in 1937 in Piotrkow, Poland. His father, Rav Moshe Chaim Lau Hy"d, served as Chief Rabbi of Piotrkow. Rav Meir Shapiro — founder of Daf Yomi and Yeshivas Chachmei Lublin — was a cousin of the Lau family and had himself been rav of Piotrkow before moving to Lublin. Rav Lau's childhood nickname was Lulek.

When the Nazis arrived in Piotrkow, his father was killed almost immediately. His mother and his older brother Naftali remained with him, the father having charged Naftali: "Take care of Lulek." At the selection — when Lulek was four or five years old — his mother shoved him toward Naftali's side, toward the group being taken to labor. He cried and ran back; she pushed him away again. She called out to her older son: "Naftali, take care of Lulek." And to the child himself, her final words: "Goodbye, Lulik. Goodbye, Naftali." He did not understand until later that those violent shoves had saved his life.

"I Am Older Than You"

By the end of the war, the child was in Buchenwald. When the American forces arrived to liberate the camp, a Jewish chaplain in the U.S. Army — Rabbi Herschel Schacter[13] — walked among the dead. The child, frightened of the Americans, had hidden behind a pile of corpses. Rabbi Schacter later described what he saw:

"I looked at a pile of Yidden, and between the corpses I saw two eyes. Two eyes burning with fire. I walked closer and realized there was a small Jewish child hidden behind the bodies — an impossibility at that point in the war. I asked him: Wie alt bist du, mein Kind? How old are you, my child?"

The boy's answer: "I am older than you."

The chaplain, fifty-five years old, asked what the child could mean. The boy explained:

"You laugh and smile like a child — I saw you. I don't laugh anymore. I don't know how to laugh; I don't know how to smile after what I witnessed. And I no longer know how to cry. Ich bin shoyn oysgeveynt. I have cried out all my tears. Am I not older than you?"

The Suitcase

An American soldier gave the orphaned child a small empty suitcase — so that the boy should feel he owned something in the world. Lulek carried it from one orphanage to the next, from displaced-persons camp to Eretz Yisrael, into adulthood and marriage and his long career in the rabbinate.

His wife eventually asked him to discard it — it was old and worn. He refused: "At my age, this is all I had. When our children complain, I want to show them this suitcase." When they moved apartments in Tel Aviv, he stored the suitcase in the boidem — the attic storage. Years later, one of his children asked to see it. He climbed up and opened the storage space — and all that remained were the handles. The suitcase had disintegrated entirely.

The image carries its own meaning: the empty vessel of his orphaning had broken down — but the neshamah it had once anchored continued, unbroken, forward.

IX. Let the Letters Testify

The Midrash at the opening of Eichah Rabbah[14] describes the moment of the Churban. Avraham, Yitzchak, Yaakov, and Moshe Rabbeinu each plead before HaKadosh Baruch Hu for Klal Yisrael; their pleas are not accepted. The letters of the aleph-beis then come forward as witnesses against Klal Yisrael: Beis testifies that they did not honor the Torah that begins with it; Gimmel testifies about tzitzis; and so down the aleph-beis. Avraham Avinu rises to silence each letter as it approaches: "Hush — do not testify against my children." Only Rachel Imeinu's plea — invoking how she had given over the simanim to her sister Leah out of pure vatranus — finally moves HaKadosh Baruch Hu to promise:

מִנְעִי קוֹלֵךְ מִבֶּכִי וְעֵינַיִךְ מִדִּמְעָה כִּי יֵשׁ שָׂכָר לִפְעֻלָּתֵךְ... וְשָׁבוּ בָנִים לִגְבוּלָם.
"Withhold your voice from weeping and your eyes from tears, for there is reward for your effort... and the children shall return to their borders." (Yirmiyahu 31:15–16)

A New Reading — The Letters as Witnesses For

Rav Lau offered a bold inversion of this midrash, drawing on Parshas Vayelech:[16]

וְעַתָּה כִּתְבוּ לָכֶם אֶת הַשִּׁירָה הַזֹּאת... כִּי לֹא תִשָּׁכַח מִפִּי זַרְעוֹ.
"And now, write for yourselves this Song... for it shall not be forgotten from the mouth of his offspring." (Devarim 31:19, 21)

Moshe Rabbeinu writes that the Torah shall be a shirah — a Song — that testifies. The standard reading is that the Torah testifies against Klal Yisrael when they violate it. Rav Lau proposed the opposite: the Torah testifies for Klal Yisrael. Through the Crusades, through the expulsions, through the Holocaust — Yidden kept the Torah:

  • In the camps, they tore strips from their shirts to make peshilos (wicks), pressed them into button-bases filled with smuggled oil, and lit Chanukah lights.
  • They acquired flour and water by means hard to imagine and baked matzos in hiding.
  • They donned tefillin in barracks, whispered Kriyas Shema, kept tznius, said tehillim.

"Therefore," said Rav Lau before the assembled congregation at Neilah, "do not silence the letters. Let them come — aleph, beis, all of them — and testify how Klal Yisrael kept the Torah even in the deepest hester panim. The same tefillin we will put on this moment are the same tefillin our fathers wore through the worst of galus. The same Shema we will say is the same one our grandfathers whispered in the camps. That is the testimony the letters must give."

The congregation wept openly. What keeps a nation alive is not geography but covenant — not a location but a shirah that cannot be forgotten because it has been carried, letter by letter, through every darkness.

X. Synthesis

What sustains Klal Yisrael is not Eretz Yisrael alone, nor a geographic location, but Torah and mitzvos. The Torah does not die — and so a nation that holds the Torah does not die. Yerushalayim, the Beis HaMikdash, the Avodah, the Kohanim and Levi'im — these are folded into the Torah-life of the people who refuse to let go, who refuse the false comfort of declaring a living covenant dead.

To sit on the floor on Tisha B'Av and weep — to refuse nechamah, to insist that what seems gone is not truly gone — is itself the greatest nechamah. The aveilus is the witness. The witness is the merit. The merit is the present-tense simchah. Zoche v'roeh b'simchasah: at this very moment, on the afternoon of Tisha B'Av, before Mashiach has come — the one who mourns faithfully already merits and already sees.

May the tikkun ha'shaleim of Mashiach and geulah sheleimah arrive bimheirah b'yameinu, amen.

Eichah Rabbah, Pesichta 24. — The Midrash's elaborate account of the Churban: the Avos and Moshe Rabbeinu pleading before HaKadosh Baruch Hu; the letters of the aleph-beis testifying against Klal Yisrael and being silenced by Avraham Avinu; and the climactic plea of Rachel Imeinu, whose vatranus on the simanim is finally accepted. The pesichta numbering follows the standard Vilna edition; verify against the Albeck critical edition, where proem numbering may differ slightly. ↩
  16. Yirmiyahu 31:15–16. — The Divine response to Rachel: מִנְעִי קוֹלֵךְ מִבֶּכִי וְעֵינַיִךְ מִדִּמְעָה כִּי יֵשׁ שָׂכָר לִפְעֻלָּתֵךְ... וְשָׁבוּ בָנִים לִגְבוּלָם. The pasuk on which the midrashic conclusion of the pesichta rests, and which Rav Lau's drashah reads in conversation with Devarim 31. ↩
  17. Devarim 31:19, 21. — וְעַתָּה כִּתְבוּ לָכֶם אֶת הַשִּׁירָה הַזֹּאת... כִּי לֹא תִשָּׁכַח מִפִּי זַרְעוֹ. Moshe Rabbeinu's command to write the Torah as testimony. The standard reading is that the Torah testifies against Klal Yisrael in their failures. Rav Lau's reading — that it testifies for them in their fidelity through the darkest periods — is a creative homiletical inversion presented as a Neilah insight; its relationship to earlier sources should be explored. ↩

Endnote A: The Mashgiach of Chachmei Lublin (fn3)
The oral tradition cites a figure identified as "Reb Shmelke of Zalachov," mashgiach of Yeshivas Chachmei Lublin under Rav Meir Shapiro, who counseled depressed bachurim to read the nechamos of Yeshayahu. The surname "Rozovsky," which appeared in earlier versions of this write-up, has been removed: Reb Shmuel Rozovsky is universally identified as the Rosh Yeshiva of Ponevezh (1913–1979), unrelated to Chachmei Lublin and not from Zalachov. The precise identity of the Zalachov figure — including whether a figure of that name and role is documented in the published literature on Chachmei Lublin — requires verification before any name is fixed in print. Readers with knowledge of relevant sources are invited to write to the editors. Until identification is confirmed, the text cites the counsel as a tradition from the Chachmei Lublin circle, without specifying the name.
Endnote B: Rabbi Herschel Schacter at Buchenwald (fn13)
Rabbi Herschel Schacter (1917–2013) served as a U.S. Army chaplain and was present at the liberation of Buchenwald on April 11, 1945. His account of finding the young Lulek (Rav Yisrael Meir Lau) hiding behind a pile of corpses is preserved in recorded testimony (including a recorded interview held by the USC Shoah Foundation and related archives) and is corroborated by Rav Lau's own account in his autobiography Out of the Depths (Hebrew: Al Tishlach Yadekhah el HaNa'ar; English translation published by Doubleday, 2011). Rabbi Schacter is not to be confused with Rabbi Hershel Schachter of Yeshiva University, a different figure of the same generation. The chaplain's son, Rabbi Jacob J. Schacter, has spoken and written publicly about his father's account of this encounter.
