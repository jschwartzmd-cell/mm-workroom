Closing the Amidah: Yihyu Leratzon, Elokai Netzor, and the Pressburg Controversy
Studies in Tefillah (תפילה) — The Close of Shemoneh Esrei
27 Tishrei 5786  ·  Sefer Edition

This discourse was delivered לעילוי נשמת ר׳ חיים יהודה בן מאיר ווילינגער ז״ל, a survivor of the war and of the death march, of the city of Selish, who left behind a beautiful family. It was sponsored by Reb Yitzchak and Mrs. Paley, his daughter. The rav of Selish was the author of a sefer entitled Tzeror HaChaim (צרור החיים): נשמתו תהא צרורה בצרור החיים. [Names, spelling of the surname and of the town to be confirmed before printing.]

This discourse takes up what follows the blessing of peace at the close of Shemoneh Esrei (שמונה עשרה): the two endings of that blessing, Oseh HaShalom (עושה השלום) and HaMevarech es Amo Yisrael BaShalom (המברך את עמו ישראל בשלום); the controversy they provoked in Pressburg (פרשבורג), as recounted in the book Chut HaMeshulash (חוט המשולש); and the verse Yihyu Leratzon (יהיו לרצון), with the question whether it is said before or after Elokai Netzor (אלהי נצור).

It then turns to the history of Elokai Netzor (אלהי נצור) and to its author, Mar bereih d’Ravina (מר בריה דרבינא), whose miracles and whose breaking of a glass at his son’s wedding the Gemara (גמרא) relates, and who is the source of the custom of the chuppah (חופה); and to the inner meaning of Yihyu Leratzon (יהיו לרצון), which asks acceptance for words said without kavanah (כוונה) and for thoughts that words cannot hold.

It closes with the plea against lashon hara (לשון הרע) with which the added prayer begins; with judgment, sechar ve’onesh (שכר ועונש) and the identification of the soul by its name; and with the segulah (סגולה) of the verse that begins and ends with the letters of one’s name, traced to its earliest printed sources.

I. The Two Endings of the Blessing of Peace

The Question of Text

The blessing of peace, with which Shemoneh Esrei (שמונה עשרה) ends, closes in most siddurim (סידורים) with the words ברוך אתה ה׳ המברך את עמו ישראל בשלום. Nusach Ashkenaz (נוסח אשכנז) reads עושה השלום in its place during Aseres Yemei Teshuvah (עשרת ימי תשובה). The siddurim differ in detail, and the related question of Sim Shalom (שים שלום) against Shalom Rav (שלום רב) is reserved for the treatment of Maariv (מעריב).

It is reported in Chut HaMeshulash (חוט המשולש) that in earlier times Nusach Ashkenaz said Oseh HaShalom (עושה השלום) throughout the year, and that the practice was later confined to the ten days.[1]

Why in the Ten Days of Repentance

The reason offered is that the gematria (גימטריא) of השלום equals that of Safriel (ספריאל), the angel who writes and records the decree of inscription and sealing for a good year. During the ten days, when the decree is being written, mercy is sought through that angel.[2] The question arises how one may address angels at all. The answer, given in an earlier discourse, is that the prayer is not addressed to them: when machnisei rachamim (מכניסי רחמים) is said, the angels are asked only to bring the petitions before God.

The matter carries its own irony. The numerical value of עושה is likewise that of השלום, so that the same angel is invoked by the single word עושה. The allusions carried by each of the two endings, said to call upon different angels and different mercies, were treated at length in an earlier discourse of this series and are not repeated here. What is to be observed is how Jewish controversy turns ironic: when Oseh HaShalom was said all the year round there was a real dispute over which ending was correct, and different siddurim printed it differently.

II. The Book Chut HaMeshulash

The book Chut HaMeshulash is a biography of three generations: Rav Akiva Eiger (רבי עקיבא איגר), the Chasam Sofer (חתם סופר), and the Ksav Sofer (כתב סופר). It was written, as reported, by a grandson of the Chasam Sofer,[3] and every devotee of the Chasam Sofer should have it at home. Rav Akiva Eiger was the father-in-law of the Chasam Sofer: as the lecturer tells it, the first wife of the Chasam Sofer was childless for many years, and he afterward married the daughter of Rav Akiva Eiger. The title is the verse והחוט המשולש לא במהרה ינתק (Koheles, קהלת 4:12), which the Gemara in Kesubos (כתובות) applies to three consecutive generations of Torah (תורה): once a family has three generations of talmidei chachamim (תלמידי חכמים), the Torah does not depart from it.[4]

The family of the Chasam Sofer bears the verse out to the present day: his children, grandchildren and great-grandchildren are great rabbis, and they number in the thousands. In the generations after him each gave his own work a title containing the name סופר: the Chasam Sofer wrote the חתם סופר; his son, the כתב סופר; the son of the Ksav Sofer, the Shevet Sofer (שבט סופר); and his son, the Da’as Sofer (דעת סופר). One of them, the lecturer relates, was the first of the family to settle in Eretz Yisrael (ארץ ישראל).[5]

III. The Controversy in Pressburg

Pressburg and Its Text of the Prayer

Pressburg (פרשבורג) was always a city apart. It was a city of Oberlander Jews (אובערלענדער), who prayed Nusach Ashkenaz. But after the Baal Shem Tov (בעל שם טוב) and the rise of Chassidus there was an upheaval over the text of the prayer. The Chasam Sofer and others changed certain things, and many persons moved to Nusach Sefard (נוסח ספרד); so that much of the dispute between the Misnagdim (מתנגדים) and the Chassidim (חסידים) was about the text of the prayers and the manner of praying. Why the community of Pressburg shifted its text as it did is a question left open here.

The Chassidic Revolution

The Chassidim arose in the eighteenth century and changed the way Jews prayed in two respects.

The first was bodily. The Vilna Gaon (הגאון מווילנא) and his disciples placed the Chassidim under cherem (חרם) — a subject on which it is customary to speak with delicacy, and one that Chassidic usage softens in speech. Among the principal charges, set out in the text of the cherem, was that in prayer they appeared as madmen: they leaped up and down, rolled on the floor and called out.[6] Swaying in prayer, as in study, is itself an established practice, grounded in the Zohar HaKadosh (זוהר הקדוש) and in the sefarim; but the Chassidim carried it further, to rolling and leaping and wild movements of the hands, and in the view of the Gaon the decorum of the synagogue became that of a circus.

The second was the text of the prayer itself. They changed it, and the responsa literature of the poskim (פוסקים) of that period is full of sharp objections to the Chassidim on that account.

In practice, however, the Chassidim prevailed. It is reported of the Chofetz Chaim (חפץ חיים) that toward the end of his life he said that, had it not been for the Baal Shem Tov and the Chassidic movement, the maskilim (משכילים) would have taken over Klal Yisrael (כלל ישראל); the warmth was needed.[7] That warmth came at a price: a change in the decorum of the synagogue, a change in the text of the prayers, and the introduction of many customs based on Kabbalah (קבלה) that had not been part of ordinary Jewish practice. Yet the outcome lies with God, who knows what He does. Nor is the other side of the argument without worth; it is a loss that genuine Misnagdim are now hardly to be found.

The Nusach HaAri (נוסח האר״י), the text used by Chabad, expresses the same decision to bring matters drawn from Kabbalah into the text of the prayer.

The Split Over the Blessing of Peace

In Pressburg the community divided over precisely this blessing. One party said Oseh HaShalom; the other said HaMevarech es Amo Yisrael BaShalom (המברך את עמו ישראל בשלום). The dispute grew so sharp in the affairs of the community that those who said Oseh HaShalom were nicknamed the Oseh-niks (עושה׳ניקס) and the others the Hamevarech-niks (המברך׳ניקס).

This account is found, according to the lecturer, only in Chut HaMeshulash; he reports that he has found it nowhere else.[8] Such disputes commonly begin in the synagogue, and later divisions within Chassidic courts have followed the same pattern.

The irony is plain. The blessing of peace became the source of two names for two factions at war with each other. Peace is all that is asked for, and over its blessing they fought; and the two endings, as noted, are the same in meaning.

IV. Yihyu Leratzon: Before, After, or Both?

The Question

After the blessing of peace and before Elokai Netzor, a verse is said: יהיו לרצון אמרי פי והגיון לבי לפניך ה׳ צורי וגואלי (Tehillim, תהלים 19:15). Is it said before Elokai Netzor, after it, or both? The siddur (סידור) prints it in both places.

What does the verse mean? That what the worshipper has said, the words of his mouth and also the thoughts of his heart, be accepted before God. But the grammar is open. The verb may point forward, to what he is about to say in the coming minutes of the prayer, or backward, to what he has already said. The Gemara in Berachos (ברכות) therefore asks: who told us to say it at the end of Shemoneh Esrei? Perhaps, like Hashem Sefasai Tiftach (ה׳ שפתי תפתח) at the beginning, it should be said at the beginning.[9]

The answer turns on what is meant by tefillah (תפילה). In the strict sense tefillah is Shemoneh Esrei; Kerias Shema (קריאת שמע) is not tefillah, though all is gathered together under the name of prayer. One may not interpose between geulah (גאולה) and tefillah; yet Hashem Sefasai Tiftach, said at the beginning, is not regarded as an interruption, for the Sages instituted it as part of the prayer itself. So too Yihyu Leratzon at the end is part of Shemoneh Esrei.

Following Dovid HaMelech

The Sages follow Dovid HaMelech (דוד המלך), especially in Shemoneh Esrei. Each blessing of the prayer corresponds to chapters of Tehillim, in the order in which Dovid set his petitions, and after eighteen chapters Dovid says יהיו לרצון אמרי פי והגיון לבי לפניך. It must therefore come at the end. Strictly it follows nineteen chapters, not eighteen; but the first two chapters of Tehillim are counted as one in the reckoning of the Gemara, and the prayer too has nineteen blessings once Birkas HaMinim (ברכת המינים) is counted.

The Dispute of the Rishonim

The Rishonim (ראשונים) differ over where the verse stands in relation to Elokai Netzor. The Rabad (ראב״ד), as cited by the Rashba (רשב״א), holds that Yihyu Leratzon must be said immediately after the eighteen blessings, as Dovid said it immediately after the eighteen chapters; that is, before Elokai Netzor, since Elokai Netzor is not part of Shemoneh Esrei.[10]

Against this the Vilna Gaon objects, in the position attributed to him. If Yihyu Leratzon is said first, Elokai Netzor is excluded from the prayer; the worshipper no longer stands in Shemoneh Esrei before God, as though he had stepped back. Yet Elokai Netzor is a prayer one wishes to say as part of Shemoneh Esrei. Is it part of the prayer or not? The solution attributed to the Gra (גר״א) is to place Elokai Netzor immediately after המברך את עמו ישראל בשלום, while still within the prayer, and to say Yihyu Leratzon only afterward.[11] The Rema (רמ״א) in the Shulchan Aruch (שולחן ערוך) likewise records the order in which Elokai Netzor precedes the verse.[12] The Mishnah Berurah (משנה ברורה) rules that the better practice is to say Yihyu Leratzon both before and after the supplications: before, because the verse is to be joined to Shemoneh Esrei; after, to satisfy the Gra. This arrangement is a case of terei desisrei (תרתי דסתרי), as has been remarked, and does not wholly cohere.[13]

A Standing Question at Minchah of Yom Kippur

A question arises among the Acharonim (אחרונים) at Minchah (מנחה) of Yom Kippur: is Al Chet (על חטא) said before Elokai Netzor or after it? The vidui (וידוי) must be part of the prayer and cannot follow Shemoneh Esrei, yet Elokai Netzor and the verse stand at its close. The Acharonim differ as to what should be done. The practice commonly given is to follow what is printed in one’s machzor (מחזור); there is on whom to rely either way (yesh al mi lismoch, יש על מי לסמוך).[14]

V. Elokai Netzor: A Late Addition

Elokai Netzor is not from Anshei Knesses HaGedolah (אנשי כנסת הגדולה); it comes from much later generations. In the period of the Geonim (גאונים) and the Rishonim not many said it, and the Avudraham (אבודרהם), among the principal commentators on the prayers, writes that it was said by yechidim (יחידים) alone.[15] Today it stands in every siddur and is recited three times a day as though it belonged to the prayer, but it was not always so. The early siddurim do not all include Yihyu Leratzon, and not all the Geonim and Rishonim are known to have said it; for some reason the practice was adopted.

Its source is a passage of the Gemara in Berachos concerning an amora (אמורא) named Mar bereih d’Ravina, son of Ravina.[16] The lecturer distinguishes him from the Ravina of the pair Ravina and Rav Ashi and places him earlier.[17]

VI. Mar bereih d’Ravina: Miracles and Customs

The Wall at Worms and the Wall at Mechoza

Visitors to Worms are shown, beside the beis midrash (בית מדרש) of Rashi (רש״י), an indentation in the wall of a very narrow alley. According to the legend, when the mother of Rashi was carrying him, a knight on horseback came riding through the alley; she would have been trampled, and there would have been no Rashi on the Torah and on the Talmud. God wrought a nes (נס): the wall opened, she pressed herself into it, the knight passed, and Rashi was born. The historian who pointed the place out mocked the tale, and showed by evidence that it never happened.[18] Whence, then, did such a story come? From the Gemara; not of Rashi, but of Mar bereih d’Ravina.

The Gemara in Berachos 54a relates that Mar bereih d’Ravina was walking in the marketplace of Mechoza (מחוזא), a place in Babylonia, in what is now Iraq,[19] when a wild camel charged at him. The wall cracked open behind him; he went inside it and was saved. Earlier, thirsty in the valley of the ערבות, a spring had been created for him. At each place he afterward recited a blessing naming both miracles, and the Gemara concludes that even a miracle done for an individual obligates him to bless.[20] Unlike the legend of Worms, the account in the Gemara is not legend: the words of Chazal (חז״ל) are true.

The familiar form of the blessing for an individual, ברוך שעשה לי נס במקום הזה, is given in the same passage, in the name of Rava, to a man saved from a lion; the Mishnah (משנה) gives the form for a place where miracles were done for Israel, ברוך שעשה נסים לאבותינו במקום הזה. Mar bereih d’Ravina is spoken of as מלומד בנסים, one to whom miracles happen continually.[21]

The Origin of Breaking the Glass at the Chuppah

Mar bereih d’Ravina is also the source of the custom of breaking a glass under the chuppah. The Gemara in Berachos relates that he made a wedding for his son and saw the Sages exceedingly merry, more than he could bear; the joy had passed into frivolity (הוללות). He brought a cup worth four hundred זוזי and broke it before them, and they were saddened. The frivolity vanished, and they understood that it had gone too far.[22] From this passage Tosafos (תוספות) derive the custom of breaking glass at weddings, and later authorities give it the added meaning of zecher lechurban (זכר לחורבן): אם אשכחך ירושלים תשכח ימיני (Tehillim 137:5) — that even at the height of joy Jerusalem is not forgotten. May all merit to break glass at the weddings of their children and grandchildren.

VII. The Prayer of Mar bereih d’Ravina

The Gemara relates that Mar bereih d’Ravina, at the close of his Shemoneh Esrei, added a prayer of his own. This is itself a chiddush (חידוש), for nothing may be added to the matbea shetavu Chachamim (מטבע שטבעו חכמים) fixed by Anshei Knesses HaGedolah, the last generation of the prophets, with Shimon HaTzaddik (שמעון הצדיק) and persons from centuries before him. The line that tradition draws is that no further Berachos may be added to the prayer, while personal petitions in one’s own words may be added after it.[23] Mar bereih d’Ravina did so: אלהי נצור לשוני מרע ושפתי מדבר מרמה.

In the text of the Gemara the prayer continues: וכל החושבים עלי רעה מהרה הפר עצתם וקלקל מחשבתם, and asks to be saved from an evil mishap, from the evil inclination, and from a bad woman.[24] The text printed in our siddurim differs from that of the Gemara, and the clause about the bad woman is lacking; yet the worshipper is to have the original sense in mind.

Whoever says Yihyu Leratzon has sealed Shemoneh Esrei; it is as though he had stepped back. Now he may add whatever he wishes. Were it not for the prayer of Mar bereih d’Ravina, the three steps back would be taken immediately after Yihyu Leratzon; since his prayer was adopted, the feet are kept together, Elokai Netzor is said, and only then are the three steps taken.

Why this prayer, of all the personal prayers of the amoraim (אמוראים) recorded in the Gemara, became the standard one is a question the Gemara does not answer; it was reserved for the following discourse.[25]

VIII. The Plea Against Lashon Hara

Of all the transgressions, why does the prayer ask specifically for protection from lashon hara? We say thank You for everything, and then: guard my tongue from evil today. The commentators on the prayers (מפרשי התפילה) explain that this is a sign of how pervasive, and how grave, the transgression is. We do not realize what we are saying: sometimes, even while taking the three steps back, a person is already speaking lashon hara to the one beside him.

The Gemara teaches that no one should say “I am a righteous man”; everyone stumbles in lashon hara, if not in the thing itself then in avak lashon hara (אבק לשון הרע).[26] A Jew prays: I know that I am about to go out into the world; I have just finished Shemoneh Esrei and spoken with You; let the words that leave my mouth be holy. That the first thing asked is protection from lashon hara teaches the gravity of the prohibition.

IX. Two Intentions in Yihyu Leratzon

Acceptance of Words Without kavanah

No one has kavanah for every word of Shemoneh Esrei unless he stands at a very high level. To say every word is difficult enough; to have the thought for every word is harder still. In saying Yihyu Leratzon the worshipper asks that the words he said be accepted, even if empty of kavanah: אמרי פי; and the thoughts in which there was kavanah, והגיון לבי, likewise. It is an appeal for mercy at the end of the prayer.

The verse אלה ברכב ואלה בסוסים ואנחנו בשם ה׳ אלהינו נזכיר (Tehillim 20:8) says that the nations have chariots and horses, a military, and Israel has the Name of God.[27] The world makes a joke of it. When a man of the nations wants to travel to Hong Kong, he must book a ticket and arrange a vehicle; when a Jew wants to travel to Hong Kong, he need only take three steps back, three steps forward, and begin Shemoneh Esrei: his mind is already there. The joke makes a point. We ask that even if our minds wandered, the words, the going through the motions, be accepted. It is lip service, indeed, but we are weak.

The Radak (רד״ק): Thoughts Beyond Words

The Radak on the verse יהיו לרצון teaches that, besides the words spoken, there are thoughts and intentions in the heart that were never voiced.[28] A person often wishes to pray for something, has it all worked out in his mind, and lacks the words. At this point in the prayer one is meant to use one’s own words, and the poskim say that one may add whatever one wishes to Elokai Netzor: a match for a child, a livelihood. But some thoughts cannot be brought out. To these the worshipper says: You are yodea machshavos (יודע מחשבות); You know what is in my heart; read my thoughts as if I had said them. That too is an intention a Jew may bring to Yihyu Leratzon: I do not know how to say it, but You know what I want; fulfil my wish and accept my prayer so.

X. When to Lengthen

Where is it better to dwell on one’s petitions: at Shomea Tefillah (שומע תפילה) within Shemoneh Esrei, or after Yihyu Leratzon? The poskim rule that it is better after Yihyu Leratzon. If one lengthens his petitions within the Amidah (עמידה), the shaliach tzibbur (שליח ציבור) will begin the chazaras hashatz (חזרת הש״ץ), and he will miss Kaddish (קדיש), Kedushah (קדושה) and the responses of Amen (אמן). After Yihyu Leratzon the prayer is complete, and one is permitted to interrupt: to answer Amen, Kaddish and Kedushah; and one may go on and ask for whatever one wishes during Elokai Netzor.[29]

XI. Judgment and the Name

Reward and Punishment

There is a tradition — the lecturer calls it a legend, since its source in Chazal is not known — that in the World to Come God will judge, and the wicked will be punished. sechar ve’onesh is real. Whatever the temptation to soften the matter, to tell children that God loves them and everything is good, everything is not good. One who performs mitzvos (מצוות) is rewarded; one who commits transgressions is punished. This must not be forgotten, even in the present day. teshuvah (תשובה) is real, and after Yom Kippur one is clean; but a sin carries its punishment.

When a wicked man comes to heaven, the sefarim say, they will strike him. The descriptions of Gehinnom (גיהנום), of its fires, and of the angels of destruction surrounding the soul, are such that one with any fear of Heaven should read them with care. The language is of physical blows, although the soul is spiritual, and how a soul receives physical punishment is left to the imagery. But the picture is grim.

The Name as the Identity of the Soul

In this world a person is identified by his appearance, his work, his career, and also by his name. In the Olam HaEmes (עולם האמת) the soul is identified by one thing alone: its name. For this reason it matters to call a Jew by his Hebrew name, his true name, and not only by the English name that he uses in business. Many great men opposed the use of English names; today necessity has prevailed. It is a familiar fact that the Hungarian Jews who came after the war took English names, though in Hungary they had borne Hungarian ones. The Hebrew name is the identity of the neshamah (נשמה). The Arizal (אריז״ל) teaches that when a father names his son at the bris milah (ברית מילה), it is ruach hakodesh (רוח הקודש): the name is a heavenly matter, the means by which God, as it were, identifies souls.[30]

By the Way — Waking by the Hebrew Name

To wake someone, a child for example, when alarm clocks, water and lights do not help, call him by his Hebrew name. One then speaks to the neshamah and not to the body; the neshamah departs from the body in sleep, and calling the name invites it to return.[31]

It is said further that when the wicked soul is being judged and struck, it will be asked its name, and will forget it. A whole life a man walks about with a name, and at that moment he forgets it. The Shelah (של״ה) is cited for this, though the source of the account is not known. To remember one’s name there is a segulah: at the end of Shemoneh Esrei say a verse that begins with the first letter of one’s name and ends with its last letter. A man named Yisrael would say a verse that begins with a yud (י) and ends with a lamed (ל).[32]

XII. The Verse of One’s Name: Its Sources

The origin of the custom is obscure. In studying Tanach (תנ״ך) with the commentary of Rashi one meets, here and there, passages printed in parentheses. Some are by Rashi; some are a different interpretation (פירוש אחר); who wrote them is not known. Shem Ephraim (שם אפרים), Ephraim Zalman Margolioth of Brody, one of the most prolific authors of his age, wrote with great sharpness in his commentary on Rashi to the Prophets and Writings (on Shoftim, chapter 20) against the printers and publishers who added material to Rashi without attribution, so that the world thinks Rashi said what he never said.[33] He did not succeed in his protest: the parentheses remain in every edition of Tanach to this day.

One such parenthetical addition, in Rashi on the book of Michah (מיכה), one of the Trei Asar (תרי עשר), is the earliest known reference to the verse of one’s name.[34] It is a later addition, not Rashi’s. It says that whoever recites daily a verse that begins and ends as his name does, the Torah saves him from Gehinnom. It does not say that the verse is recited in prayer.

The first appearance of the practice in a siddur, as part of the prayer, is in Amsterdam in 1699. From then on the printers included it universally; why not? Everyone wants an additional merit against Gehinnom. The Yaavetz (יעב״ץ), Rabbi Yaakov Emden, brings the custom in his siddur, and after 1699 it appears in nearly all siddurim.[35]

The idea that a person’s name is his neshamah is cited also from the Or HaChaim (אור החיים), in Parshas Haazinu.[36] By saying the verse of his name a man invokes his neshamah, and the power of the Torah in repeating the verse protects him.

The custom does not, then, originate with the Shelah, though he is its best-known advocate. It is a beautiful custom, and it is to be encouraged.

XIII. Names from the Tanach

Which biblical names are fitting to give a child? Nimrod (נמרוד) is a popular name in Eretz Yisrael, yet he is an infamous figure in Tanach. Yishmael (ישמעאל) is also a name in Tanach, and there are Jews named Yishmael; there was also Yishmael the Kohen Gadol (כהן גדול).[37] It is a sorrow that persons take biblical names without understanding them; yet one must judge Jews favorably. Many simply do not know the biblical context; they hear a name that sounds biblical and pleasant and use it. There is a story of a mohel (מוהל) who refused to perform a bris milah when the parents wished to name the child Nimrod, precisely because of that figure’s reputation in Tanach.

XIV. Closing

May all merit to pray to God so that all their prayers are accepted, and to guard themselves from lashon hara.

The discourse closed with the Mishnah: רבי חנניא בן עקשיא אומר: רצה הקדוש ברוך הוא לזכות את ישראל, לפיכך הרבה להם תורה ומצוות, שנאמר: ה׳ חפץ למען צדקו יגדיל תורה ויאדיר.[38] The treatment of Elokai Netzor itself continues in the following discourse.

Each note gives the source first, then the report or elaboration. Where a statement is the lecturer’s report, the note says so.

  1. חוט המשולש — the three-generation biography of the family of the חתם סופר. The book is not in the text consulted, and the details given here rest on the lecturer. The lecturer reports that it records the earlier practice of נוסח אשכנז to say עושה השלום throughout the year. The author’s name and dates have not been established (Open Questions, no. 1). ↩
  2. The גימטריא of השלום and of ספריאל. Each equals 381 (השלום: 5+30+300+30+6+40; ספריאל: 60+80+200+10+1+30). The identification of ספריאל as the angel who writes the decree is the lecturer’s report of a source not located; the lecturer also states that עושה has the same value. The question of addressing angels is treated in an earlier discourse of the series. ↩
  3. The authorship of חוט המשולש. As reported by the lecturer, a grandson of the חתם סופר. Not verified; see Open Questions, no. 1. ↩
  4. קהלת ד׳:י״ב; כתובות ס״ב ע״ב; cf. בבא מציעא פ״ה ע״א. והחוט המשולש לא במהרה ינתק. In כתובות ס״ב ע״ב Rami bar Chama cites the verse of Rabbi Oshaya bar Rabbi Chama bar Bisa, a third generation of Torah scholars in his family. A related teaching, בבא מציעא פ״ה ע״א, states in the name of Rabbi Yochanan that when a scholar, his son and his grandson are scholars, the Torah does not depart from his seed (ישעיה נ״ט:כ״א). ↩
  5. The series of titles containing סופר. חתם סופר, כתב סופר, שבט סופר, דעת סופר, as the lecturer lists them. The lecturer did not name the member of the family who first settled in ארץ ישראל; the identification is left open (Open Questions, no. 2). ↩
  6. The charges in the חרם against the חסידים. The lecturer’s description of the content of the texts of the ban; the passages have not been cited from the texts themselves here (Open Questions, no. 3). ↩
  7. The remark of the חפץ חיים. Reported by the lecturer; the source of the remark has not been located (Open Questions, no. 4). ↩
  8. The Pressburg controversy over עושה השלום. Reported by the lecturer from חוט המשולש, and according to him found nowhere else. The account has not been independently confirmed; no source outside that book is cited here. ↩
  9. ברכות ד׳ ע״ב and ברכות ט׳ ע״ב; תהלים נ״א:י״ז, י״ט:ט״ו. Rabbi Yochanan requires ה׳ שפתי תפתח (תהלים נ״א:י״ז) before the prayer and יהיו לרצון (תהלים י״ט:ט״ו) after it (ד׳ ע״ב). In ט׳ ע״ב the Gemara asks why יהיו לרצון is placed at the end, answers in the name of Rabbi Yehudah bar Rabbi Shimon ben Pazi that David said it after eighteen chapters of Tehillim, and resolves the count of nineteen by counting chapters one and two as one. The same passage treats ה׳ שפתי תפתח as an extension of the prayer, so that it is no interruption between redemption and prayer. ↩
  10. רשב״א, ברכות י״ז ע״א, citing the ראב״ד. The Rashba on ברכות י״ז ע״א (on the prayer of מר בריה דרבינא) cites the Rabad, who wrote that יהיו לרצון is not to be said after those supplications but before them, immediately after the eighteen blessings, as David said it after eighteen psalms. The lecturer ascribed the position to the Rashba himself; in the comment as available it is the Rabad’s, and the Rashba’s own conclusion has not been established. ↩
  11. ביאור הגר״א, אורח חיים קכ״ב. The lecturer reports that the Gaon of Vilna objects to the position of the Rabad and places אלהי נצור within the Amidah, with יהיו לרצון only after. The Biur HaGra on אורח חיים קכ״ב, as available in the text consulted, treats only יהיו לרצון and does not mention אלהי נצור; the Gaon’s position as reported rests on tradition and has not been verified (Open Questions, no. 5). ↩
  12. רמ״א, שלחן ערוך אורח חיים קכ״ב:א׳. The gloss of the Rema records the custom, in some places, of interrupting with אלהי נצור before יהיו לרצון, and therefore of interrupting also for קדיש and קדושה. ↩
  13. משנה ברורה, אורח חיים קכ״ב, ס״ק ג׳. The Mishnah Berurah states וטוב לומר יהיו לרצון קודם התחנונים ואחריהם — it is good to say it both before the supplications and after them. The explanation that the first recital satisfies the Rabad and the second the Gaon, and the description of the arrangement as תרתי דסתרי, are the lecturer’s; neither was located in the Mishnah Berurah. ↩
  14. The question at מנחה of יום כיפור. The lecturer’s account; the discussion among the later authorities has not been cited here (Open Questions, no. 6). ↩
  15. אבודרהם, on the close of the Amidah. Rabbi David Abudarham (Seville, fourteenth century), author of the classic commentary on the prayers. As the lecturer reports, he records that אלהי נצור was originally said by individuals. The exact location in the printed text has not been verified. ↩
  16. ברכות י״ז ע״א. The prayer of מר בריה דרבינא follows those of Rabbi Alexandri and Rava, and precedes that of Rav Sheshet. It begins אלהי נצור לשוני מרע ושפתי מדבר מרמה and ends with יהיו לרצון אמרי פי והגיון לבי לפניך ה׳ צורי וגואלי. ↩
  17. The chronology of מר בריה דרבינא. The lecturer’s placement of him earlier than the Ravina of Ravina and Rav Ashi; the matter has not been verified. ↩
  18. The legend of the wall at Worms. Related by the lecturer, who adds that a historian shows the story to be without basis. No source for the legend is cited here. ↩
  19. The location of מחוזא. The lecturer placed it near Mosul. The usual identification is a Babylonian Jewish center on the Tigris near Ctesiphon, south of modern Baghdad (Open Questions, no. 7). ↩
  20. ברכות נ״ד ע״א; משנה ברכות ט׳:א׳. The Mishnah: הרואה מקום שנעשו בו נסים לישראל אומר ברוך שעשה נסים לאבותינו במקום הזה. In the Gemara (נ״ד ע״א) Rava tells a man saved from a lion to say, whenever he comes to that place, ברוך שעשה לי נס במקום הזה. Of מר בריה דרבינא two miracles are told: a spring created for him in the valley of the ערבות when he was thirsty, and, in מחוזא, a wild camel (גמלא פריצא) attacked him and the wall cracked open and he entered it. He blessed at each place naming both miracles, and the Gemara concludes that a miracle done for an individual obligates him to bless. ↩
  21. The epithet מלומד בנסים. Not located in the passage of ברכות נ״ד ע״א; the epithet is the lecturer’s (Open Questions, no. 8). ↩
  22. ברכות ל׳ ע״ב – ל״א ע״א; תוספות; רמ״א, אורח חיים תקס:ב׳; אבן העזר ס״ה:ג׳. מר בריה דרבינא made a wedding feast for his son, saw the Sages excessively joyous, brought a cup worth four hundred זוזי and broke it before them, and they were saddened; Rav Ashi did likewise with a cup of white glass. Tosafos on the latter: מכאן נהגו לשבר זכוכית בנשואין. The reason of remembrance of the destruction is not in Tosafos: the Shulchan Aruch אורח חיים תקס:ב׳ ties the remembrance of Jerusalem at times of joy to תהלים קל״ז:ה׳, and the Rema there records the custom, in some places, of breaking a glass at the חופה; the Rema אבן העזר ס״ה:ג׳ records the custom, in some places, of breaking a cup after the seven blessings. ↩
  23. The rule on adding to the fixed prayer. The lecturer’s statement of the rule; no source is cited here (Open Questions, no. 9). ↩
  24. ברכות י״ז ע״א. After אלהי נצור לשוני מרע ושפתי מדבר מרמה the Gemara continues וכל החושבים עלי רעה מהרה הפר עצתם וקלקל מחשבתם. The later petitions in the Gemara (against evil mishap, the evil inclination and a bad woman) are not in our printed text. ↩
  25. Why this prayer became the standard one. The Gemara gives no reason why the prayer of מר בריה דרבינא in particular became the standard one. The lecturer reserved the question for the following discourse. ↩
  26. בבא בתרא קס״ה ע״א. The Gemara teaches that most stumble in robbery, a minority in immorality, and all in אבק לשון הרע. ↩
  27. תהלים כ׳:ח׳. אלה ברכב ואלה בסוסים ואנחנו בשם ה׳ אלהינו נזכיר. ↩
  28. רד״ק על תהלים י״ט:ט״ו. Rabbi David Kimchi (Provence, c. 1160–1235) distinguishes אמרי פי, what David spoke, from הגיון לבי, what he thought in his heart without voicing it, and asks that this too be accepted. The development that follows — thoughts that cannot be put into words, addressed to the One who knows them — is the lecturer’s extension. The lecturer placed the Radak at תהלים כ׳:ח׳; the comment is on י״ט:ט״ו. ↩
  29. שלחן ערוך, אורח חיים קכ״ב. Se’if 1 rules that between the eighteen blessings and יהיו לרצון one does not interrupt for קדיש or קדושה; the Rema adds that where the custom is to say אלהי נצור first, one does interrupt. Se’if 2 speaks of one who says supplications after his prayer when the chazzan reaches קדיש or קדושה. That personal petitions are best lengthened after יהיו לרצון is the practical conclusion of the authorities, here in the lecturer’s words. ↩
  30. The name given at the ברית. Ascribed by the lecturer to the Arizal. The passage in his writings has not been located here (Open Questions, no. 10). ↩
  31. Waking by the Hebrew name. Ascribed by the lecturer to the בני יששכר. The passage has not been located here (Open Questions, no. 10). ↩
  32. The סגולה of the verse of one’s name; the של״ה. Rabbi Yeshayahu Horowitz (1565–1630), author of שני לוחות הברית. The custom is associated with him in popular tradition; the lecturer notes that the direct attribution to him is uncertain, and the source has not been located (Open Questions, no. 11). ↩
  33. שם אפרים, Rabbi Ephraim Zalman Margolioth of Brody (d. 1828). As the lecturer describes it, he protests the printers’ interpolations into the commentary of Rashi on the Prophets and Writings (the lecturer cites שופטים, chapter 20). The Hebrew text of Rashi on שופטים כ׳ does contain parenthetical passages, chiefly verse citations; the שם אפרים itself was not located. ↩
